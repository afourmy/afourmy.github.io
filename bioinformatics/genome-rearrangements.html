Chapter 6: Are There Fragile Regions in the Human Genome?

Chapters 1 to 5 compared sequences letter by letter. Over the distance separating a human from a mouse that comparison stops working: the letters have changed too much, but whole blocks of the genome are still recognisably the same, sitting in a different order. This chapter measures the cost of that reordering, and uses the measurement to decide whether the places where genomes break are spread at random or concentrated in a few fragile spots.

Synteny Blocks

Comparing a human genome with a mouse genome finds long stretches that carry the same genes in the same internal order. Such a stretch is a synteny block. Between the two species the blocks themselves are nearly intact, and what differs is the order they appear in along the chromosome and the direction each one points.

A genome rearrangement is a mutation that cuts the chromosome and reassembles the pieces in a different arrangement. These mutations are rare, and they are rare for a reason: a cut that lands inside a gene destroys it, and an organism carrying a large rearrangement is usually dead or sterile. What survives to be compared is the small remainder that did no harm.

Writing each block as a number and its direction as a sign turns a genome into a signed permutation, and turns the comparison of two genomes into a question about permutations.

Random Breakage Model. Proposed by Nadeau and Taylor in 1984: rearrangements cut the chromosome at positions chosen uniformly at random, with no position more likely than any other.

The model makes a prediction that can be checked. If breakage points fall uniformly, the lengths of the synteny blocks they carve out follow an exponential distribution, and the measured block lengths did fit one. The model stood for nearly twenty years. Testing it properly needs a way to count how many rearrangements separate two genomes, and that count is what the rest of the chapter builds.

Reversals

A reversal cuts a chromosome in two places and reinserts the segment between the cuts back to front. The blocks in that segment reverse their order, and each one also reverses direction, so each of their signs flips.

Reversal Distance Problem. Given two signed permutations of the same length, find the minimum number of reversals transforming one into the other.

Relabelling the blocks so that the second genome reads $(+1\ +2\ \dots\ +n)$ costs nothing and turns every instance into the same shape: reversals are reversible, so the distance from $P$ to $Q$ is the distance from $Q^{-1}P$ to the identity. The problem is therefore to sort a signed permutation using as few reversals as possible.

Greedy Sorting

The obvious approach fixes one position at a time. To settle position $k$, find the block $k$ wherever it currently sits and reverse the stretch from position $k$ to that position, which brings it home; if it arrives pointing the wrong way, reverse it alone to flip its sign. Positions $1$ through $k$ are then correct and are never touched again. 

def greedy_sorting(permutation):
    order, steps = list(permutation), []
    for k in range(1, len(order) + 1):
        if abs(order[k - 1]) != k:
            position = next(i for i in range(k - 1, len(order)) if abs(order[i]) == k)
            order[k - 1:position + 1] = [-x for x in reversed(order[k - 1:position + 1])]
            steps.append(list(order))
        if order[k - 1] == -k:
            order[k - 1] = k
            steps.append(list(order))
    return steps

Each of the first $n - 1$ positions costs at most two reversals and the last costs at most one, so greedy sorting never uses more than $2n - 1$ reversals, and it does the work in $O(n^2)$ time. The bound is tight: $(-2\ -4\ +1\ -5\ +3)$ costs it 9.

What the algorithm does not give is any reason to believe $2n - 1$ is close to the true distance. On $(+n\ +(n-1)\ \dots\ +2\ +1)$ it spends $n + 1$ reversals, and a single reversal of the whole chromosome followed by $n$ sign flips would do the same job. Deciding whether an answer is good needs a lower bound on the distance, which greedy sorting does not supply.

Breakpoints

Pad the permutation with $0$ in front and $n + 1$ behind. Each of the $n + 1$ gaps between consecutive values is an adjacency when the right value is exactly one more than the left, and a breakpoint otherwise. Every gap is one or the other, so the two counts always sum to $n + 1$.

Breakpoint Counting Problem. Given a signed permutation, count its breakpoints. 

def breakpoints(permutation):
    extended = [0] + list(permutation) + [len(permutation) + 1]
    return sum(1 for a, b in zip(extended, extended[1:]) if b - a != 1)

The count costs $O(n)$. On $(+3\ +4\ +5\ -12\ -8\ -7\ -6\ +1\ +2\ +10\ +9\ -11\ +13\ +14)$ it returns 8. The identity permutation is the only one with no breakpoints, so sorting is the destruction of breakpoints, and counting them measures how far there is to go.

A Lower Bound

A reversal touches only the two gaps at its boundaries. Every gap strictly inside the reversed segment survives it, because reversing a run of blocks reverses the whole run and leaves the differences within it unchanged, and every gap outside the segment is never looked at.

Theorem. $d_{\text{rev}}(P) \ge \text{breakpoints}(P) / 2$.

Each reversal removes at most two breakpoints, and sorting must remove all of them, so no sequence shorter than $\text{breakpoints}(P)/2$ can finish. This is the first statement in the chapter about the distance itself rather than about one algorithm's behaviour. It is only a bound, though, and it is not tight: there are permutations whose reversal distance exceeds half their breakpoint count.

Rearrangements Across Chromosomes

A reversal keeps the chromosome intact. Real genomes are several chromosomes, and rearrangements move material between them. A translocation exchanges the ends of two chromosomes, a fusion joins two chromosomes into one, and a fission splits one into two.

These are not exotic. Human chromosome 2 is a fusion of two chromosomes that remain separate in the chimpanzee, which is why humans carry 23 pairs and chimpanzees 24. Translocations also appear in cancer: a specific exchange between chromosomes 9 and 22 creates a fused gene whose protein drives chronic myeloid leukaemia, and the drug imatinib works by blocking exactly that protein.

A signed permutation cannot express any of the three, because it describes a single sequence of blocks. Reversal distance is the wrong measurement for real genomes, and a representation that covers all four operations is needed before the distance can be defined at all.

Genomes as Graphs

Give every block two ends, a tail and a head, and read a block pointing right as tail then head, one pointing left as head then tail. Block $i$ owns the two nodes $2i - 1$ and $2i$. Joining the two ends of a block gives a block edge, and joining consecutive blocks gives an adjacency edge. Treating each chromosome as circular, which costs nothing and removes the special case at the ends, makes each chromosome a cycle that alternates between the two kinds of edge.

A block edge is a fact about a block and never changes. Every rearrangement is therefore a change to the adjacency edges alone, and the whole genome is recoverable from that list of edges. 

def chromosome_to_cycle(chromosome):
    nodes = []
    for block in chromosome:
        nodes += [2 * block - 1, 2 * block] if block > 0 else [-2 * block, -2 * block - 1]
    return nodes


def cycle_to_chromosome(nodes):
    chromosome = []
    for j in range(0, len(nodes), 2):
        head, tail = nodes[j], nodes[j + 1]
        chromosome.append(tail // 2 if head < tail else -head // 2)
    return chromosome


def colored_edges(genome):
    edges = []
    for chromosome in genome:
        cycle = chromosome_to_cycle(chromosome)
        for j in range(len(chromosome)):
            edges.append((cycle[2 * j + 1], cycle[(2 * j + 2) % len(cycle)]))
    return edges

Going back is a walk: from any unvisited node, cross its block edge to the partner end, follow the adjacency edge to the next block, and continue until the cycle closes. Each cycle recovered this way is one chromosome. 

def graph_to_genome(edges):
    neighbour = {}
    for a, b in edges:
        neighbour.setdefault(a, []).append(b)
        neighbour.setdefault(b, []).append(a)
    partner = lambda node: node + 1 if node % 2 else node - 1

    genome, seen = [], set()
    for start in sorted(neighbour):
        if start in seen:
            continue
        nodes, node = [], start
        while node not in seen:
            seen.add(node)
            seen.add(partner(node))
            nodes += [node, partner(node)]
            options = [x for x in neighbour[partner(node)] if x not in seen]
            node = options[0] if options else start
        genome.append(cycle_to_chromosome(nodes))
    return genome

Both directions cost $O(n)$ for a genome of $n$ blocks, and graph_to_genome(colored_edges(P)) returns $P$.

Every Rearrangement Is a 2-Break

A 2-break deletes two adjacency edges and adds two new adjacency edges on the same four nodes. That is the whole definition, and it is the only operation the chapter needs.

A reversal and a translocation are each one 2-break already. They look nothing alike in a genome, but in the graph a reversal removes two adjacency edges of one chromosome and a translocation removes one from each of two chromosomes, and both put two edges back on the same four block ends.

Fusion and fission need one more piece. A chromosome end carries no adjacency edge, so a fusion has only one edge to add and a fission only one to remove. Giving every chromosome end, every telomere, an edge of its own restores the balance: a fusion trades the two telomere edges it consumes for one adjacency edge and one empty chromosome, and a fission is the reverse. With telomere edges in place all four rearrangements are one operation, two edges out and two edges in, and which name a biologist gives the result depends only on which four ends were rejoined.

Everything from here on works with circular chromosomes, which costs nothing and removes the telomeres again. There a 2-break within one cycle is a reversal or a fission depending on how the ends are rejoined, and a 2-break across two cycles is a fusion. 

def two_break_on_graph(edges, i1, i2, i3, i4):
    kept = [e for e in edges if set(e) not in ({i1, i2}, {i3, i4})]
    return kept + [(i1, i3), (i2, i4)]


def two_break_on_genome(genome, i1, i2, i3, i4):
    return graph_to_genome(two_break_on_graph(colored_edges(genome), i1, i2, i3, i4))

2-Break Distance Problem. Given two genomes on the same synteny blocks, find the minimum number of 2-breaks transforming one into the other.

The Breakpoint Graph

Both genomes are built from the same blocks, so both have adjacency edges on the same $2n$ nodes. Drawing both sets at once gives the breakpoint graph of $P$ and $Q$. Every node has exactly one adjacency edge from $P$ and exactly one from $Q$, so every node has degree two and the graph falls apart into cycles that alternate between the two genomes.

Write $\text{Cycles}(P, Q)$ for the number of these cycles and $\text{Blocks}(P, Q)$ for the number of synteny blocks. A genome against itself gives the most cycles there can be: every $P$ edge coincides with a $Q$ edge, each pair forming a cycle of length two, so $\text{Cycles}(P, P) = \text{Blocks}$. The further apart the genomes, the fewer and longer the cycles.

The Cycle Theorem

Cycle Theorem. A 2-break applied to $P$ changes $\text{Cycles}(P, Q)$ by at most $+1$.

The two new edges touch the same four nodes as the two deleted ones. Deleting two edges of a cycle splits it into at most two pieces, and the two new edges rejoin those pieces into either one cycle or two, so the count rises by at most one. If the two deleted edges lie in different cycles, the two cycles merge and the count falls.

The 2-Break Distance

2-Break Distance Theorem. $d(P, Q) = \text{Blocks}(P, Q) - \text{Cycles}(P, Q)$.

Transforming $P$ into $Q$ means turning the breakpoint graph of $P$ against $Q$ into the breakpoint graph of $Q$ against itself, which has $\text{Blocks}$ cycles. Each 2-break gains at most one cycle, so at least $\text{Blocks} - \text{Cycles}$ of them are needed. That many also suffice: as long as some cycle is longer than two, a 2-break exists that splits it and gains exactly one, so the bound is reached. 

def cycles(genome_p, genome_q):
    neighbour = {}
    for a, b in colored_edges(genome_p) + colored_edges(genome_q):
        neighbour.setdefault(a, []).append(b)
        neighbour.setdefault(b, []).append(a)
    seen, total = set(), 0
    for start in neighbour:
        if start in seen:
            continue
        total += 1
        stack = [start]
        while stack:
            node = stack.pop()
            if node in seen:
                continue
            seen.add(node)
            stack += neighbour[node]
    return total


def two_break_distance(genome_p, genome_q):
    blocks = sum(len(chromosome) for chromosome in genome_p)
    return blocks - cycles(genome_p, genome_q)

Building the graph and counting its components costs $O(n)$. A distance that looked like it required searching through sequences of rearrangements is a subtraction between two counts. For $P = (+1\ +2\ +3\ +4\ +5\ +6)$ and $Q = (+1\ -3\ -6\ -5)(+2\ -4)$ it returns $6 - 3 = 3$.

Sorting by 2-Breaks

The proof is constructive, so it is also the algorithm. While some cycle is longer than two, pick an adjacency edge of $Q$ that is not yet an edge of $P$, and apply the 2-break that installs it. That edge closes off part of its cycle, gaining one, and $\text{Blocks} - \text{Cycles}$ steps land on $Q$. 

def two_break_sorting(genome_p, genome_q):
    path = [genome_p]
    while two_break_distance(path[-1], genome_q):
        red = colored_edges(path[-1])
        neighbour = {}
        for a, b in red:
            neighbour[a] = b
            neighbour[b] = a
        installed = {frozenset(e) for e in red}
        for j1, j2 in colored_edges(genome_q):
            if frozenset((j1, j2)) in installed:
                continue
            path.append(two_break_on_genome(path[-1], j1, neighbour[j1], j2, neighbour[j2]))
            break
    return path

Every round installs one edge of $Q$ for good, so there are $d(P, Q)$ rounds and the whole procedure costs $O(n^2)$. On the pair above it produces $(+1\ +2\ +3\ +4\ +5\ +6)$, then $(+1\ -3\ -2\ +4\ +5\ +6)$, then $(+1\ -3\ -6\ -5\ -4\ +2)$, then $(+1\ -3\ -6\ -5)(+2\ -4)$: three 2-breaks, matching the distance. The last of them is a fission, which is where the second chromosome comes from.

Finding the Blocks

Everything so far assumed the synteny blocks were already known. They are not; they have to be found from the two sequences, and the tool is the k-mer.

Shared k-mers Problem. Given two strings, find every pair of positions $(i, j)$ such that the k-mer at position $i$ of the first equals the k-mer at position $j$ of the second, or its reverse complement.

The reverse complement is included because a synteny block may sit on either strand, using reverse_complement from Chapter 1. Indexing the first string's k-mers in a dictionary makes the search one pass over the second. 

def shared_kmers(k, first, second):
    index = {}
    for i in range(len(first) - k + 1):
        index.setdefault(first[i:i + k], []).append(i)
    pairs = []
    for j in range(len(second) - k + 1):
        window = second[j:j + k]
        for pattern in {window, reverse_complement(window)}:
            for i in index.get(pattern, []):
                pairs.append((i, j))
    return sorted(set(pairs))

The cost is $O(nk)$ for strings of length $n$, and on AAACTCATC against TTTCAAATC with $k = 3$ it returns $(0,0)$, $(0,4)$, $(4,2)$ and $(6,6)$.

Plotting every returned pair as a point, with one genome on each axis, gives a genomic dot plot. A synteny block shared by the two genomes appears as a diagonal run of points, sloping one way if the block points the same way in both and the other way if it is reversed. Isolated points are k-mers that match by chance. Reading the blocks off a dot plot is what supplies the signed permutations the rest of the chapter consumes.

Application: Are There Fragile Regions?

The Random Breakage Model says breakage points fall uniformly at random. A uniform process almost never uses the same position twice, so under that model the endpoints of the synteny blocks between two genomes should be nearly all distinct.

The 2-break machinery makes this checkable. Take the synteny blocks of human against mouse, compute a shortest sequence of 2-breaks between the two genomes, and count how often a breakage point is used more than once along it. Under random breakage the count should be close to zero.

It is not. Pevzner and Tesler carried out this comparison in 2003 and found extensive breakpoint reuse: the same regions are broken again and again across independent rearrangements, far more often than a uniform process would allow. The observation that had supported the Random Breakage Model, the exponential distribution of block lengths, turns out to be consistent with other models too, so it never distinguished them.

Fragile Breakage Model. Rearrangements do not cut anywhere. The genome contains a limited number of fragile regions, and breakage happens there.

The question the chapter opened on has an answer, and it came from counting. Whether breakage is random or concentrated is not a question that can be settled by looking at genomes, only by measuring the distance between them, and that measurement rests on the observation that a reversal, a translocation, a fusion and a fission are one operation once a genome is written as a graph.
