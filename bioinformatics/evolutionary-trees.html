Chapter 7: Which Animal Gave Us SARS?

A tree is a claim about descent: two sequences meet at the ancestor they last shared. This chapter builds such trees from sequence data, first by reducing the sequences to a matrix of pairwise distances, then by giving that up and working from the sequences themselves. It ends by naming the animal.

Phylogeny

An outbreak of severe acute respiratory syndrome began in southern China in 2002 and spread through 2003. The cause was a coronavirus, an RNA virus, and RNA viruses mutate fast enough that a few years of spread leaves a readable record of who descended from whom.

The virus had not been circulating in people before. It came from an animal, and the question is which. The method is comparative: sequence the human virus, sequence viruses from candidate animals, and build a phylogenetic tree whose leaves are those sequences. If the human virus sits on a very short branch next to one animal's virus, that animal's population is where it came from. The same reasoning had already identified the origin of HIV, whose human strains sit inside the diversity of the simian viruses rather than beside it.

From Alignment to Distances

Aligning the sequences and counting, for each pair, the positions at which they differ turns $n$ sequences into an $n \times n$ matrix. The alignment machinery is Chapter 5; what matters here is what comes out.

A matrix $D$ is a distance matrix when $D_{ii} = 0$, $D_{ij} = D_{ji}$, and $D_{ij} \le D_{ik} + D_{kj}$ for every $i$, $j$ and $k$. The last of these says a detour never beats the direct route.

Trees and the Distances They Induce

A weighted unrooted tree whose leaves are the $n$ sequences induces a distance matrix: the distance between two leaves is the total weight of the unique path between them.

Distances Between Leaves Problem. Given a weighted tree with $n$ leaves, compute the $n \times n$ matrix of distances between leaves. 

def distances_between_leaves(n, tree):
    """Row i, column j is the total weight of the path from leaf i to leaf j."""
    matrix = [[0] * n for _ in range(n)]
    for leaf in range(n):
        seen, stack = {leaf: 0}, [leaf]
        while stack:
            node = stack.pop()
            for neighbour, weight in tree[node]:
                if neighbour not in seen:
                    seen[neighbour] = seen[node] + weight
                    stack.append(neighbour)
        for other in range(n):
            matrix[leaf][other] = seen[other]
    return matrix

One walk per leaf costs $O(n)$ each, so the whole matrix costs $O(n^2)$. The useful direction is the reverse.

Distance-Based Phylogeny Problem. Given a distance matrix, find a weighted tree whose induced distances are that matrix.

Which Matrices Fit a Tree

The problem as stated need not have a solution. A matrix for which some tree exists is called additive, and most matrices are not.

Even for additive matrices the answer is not unique unless trees with nodes of degree two are excluded, since such a node can be inserted anywhere along an edge without changing any distance.

With that restriction the answer is pinned down: an additive matrix is induced by exactly one simple tree. Finding it is what the next four sections do.

Neighbouring Leaves

Two leaves are neighbours when they share a parent. Every simple tree with at least three nodes has a pair of them, which can be seen by walking away from any leaf until the path can go no further: the node reached last has at least two leaves hanging off it.

This suggests a recursion. Find a pair of neighbouring leaves, delete both rows and columns, add one row for their parent using the formula above, solve the smaller problem, and hang the two leaves back on. Everything depends on finding the neighbouring pair.

Why the Nearest Pair Is Not a Neighbouring Pair

The obvious guess is that the smallest entry of the matrix identifies a neighbouring pair. It does not.

Two leaves on short limbs at opposite ends of a tree can be closer to each other than either is to its own neighbour on a long limb. The recursion has to find something else to strip away, and the something else is a single leaf rather than a pair.

Limb Length

Limb Length Problem. Given an additive matrix and a leaf $j$, find the weight of the edge joining $j$ to its parent.

Theorem. $\text{LimbLength}(j) = \min_{i,k}\ (D_{ij} + D_{jk} - D_{ik}) / 2$ over all leaves $i$ and $k$ distinct from $j$ and from each other.

The path from $i$ to $j$ and the path from $j$ to $k$ both end with $j$'s limb. Adding them counts the limb twice and counts the rest of both paths once; subtracting $D_{ik}$ removes exactly the rest, provided the two paths separate at $j$'s parent. If they separate further from $j$ the subtraction leaves something over, so the expression is never below twice the limb and reaches it whenever $i$ and $k$ lie on opposite sides of the parent. Such a pair always exists, so the minimum is the limb. 

def limb_length(n, j, matrix):
    return min((matrix[i][j] + matrix[j][k] - matrix[i][k]) / 2
               for i in range(n) for k in range(n) if i != j and k != j and i != k)

The minimum ranges over $O(n^2)$ pairs, so one limb costs $O(n^2)$.

Additive Phylogeny

Subtracting the limb length from row $j$ and column $j$ makes leaf $j$ bald: its limb now has weight zero, so it no longer hangs off the tree but sits directly on it, at a point that the remaining distances identify. Delete it, solve the smaller problem, find that point, and put the limb back. 

def additive_phylogeny(matrix, n, fresh=None):
    if fresh is None:
        fresh = [len(matrix)]
    if n == 2:
        tree = {}
        link(tree, 0, 1, matrix[0][1])
        return tree

    limb = limb_length(n, n - 1, matrix)
    for i in range(n - 1):
        matrix[i][n - 1] -= limb
        matrix[n - 1][i] = matrix[i][n - 1]
    i, k = next((i, k) for i in range(n - 1) for k in range(n - 1)
                if i != k and matrix[i][k] == matrix[i][n - 1] + matrix[n - 1][k])
    x = matrix[i][n - 1]

    tree = additive_phylogeny(matrix, n - 1, fresh)

    walked, travelled = path_between(tree, i, k), 0
    attach = None
    for a, b in zip(walked, walked[1:]):
        weight = next(w for node, w in tree[a] if node == b)
        if travelled + weight > x:
            attach = fresh[0]
            fresh[0] += 1
            unlink(tree, a, b)
            link(tree, a, attach, x - travelled)
            link(tree, attach, b, travelled + weight - x)
            break
        travelled += weight
        if travelled == x:
            attach = b
            break
    if attach is None:
        attach = walked[0]
    link(tree, n - 1, attach, limb)
    return tree

Each of the $n$ rounds computes a limb in $O(n^2)$ and walks a path in $O(n)$, so the whole algorithm is $O(n^3)$. On the running matrix it returns the tree at the top of this chapter, and feeding that tree back through distances_between_leaves returns the matrix it started from.

When the Data Is Not Additive

Real distance matrices are built from real alignments and are never exactly additive. No tree fits, so the question becomes which tree misses by least. Writing $d_{ij}(T)$ for the distance the tree induces, the discrepancy is $\sum_{i,j} (d_{ij}(T) - D_{ij})^2$.

Least-Squares Distance-Based Phylogeny Problem. Given a distance matrix, find the tree minimizing the discrepancy.

For a fixed tree shape the best edge weights follow from linear algebra, but the number of shapes grows faster than exponentially and the problem is NP-complete. Everything from here on is a heuristic.

Molecular Clocks and UPGMA

An ultrametric tree is a rooted tree in which every leaf is the same distance from the root. It is what a constant rate of mutation would produce: if changes accumulate at the same speed in every lineage, then time and distance are the same measurement. 

def upgma(matrix, n):
    clusters = {i: [i] for i in range(n)}
    gap = {(i, j): matrix[i][j] for i in range(n) for j in range(n)}
    age, tree, fresh = {i: 0 for i in range(n)}, {i: [] for i in range(n)}, n

    while len(clusters) > 1:
        i, j = min(((a, b) for a in clusters for b in clusters if a < b),
                   key=lambda pair: gap[pair])
        new, fresh = fresh, fresh + 1
        tree[new] = []
        age[new] = gap[i, j] / 2
        link(tree, new, i, age[new] - age[i])
        link(tree, new, j, age[new] - age[j])
        size_i, size_j = len(clusters[i]), len(clusters[j])
        for other in clusters:
            if other in (i, j):
                continue
            merged = (gap[min(i, other), max(i, other)] * size_i
                      + gap[min(j, other), max(j, other)] * size_j) / (size_i + size_j)
            gap[min(new, other), max(new, other)] = merged
        clusters[new] = clusters[i] + clusters[j]
        del clusters[i], clusters[j]
    return tree

Each of $n$ rounds scans the surviving pairs, so UPGMA costs $O(n^3)$. Its weakness is the assumption rather than the arithmetic. Mutation rates differ between lineages, real trees are not ultrametric, and UPGMA will still return an ultrametric one, which means it can place a fast-evolving lineage in the wrong part of the tree entirely.

Neighbour-Joining

Neighbour-joining drops the clock. It repairs the failed idea of Section 6 by transforming the matrix so that its smallest entry does identify a neighbouring pair. Writing $\text{Total}(i)$ for the sum of row $i$, the transformed matrix is $D^*_{ij} = (n - 2) D_{ij} - \text{Total}(i) - \text{Total}(j)$.

Neighbour-Joining Theorem. For an additive matrix, the smallest element of $D^*$ corresponds to a pair of neighbouring leaves. 

def total_distance(matrix):
    return [sum(row) for row in matrix]


def neighbor_joining_matrix(matrix):
    n = len(matrix)
    total = total_distance(matrix)
    return [[0 if i == j else (n - 2) * matrix[i][j] - total[i] - total[j]
             for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]


def neighbor_joining(matrix, labels=None, fresh=None):
    n = len(matrix)
    if labels is None:
        labels = list(range(n))
    if fresh is None:
        fresh = [n]
    if n == 2:
        tree = {}
        link(tree, labels[0], labels[1], matrix[0][1])
        return tree

    star = neighbor_joining_matrix(matrix)
    total = total_distance(matrix)
    i, j = min(((a, b) for a in range(n) for b in range(n) if a < b),
               key=lambda pair: star[pair[0]][pair[1]])
    delta = (total[i] - total[j]) / (n - 2)
    limb_i, limb_j = (matrix[i][j] + delta) / 2, (matrix[i][j] - delta) / 2

    rest = [k for k in range(n) if k not in (i, j)]
    parent, fresh[0] = fresh[0], fresh[0] + 1
    smaller = [[matrix[a][b] for b in rest]
               + [(matrix[a][i] + matrix[a][j] - matrix[i][j]) / 2] for a in rest]
    smaller.append([(matrix[i][b] + matrix[j][b] - matrix[i][j]) / 2 for b in rest] + [0])

    tree = neighbor_joining(smaller, [labels[k] for k in rest] + [parent], fresh)
    link(tree, labels[i], parent, limb_i)
    link(tree, labels[j], parent, limb_j)
    return tree

Each round builds $D^*$ in $O(n^2)$ and there are $n$ rounds, so neighbour-joining costs $O(n^3)$. It is exact on additive matrices and degrades gracefully on matrices that are nearly additive, which is why it is the method that gets used.

What Distances Throw Away

Every method so far reads only the matrix. Two alignments that differ in which sequences share which changes can produce the same matrix, and once the sequences are reduced to numbers that difference is unrecoverable. A distance says how many positions two sequences differ at; it does not say which, and which is exactly the evidence that says who inherited what from whom.

Characters and Parsimony

A character is any feature a species either has or lacks, or more generally any position whose value can be compared across species. A character table has one row per species and one column per character, and each column is a hypothesis about grouping: the species sharing a value may share an ancestor in which it arose.

The reason this is evidence at all is Dollo's principle of irreversibility: a complex character, once lost, is not regained in the same form. Insect wings arose once and have been lost repeatedly, so wingless insects are scattered across the tree while the winged ones descend from a single origin. A shared complex character is therefore better explained by common descent than by two independent inventions, and a tree that requires fewer changes is a better explanation than one that requires more.

Counting changes on a tree whose leaves carry strings, the parsimony score is the total Hamming distance summed over all edges.

Small Parsimony

Small Parsimony Problem. Given a rooted binary tree whose leaves are labelled by strings of length $m$, label every other node by a string of length $m$ so as to minimize the parsimony score.

Positions do not interact, so the problem splits into $m$ independent problems on the same tree, each over a four-letter alphabet. Each of those is a dynamic program over the tree, in the same shape as the longest-path recurrence of Chapter 5: the answer at a node is built from the answers at its children, and the children are settled first. 

ALPHABET = 'ACGT'
INF = float('inf')


def small_parsimony(children, labels, root):
    """Sankoff's dynamic program, one independent pass per position."""
    order, stack = [], [root]
    while stack:                                  # children before parents
        node = stack.pop()
        order.append(node)
        stack += children.get(node, [])
    order.reverse()

    length = len(next(iter(labels.values())))
    assigned = {node: [] for node in order}
    score = 0
    for position in range(length):
        cost, choice = {}, {}
        for node in order:
            if node in labels:
                cost[node] = {k: 0 if labels[node][position] == k else INF
                              for k in ALPHABET}
                continue
            cost[node], choice[node] = {}, {}
            for k in ALPHABET:
                total, picks = 0, []
                for child in children[node]:
                    best = min(ALPHABET,
                               key=lambda s: cost[child][s] + (s != k))
                    total += cost[child][best] + (best != k)
                    picks.append(best)
                cost[node][k], choice[node][k] = total, picks
        best_root = min(ALPHABET, key=lambda k: cost[root][k])
        score += cost[root][best_root]

        chosen = {root: best_root}
        for node in reversed(order):
            if node in labels:
                continue
            for child, symbol in zip(children[node], choice[node][chosen[node]]):
                chosen[child] = symbol
        for node in order:
            assigned[node].append(labels[node][position] if node in labels
                                  else chosen[node])
    return score, {node: ''.join(symbols) for node, symbols in assigned.items()}

The table has one entry per node, symbol and position, so the cost is $O(nm)$ for a tree with $n$ nodes and strings of length $m$. On the four sequences CAAATCCC, ATTGCGAC, CTGCGCTG and ATGGACGA arranged as two pairs, the score is 16.

An unrooted tree is handled by rooting it: insert a node in the middle of any internal edge, solve the rooted problem, then dissolve the inserted node again. The score does not depend on which edge is chosen. 

def small_parsimony_unrooted(tree, labels):
    """Root any edge, solve the rooted problem, then dissolve the root again."""
    a, b = next((a, b) for a in tree for b, _ in tree[a]
                if a not in labels and b not in labels)
    children = {'root': [a, b]}
    seen = {'root', a, b}                  # cutting the root edge splits the tree in two
    stack = [a, b]
    while stack:
        node = stack.pop()
        kids = [x for x, _ in tree[node] if x not in seen]
        seen.update(kids)
        if kids:
            children[node] = kids
            stack += kids
    score, assigned = small_parsimony(children, labels, 'root')
    return score, assigned, (a, b)

Large Parsimony

Small parsimony took the tree as given. The real problem does not.

Large Parsimony Problem. Given a collection of strings of equal length, find the unrooted binary tree with those strings as leaves that minimizes the parsimony score.

This is NP-complete, and the number of unrooted binary trees on $n$ leaves is $(2n-5)!!$, which is already over two million at $n = 10$. What is done instead is local search. 

def neighbours(tree, a, b):
    """The two trees one nearest-neighbour interchange away across the edge a-b."""
    a_side = [x for x, _ in tree[a] if x != b]
    b_side = [x for x, _ in tree[b] if x != a]
    out = []
    for swap in range(2):
        new = {node: list(edges) for node, edges in tree.items()}
        x, y = a_side[0], b_side[swap]
        unlink(new, a, x)
        unlink(new, b, y)
        link(new, a, y, 1)
        link(new, b, x, 1)
        out.append(new)
    return out


def nearest_neighbour_interchange(tree, labels):
    """Hill-climb: keep taking the best neighbouring topology while it improves."""
    best_score, _, _ = small_parsimony_unrooted(tree, labels)
    best_tree = tree
    improved = True
    while improved:
        improved = False
        for a, b in internal_edges(best_tree, labels):
            for candidate in neighbours(best_tree, a, b):
                score, _, _ = small_parsimony_unrooted(candidate, labels)
                if score < best_score:
                    best_score, best_tree, improved = score, candidate, True
    return best_score, best_tree

Each round scores $O(n)$ candidate trees at $O(nm)$ each. On the four sequences above, starting from the tree that pairs the first two and the last two, the score is 16; the search moves to the tree pairing the first with the third and reaches 15, which is the true minimum over all three topologies. What it finds in general is a local optimum, and the usual remedy is to run it from many starting trees.

Application: Which Animal Gave Us SARS?

Seven complete coronavirus genomes, taken from GenBank: the human SARS-CoV sequenced in Toronto in 2003, a SARS-CoV sequenced from a masked palm civet in a Guangdong market, a SARS-like coronavirus from a horseshoe bat, SARS-CoV-2, MERS-CoV, and the two mild human coronaviruses OC43 and 229E as an outgroup.

Aligning thirty-thousand-nucleotide genomes pairwise is expensive, so the distance is estimated instead from the proportion of 12-mers two genomes share, counting each k-mer and its reverse complement as the same. That gives a distance matrix without an alignment, and neighbor_joining turns it into a tree.

The human and civet viruses differ by 0.0020. Every other pair in the matrix differs by at least 0.16, so the two of them are eighty times closer to each other than either is to anything else, and neighbour-joining pairs them in its first round. The bat virus attaches one node further out, and MERS, OC43 and 229E sit far away in the outgroup.

The tree says the human virus and the civet virus are nearly the same virus. It does not by itself say which way it travelled, and a tree never can: shared ancestry constrains the direction of transmission but does not determine it. What settled the direction was epidemiology, that early cases were animal handlers in markets where civets were sold, and the wider sampling that found the deeper reservoir in bats, with the civet as the intermediate the virus passed through on its way to us.
