Chapter 8: How Did Yeast Become a Wine Maker?

Yeast turns sugar into alcohol, and it keeps doing so even when there is enough oxygen to burn the sugar outright. When the sugar finally runs out the cell reverses course and starts consuming the alcohol it made. Thousands of genes change their activity across that switch, and the question this chapter answers is which of them change together. Answering it means partitioning points in space into groups, which is a problem with several precise statements and no efficient exact solution to any of them.

Gene Expression and the Diauxic Shift

Saccharomyces cerevisiae is a single-celled fungus, the yeast used for bread, beer and wine. Given glucose it performs fermentation, breaking each sugar molecule into ethanol and carbon dioxide. Fermentation releases far less energy per molecule of sugar than respiration, which burns the sugar completely using oxygen, but yeast ferments anyway whenever glucose is plentiful, oxygen present or not. The ethanol that accumulates as a result is what makes wine wine.

Fermentation leaves the ethanol unburned, so when the glucose is gone the cell still has fuel available. It responds by switching to respiration and consuming its own ethanol. The switch is called the diauxic shift, and it is not a small adjustment: growth slows, and several hundred genes change their activity across it.

A gene is expressed by being transcribed into RNA, and the amount of that RNA present in the cell is its expression level. A microarray measures that level for every gene of an organism at once, and reports each one as a ratio against a reference sample, so a value of 2 means the gene is twice as active as it was at the start. Measuring a population of cells repeatedly gives, for each gene, one number per timepoint.

Those numbers form an expression matrix: one row per gene, one column per timepoint, and the row belonging to a gene is its expression vector. A gene measured at $m$ timepoints is therefore a point in $m$-dimensional space. The data used throughout this chapter is the diauxic shift experiment of DeRisi, Iyer and Brown, which measured 6153 yeast genes at seven successive timepoints as a culture exhausted its glucose. Ratios are converted to $\log_2$, so that a twofold rise and a twofold fall are the same distance from no change.

Genes that respond to the same signal tend to move together, so a group of genes with similar expression vectors is a candidate for a group of genes under common control, and often for a group with related function. That turns a biological question into a geometric one: partition $n$ points in $m$-dimensional space into groups of nearby points.

What Makes a Clustering Good

A first attempt at saying what "nearby" should mean is a condition on the partition rather than a quantity to optimise. The Good Clustering Principle asks that every pair of points in the same cluster be closer to each other than any pair of points in different clusters. It is exactly the statement that the largest within-cluster distance is smaller than the smallest between-cluster distance.

The principle is a test, not an algorithm, and for most real data no partition passes it at all. Expression measurements are continuous and noisy, and clusters generally touch rather than sit in separated balls, so some within-cluster pair will be further apart than some between-cluster pair no matter how the points are divided. What is needed instead is a numerical score that every partition has, so that clustering becomes minimisation.

Clustering as an Optimization Problem

Distance between two points $v$ and $w$ in $m$-dimensional space is Euclidean, $d(v, w) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m} (v_i - w_i)^2}$. Rather than score a partition directly, score a set of $k$ points called centres, and let each data point belong to the centre nearest it. The distance from a point to a whole set of centres is the distance to the nearest one, $d(\mathrm{DataPoint}, \mathrm{Centers}) = \min_{x \in \mathrm{Centers}} d(\mathrm{DataPoint}, x)$, and the score of the set is the worst of those, $\mathrm{MaxDistance}(\mathrm{Data}, \mathrm{Centers}) = \max_{\mathrm{DataPoint} \in \mathrm{Data}} d(\mathrm{DataPoint}, \mathrm{Centers})$.

The k-Center Clustering Problem is then: given a set of points $\mathrm{Data}$ and an integer $k$, find the set $\mathrm{Centers}$ of $k$ points minimising $\mathrm{MaxDistance}(\mathrm{Data}, \mathrm{Centers})$. Geometrically it asks for $k$ balls of equal radius covering all the data, with the radius as small as possible. The problem is NP-hard, so unless P = NP no polynomial-time algorithm solves it exactly, and the practical question is what a heuristic gives instead.

Farthest First Traversal

The obvious greedy heuristic puts the next centre wherever the current centres serve worst. Start from an arbitrary data point, then repeatedly add as a new centre the data point that is farthest from every centre chosen so far, stopping at $k$. Round $j$ measures each of the $n$ points against the $j$ centres already chosen at $O(m)$ per measurement, so the code below costs $\sum_{j=1}^{k} O(njm) = O(nk^{2}m)$ for $n$ points in $m$ dimensions. Storing each point's distance to the nearest centre so far and updating it against the single new centre each round removes a factor of $k$ and brings this to $O(nkm)$. 

def distance(v, w):
    return sum((a - b) ** 2 for a, b in zip(v, w)) ** 0.5


def nearest(point, centers):
    return min(centers, key=lambda c: distance(point, c))


def farthest_first_traversal(data, k):
    centers = [data[0]]
    while len(centers) < k:
        centers.append(max(data, key=lambda p: distance(p, nearest(p, centers))))
    return centers

Theorem. Farthest first traversal returns a set of centres whose $\mathrm{MaxDistance}$ is at most twice the smallest possible.

The proof is the pigeonhole principle applied to $k + 1$ points. Write $C_j$ for the first $j$ centres the algorithm chooses and $r_j = \max_{p \in \mathrm{Data}} d(p, C_j)$, so the value returned is $r = r_k$, attained at some point $p$. Adding a centre to a set can only decrease $d(p, C)$ for every $p$, so $r_1 \geq r_2 \geq \cdots \geq r_k = r$. The centre $c_{j+1}$ is by definition a point at which $r_j$ is attained, so $d(c_{j+1}, C_j) = r_j \geq r$, which says that $c_{j+1}$ is at distance at least $r$ from every centre chosen before it, and $d(p, C_k) = r$ puts $p$ at distance at least $r$ from all $k$ of them. The $k + 1$ points $c_1, \ldots, c_k, p$ are therefore pairwise at distance at least $r$.

Let $r^{*}$ be the optimal value, attained by some set of $k$ centres. Every data point lies within $r^{*}$ of one of those centres, so assign each of the $k + 1$ points above to such a centre. There are $k + 1$ points and $k$ optimal centres, so two of the points, $u$ and $v$, are assigned to the same one, say $c^{*}$. Then $d(u, v) \leq d(u, c^{*}) + d(c^{*}, v) \leq 2r^{*}$ by the triangle inequality, while $d(u, v) \geq r$ because those $k + 1$ points are pairwise that far apart. Hence $r \leq 2r^{*}$. The factor 2 is the best any polynomial-time algorithm can guarantee, since approximating the k-Center Clustering Problem within any smaller factor is itself NP-hard.

The guarantee is good and the algorithm is still the wrong tool for expression data. Minimising the largest distance means the score is decided entirely by the single worst-served point, and in a microarray experiment the points furthest from everything else are usually the ones whose measurements went wrong. A gene whose value is extreme because of a flaw in one spot on the array will pull a whole centre out to itself, and that centre is then unavailable for the bulk of the data.

Squared Error Distortion

The repair is to score the centres by a typical distance rather than the worst one. The squared error distortion of a set of centres is the mean squared distance from a data point to its nearest centre, $\mathrm{Distortion}(\mathrm{Data}, \mathrm{Centers}) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} d(\mathrm{DataPoint}_i, \mathrm{Centers})^2$. Every point contributes, so one bad measurement moves the score by roughly $1/n$ of its own error instead of setting the score outright. 

def distortion(data, centers):
    return sum(distance(p, nearest(p, centers)) ** 2 for p in data) / len(data)

This gives two problems. The Squared Error Distortion Problem is the easy one: given $\mathrm{Data}$ and $\mathrm{Centers}$, compute the distortion, which the function above does in $O(n k m)$. The k-Means Clustering Problem is the hard one: given $\mathrm{Data}$ and $k$, find the $k$ centres minimising the distortion. It is NP-hard already for $k = 2$ when the dimension is part of the input, and NP-hard in the plane when $k$ is part of the input. With $k$ and $m$ both fixed it can be solved in polynomial time, which is no help here, since $m$ is the number of timepoints and $k$ is exactly what is being chosen.

The two objectives disagree on real data. On the points drawn above, the best possible k-centre solution has $\mathrm{MaxDistance}$ 77.1 and distortion 3594, while the best possible k-means solution has $\mathrm{MaxDistance}$ 143.8 and distortion 1554. Choosing the objective chooses whether the outlier gets a centre of its own.

The Center of Gravity Theorem

Squaring the distances is what makes the k-means problem tractable one cluster at a time, because for a single cluster the best centre has a closed form.

Center of Gravity Theorem. The centre of gravity of a set of points $\mathrm{Data}$, that is the point whose every coordinate is the mean of that coordinate over the points, is the unique solution of the k-Means Clustering Problem for $k = 1$.

The proof is one line of algebra. Distortion is a sum divided by the positive constant $n$, so minimising it is the same as minimising the sum. Write $g$ for the centre of gravity and $c$ for any candidate centre. Then $\sum_i \lVert x_i - c \rVert^2 = \sum_i \lVert (x_i - g) + (g - c) \rVert^2 = \sum_i \lVert x_i - g \rVert^2 + 2 (g - c) \cdot \sum_i (x_i - g) + n \lVert g - c \rVert^2$. The middle term vanishes because $\sum_i (x_i - g) = 0$, which is exactly the statement that $g$ is the mean. What is left is $\sum_i \lVert x_i - g \rVert^2 + n \lVert g - c \rVert^2$, a constant plus a term that is zero when $c = g$ and strictly positive otherwise, so $g$ is the unique minimum. 

def center_of_gravity(points):
    return [sum(axis) / len(points) for axis in zip(*points)]

The theorem does not solve the k-means problem, because it assumes the partition is already known. What it gives is one half of a fixed point: given the clusters, the best centres are their means. The other half is equally easy, since given the centres the best clusters assign each point to its nearest centre. Neither half is the answer, and alternating between them is.

The Lloyd Algorithm

The Lloyd algorithm chooses $k$ points from the data as initial centres and then repeats two steps until nothing changes. Centres to Clusters assigns every data point to the cluster of its nearest centre, breaking ties arbitrarily. Clusters to Centres replaces each centre by the centre of gravity of its cluster. The algorithm has converged when a round leaves the centres where they were, at which point the clusters are also unchanged and every further round is identical. 

def lloyd(data, k, centers=None):
    centers = list(centers) if centers else data[:k]
    while True:
        clusters = [[] for _ in centers]
        for point in data:
            clusters[centers.index(nearest(point, centers))].append(point)
        moved = [center_of_gravity(c) if c else centers[i]
                 for i, c in enumerate(clusters)]
        if all(distance(a, b) < 1e-9 for a, b in zip(moved, centers)):
            return moved
        centers = moved

Write $\phi(P, C) = \sum_{i} \sum_{x \in P_i} \lVert x - c_i \rVert^2$ for a partition $P$ into $k$ parts and a list $C$ of $k$ centres, which is $n$ times the distortion when $P$ puts every point with its nearest centre. The first step minimises $\phi$ over $P$ with $C$ held fixed, because the sum splits into one independent term per point and each point is put with the centre nearest it, and the second minimises $\phi$ over $C$ with $P$ held fixed, by the Center of Gravity Theorem applied to each part separately. So $\phi$ never increases. If ties are broken so that a point moves only when some centre is strictly nearer than the one it currently has, then any round that changes the partition strictly decreases $\phi$, so no partition can occur twice; there are finitely many partitions, so the algorithm stops. Each round costs $O(nkm)$, dominated by measuring every point against every centre.

When Lloyd Fails

Termination is not optimality. The distortion falls at every round and stops falling at a set of centres that no single round can improve, which is a local minimum of the objective and generally not the global one. Which local minimum is reached is fixed entirely by the initial centres, so the initialisation, and not the iteration, decides the quality of the answer.

The size of the gap is easy to measure. Running the algorithm from 400 random starts on the 27 points drawn above gives a best distortion of 531 and a worst of 2217, a factor of 4.2 between two runs of the same algorithm on the same data, and the best value is reached 80% of the time. The bad answer splits one group between two centres and covers the other two groups with one, and it strands a centre that owns no points at all, an empty cluster the algorithm has no way to refill.

Against brute force the same weakness shows on instances small enough to solve exactly. Over 60 random instances of nine points in the plane with $k = 3$, taking the first three points as centres, the Lloyd algorithm never beat the optimum, matched it 14 times, and missed it 46 times, in the worst case by a factor of 3.05.

k-Means++

Since the starting centres decide the outcome, the fix is to choose them better. Picking the farthest point each time, as in farthest first traversal, spreads the centres out but walks straight into every outlier. k-means++ keeps the spreading and drops the determinism: choose each new centre at random from the data, with a point's probability proportional to the square of its distance to the nearest centre already chosen.

A distant point is therefore likely to be chosen and an outlier is no longer certain to be, so a single bad measurement can no longer capture a centre by itself. The squared distance is the same quantity the distortion is built from, which is why the same weight appears here. 

import random


def kmeans_plus_plus(data, k):
    centers = [random.choice(data)]
    while len(centers) < k:
        weights = [distance(p, nearest(p, centers)) ** 2 for p in data]
        centers.append(random.choices(data, weights)[0])
    return centers

The improvement is measurable. On 300 datasets of three well separated groups of nine points, seeding Lloyd with three points drawn uniformly at random reaches the best distortion 78% of the time, while seeding it with kmeans_plus_plus reaches it 99% of the time. Neither is a guarantee, and the standard practice remains to run the whole procedure many times and keep the centres with the lowest distortion.

Hard and Soft Assignments

Every algorithm so far gives each point to exactly one cluster. That is a hard assignment, and it discards information whenever a point is not clearly nearer to one centre than another. A gene sitting halfway between the profile of two groups is recorded as a full member of one of them and as having nothing to do with the other, and the tie is broken by whichever centre the code happened to test first.

A soft assignment replaces the choice with a distribution: point $j$ is assigned to cluster $i$ with responsibility $\mathrm{HiddenMatrix}_{i,j} \in [0, 1]$, and the responsibilities of the $k$ clusters for a fixed point sum to 1. A point in the middle is then half in each cluster, which is what the data says about it. Turning that idea into an algorithm needs a way to compute the responsibilities, and the shortest route to it is a problem with no geometry in it at all.

Coin Flipping and Maximum Likelihood

A coin comes up heads with unknown probability $\theta$. Flipping it $n$ times and seeing $i$ heads, the probability of that outcome is $\theta^{i} (1 - \theta)^{n - i}$, which as a function of $\theta$ is the likelihood. The estimate of $\theta$ is the value maximising it. Taking the logarithm first leaves the maximum where it is, because $\log$ is increasing, and turns the product into the sum $i \log \theta + (n - i) \log(1 - \theta)$, whose derivative with respect to $\theta$ is $\frac{i}{\theta} - \frac{n - i}{1 - \theta}$. A maximum in the interior of the interval is a point where that derivative vanishes, so setting it to zero gives $i(1 - \theta) = \theta(n - i)$, which expands to $i - i\theta = n\theta - i\theta$ and leaves $\theta = i/n$. For $0 < i < n$ the likelihood is zero at $\theta = 0$ and at $\theta = 1$ and positive between them, so this one stationary point is the maximum, and the estimated bias is the observed fraction of heads.

Now take two coins with unknown biases $A$ and $B$. An experiment consists of picking one of them, flipping it $n$ times, and recording only the number of heads, so the record is a vector $\mathrm{Data}$ of head counts and the identity of the coin used in each experiment is lost. Write $\mathrm{HiddenVector}_j = 1$ if experiment $j$ used coin $A$ and $0$ if it used coin $B$.

Knowing $\mathrm{HiddenVector}$ makes the biases immediate, since each coin's estimate is its own total heads over its own total flips, $A = \frac{\sum_j \mathrm{HiddenVector}_j \cdot \mathrm{Data}_j} {n \sum_j \mathrm{HiddenVector}_j}$ and $B = \frac{\sum_j (1 - \mathrm{HiddenVector}_j) \cdot \mathrm{Data}_j} {n \sum_j (1 - \mathrm{HiddenVector}_j)}$. Knowing $A$ and $B$ instead makes the hidden vector immediate, since experiment $j$ is attributed to whichever coin makes its outcome more likely, comparing $A^{\mathrm{Data}_j}(1 - A)^{n - \mathrm{Data}_j}$ against the same expression in $B$. Neither is known, and each is easy given the other.

This is the same shape as clustering. The head counts are one-dimensional data points, the two coins are two clusters, the biases are the centres, and the hidden vector is the assignment. The two steps of the Lloyd algorithm are the two implications just written down: Centres to Clusters is $(\mathrm{Data}, ?, \mathrm{Parameters}) \rightarrow \mathrm{HiddenVector}$, and Clusters to Centres is $(\mathrm{Data}, \mathrm{HiddenVector}, ?) \rightarrow \mathrm{Parameters}$.

Expectation Maximization

Alternating those two implications is expectation maximization. The E-step takes the current parameters and computes the hidden data, and the M-step takes the hidden data and recomputes the parameters by maximum likelihood. The Lloyd algorithm is the case in which the hidden data is forced to be a 0/1 assignment, which is why it behaves the way it does: it converges, it never worsens its objective, and it lands in a local optimum that its starting point selected.

The soft version keeps the same two steps and lets the hidden data be fractional. In the E-step, instead of asking which centre is nearest, the responsibility of centre $x_i$ for point $\mathrm{Data}_j$ is its share of a total, $\mathrm{HiddenMatrix}_{i,j} = \frac{\Pr(\mathrm{Data}_j \mid x_i)} {\sum_{t=1}^{k} \Pr(\mathrm{Data}_j \mid x_t)}$. In the M-step, instead of a plain mean, each centre becomes the mean of all the points weighted by its responsibility for them.

Soft k-Means Clustering

What remains is to give $\Pr(\mathrm{Data}_j \mid x_i)$ a form. Soft k-means takes it proportional to $e^{-\beta \, d(\mathrm{Data}_j, x_i)}$, with a constant of proportionality that does not depend on $i$ and so cancels between the numerator and the denominator, which is why only the exponentials are ever computed. The parameter $\beta \geq 0$ is the stiffness.

Both extremes follow from the formula. At $\beta = 0$ every exponential equals 1, so every responsibility is exactly $1/k$ and the assignment carries no information. At the other end, let $x_1$ be the centre nearest $\mathrm{Data}_j$ and let $\delta > 0$ be the amount by which the second nearest is further away. Dividing the numerator and the denominator by $e^{-\beta d(\mathrm{Data}_j, x_1)}$ makes the responsibility of $x_1$ equal to $1 / \left(1 + \sum_{t \geq 2} e^{-\beta (d(\mathrm{Data}_j, x_t) - d(\mathrm{Data}_j, x_1))} \right)$, and every exponent in that sum is at most $-\beta \delta$. So as $\beta \to \infty$ the sum tends to 0, the responsibility of the nearest centre tends to 1 and the rest to 0, and the soft assignment becomes the hard one, provided the nearest centre is unique. 

from math import exp


def responsibilities(data, centers, beta):
    near = [min(distance(c, p) for c in centers) for p in data]
    pull = [[exp(-beta * (distance(c, p) - near[j])) for j, p in enumerate(data)]
            for c in centers]
    total = [sum(row[j] for row in pull) for j in range(len(data))]
    return [[row[j] / total[j] for j in range(len(data))] for row in pull]


def soft_lloyd(data, k, beta, steps=100):
    centers = data[:k]
    for _ in range(steps):
        hidden = responsibilities(data, centers, beta)
        centers = [[sum(row[j] * data[j][d] for j in range(len(data))) / sum(row)
                    for d in range(len(data[0]))] for row in hidden]
    return centers

The subtraction of near[j] in the E-step is the cancellation derived above, chosen so that the smallest exponent for each point is exactly 0. It is not an optimisation but a requirement: a double underflows to exactly zero once its exponent drops below $-745.13$, so without the shift every entry of a column becomes 0 at large $\beta$ and the normalisation divides by zero. With the shift the largest term in every column is 1, so the denominator is at least 1 whatever $\beta$ is.

The M-step here is the weighted centre of gravity: each coordinate of centre $i$ is $\frac{\sum_j \mathrm{HiddenMatrix}_{i,j} \cdot \mathrm{Data}_{j}} {\sum_j \mathrm{HiddenMatrix}_{i,j}}$, which reduces to the ordinary mean when the responsibilities are 0 and 1. Unlike lloyd there is no convergence test, because the centres in general move by ever smaller amounts without ever repeating exactly, so the loop runs a fixed number of steps.

Hierarchical Clustering

Both algorithms so far need $k$ in advance, and for expression data there is rarely a reason to prefer one value over another. Hierarchical clustering avoids the choice by producing every value at once. It works from a matrix of pairwise distances rather than from the points, starts with each element in its own cluster, and repeatedly merges the two closest clusters until one remains. The distance between two clusters is the average distance between their members, and merging updates that average by weighting each contribution by cluster size. 

def hierarchical_clustering(matrix, n):
    clusters = {i: [i] for i in range(n)}
    gap = {(i, j): matrix[i][j] for i in range(n) for j in range(n)}
    merges, fresh = [], n
    while len(clusters) > 1:
        i, j = min(((a, b) for a in clusters for b in clusters if a < b),
                   key=lambda pair: gap[pair])
        new, fresh = fresh, fresh + 1
        size_i, size_j = len(clusters[i]), len(clusters[j])
        for other in clusters:
            if other in (i, j):
                continue
            gap[min(new, other), max(new, other)] = (
                gap[min(i, other), max(i, other)] * size_i
                + gap[min(j, other), max(j, other)] * size_j) / (size_i + size_j)
        clusters[new] = clusters[i] + clusters[j]
        del clusters[i], clusters[j]
        merges.append(clusters[new])
    return merges

The sequence of merges is a tree whose leaves are the original elements, drawn with each merge at a height equal to the distance at which it happened. Cutting the tree at a height leaves one cluster per surviving branch, and sliding the cut from the leaves to the root runs through every number of clusters from $n$ down to 1. The choice of $k$ becomes the choice of where to cut, made after seeing the tree rather than before.

Each round scans every pair of surviving clusters, so the cost is $O(n^3)$ for the whole run, against $O(nkm)$ per round for Lloyd. On the other hand it needs only the distance matrix, so it applies whenever a distance can be defined even if the objects are not points in a space, and it is deterministic, with no initialisation and no local optima.

Hierarchical Clustering Is UPGMA

The merge rule above is the one Chapter 7 used to build evolutionary trees. UPGMA also starts with singleton clusters, also merges the closest pair, and also replaces their two rows by one whose distances are the size-weighted averages of theirs. Running hierarchical_clustering and upgma on the same matrix produces the same merges in the same order, because the code that picks and updates is identical; the only part of upgma missing here is the bookkeeping that assigns ages to internal nodes and turns the merges into a tree with branch lengths.

The two uses of the same computation claim different things. In Chapter 7 the tree was a hypothesis about descent, and the ages were times, which is why the ultrametric assumption mattered and why UPGMA is wrong when lineages evolve at different rates. Here nothing is being claimed about history. The tree is a record of the order in which groups fused, and its heights are distances, so no assumption about the data can make it wrong; it can only be more or less useful.

Application: How Did Yeast Become a Wine Maker?

The diauxic shift dataset has 6153 genes, and most of them do nothing across the experiment. The genes worth clustering are the ones that move, so the set is restricted to those whose $\log_2$ ratio spans at least 2.5 between its highest and lowest timepoint, a 5.7-fold change, which keeps 254 genes. Each of those is then rescaled to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 across its seven timepoints, so that clustering groups genes by the shape of their response and not by how large it was.

Running kmeans_plus_plus followed by lloyd with $k = 6$, keeping the best of 40 restarts, reaches distortion 0.3704, against 0.4029 for a single run started from the first six genes in the file. The six clusters are shown below, each drawn as the profile of its centre across the seven timepoints.

The split is between genes that fall and genes that rise, and the biology of each group is consistent across its members without any biological information having been used to build it. The two falling clusters, of 46 and 77 genes, are the machinery of growth: they contain all 36 ribosomal protein genes in the filtered set, 32 of them in the single cluster of 77, together with translation factors and the enzymes that make amino acids and nucleotides. Making ribosomes is what a cell does when it is dividing, and the culture stops dividing when the glucose runs out.

The rising clusters are the respiration programme. The 22-gene cluster is flat until the last timepoint and then rises sharply, and it holds PCK1 and FBP1, the two enzymes specific to gluconeogenesis, which builds sugar back up from smaller molecules, alongside ICL1 and MLS1 of the glyoxylate cycle and ACS1, which activates the acetate that ethanol is oxidised into. Their measured rises at the final timepoint are 14.3, 14.3, 12.5, 9.1 and 12.5 fold. The 37-gene cluster rises earlier and contains CIT1 and CIT2 of the citric acid cycle, the respiratory chain subunit COX5A, the transporter JEN1, and HAP4, which is the transcriptional activator of the respiratory genes.

A dendrogram over a handful of named genes shows the same structure without $k$ being chosen at all.

So the answer the data gives is that yeast makes wine by keeping the second programme switched off. Fermentation and respiration are not two settings of one system but two sets of genes, and while glucose is present the respiration set is held down and the growth set is held up. Ethanol accumulates for as long as that holds, which is the whole of a wine fermentation. Only when the glucose is exhausted is the switch thrown, and the clusters show it thrown once, in a couple of hours, across hundreds of genes at a time.

What the clustering establishes is co-expression and nothing more. It shows that these genes move together and when they move, which is enough to read the programme off the data and enough to guess the function of an uncharacterised gene from the company it keeps. It does not show that any gene controls any other. HAP4 landing among the respiratory genes is consistent with its being their activator, but an activator and its targets rise together whichever way the causation runs, and separating the two needs an experiment that changes HAP4 and watches what follows, not a partition of the points.
