Chapter 11: Was T. rex Just a Big Chicken?

A protein is a chain of amino acids, and for most of them the chain can be read off a gene. There are two situations in which it cannot. The first was Chapter 4: a non-ribosomal peptide is assembled by an enzyme, so no gene spells it out, and its sequence has to be recovered from the masses of its fragments. The second is a fossil. DNA breaks down over tens of thousands of years, while collagen, the protein that makes up most of the organic matter in bone, is wound into a triple helix and mineralised, and fragments of it have been reported from bone tens of millions of years old. In 2007 a group extracted a small number of peptides from the femur of a Tyrannosaurus rex and read their sequences on a mass spectrometer. This chapter builds the algorithms that turn a list of masses into a peptide, then into a name, then into a number saying how much the name is worth, and applies them to those peptides.

Proteins Without a Gene

A mass spectrometer takes many copies of a molecule, breaks each copy into fragments, and weighs the fragments. Masses are in daltons (Da) and are rounded to whole numbers here, as in Chapter 4, so that a peptide is a list of integers. That chapter's table of amino acid masses carries over unchanged, and so does its central fact: the twenty amino acids have only eighteen distinct integer masses, because leucine and isoleucine both weigh 113 and lysine and glutamine both weigh 128. No measurement of mass ever separates either pair, so every algorithm below works over eighteen masses and prints one letter for each. 

MASS = {'G': 57, 'A': 71, 'S': 87, 'P': 97, 'V': 99, 'T': 101, 'C': 103,
        'I': 113, 'L': 113, 'N': 114, 'D': 115, 'K': 128, 'Q': 128,
        'E': 129, 'M': 131, 'H': 137, 'F': 147, 'R': 156, 'Y': 163, 'W': 186}


def distinct_masses(mass=MASS):
    seen, distinct = set(), {}
    for amino, weight in sorted(mass.items()):
        if weight not in seen:
            seen.add(weight)
            distinct[amino] = weight
    return distinct


def prefix_masses(peptide, mass=MASS):
    prefix = [0]
    for amino in peptide:
        prefix.append(prefix[-1] + mass[amino])
    return prefix

The amount of protein that survives in a fossil is small, and what is recovered is not a chain but a handful of short pieces of one. The two archived records of the T. rex work, which the last section of this chapter fetches and uses, are 570 and 18 residues long, and in the longer one every position that was not sequenced is written X. Five stretches carry letters, and they total 89 residues. Modern chicken type I collagen is 1453 and 1363 residues in its two chains, so the fossil evidence covers 89 positions out of 2816, or 3.2 per cent of the molecule. Every algorithm in this chapter has to work from that little.

The problem this chapter solves has two halves. Given the masses coming off the instrument, reconstruct the peptide that produced them; and given a peptide so reconstructed, decide whether it means anything. The second half turns out to need as much machinery as the first, because a database of proteins is large enough that some peptide in it fits any spectrum reasonably well by chance.

The Ideal Spectrum

A peptide of $n$ amino acids has $n - 1$ internal bonds. Breaking it at one of them produces two pieces: a prefix carrying the first few amino acids and a suffix carrying the rest. A spectrometer works on many copies at once, so every bond is broken in some copy, and the instrument sees the masses of all $n - 1$ prefixes and all $n - 1$ suffixes, plus the mass of the whole peptide. The masses of the prefixes are the running sums of the amino acid masses, which is what prefix_masses computes, and the mass of the suffix cut at the same bond is the total minus the mass of that prefix.

The ideal spectrum of a peptide is the sorted collection of the masses of all its prefixes and all its suffixes. It differs from the cyclospectrum of Chapter 4 in two ways: the peptide is linear rather than a ring, so a fragment is a prefix or a suffix rather than any contiguous stretch; and both ends of the molecule contribute, so each cut is seen twice. This is the spectrum an instrument would return if it detected every fragment and nothing else. 

def ideal_spectrum(peptide, mass=MASS):
    prefix = prefix_masses(peptide, mass)
    total = prefix[-1]
    return sorted({p for p in prefix[1:]} | {total - p for p in prefix[:-1]})

Two masses of an ideal spectrum are twins when they sum to the mass of the whole peptide. The prefix of mass $m$ and the suffix of mass $\text{total} - m$ come from the same cut, so they carry exactly the same information about where that cut is, and every mass in the spectrum has a twin. This is why an ideal spectrum of a peptide of $n$ amino acids holds at most $2n - 1$ masses rather than $2n$: the prefix and the suffix of the last cut are both the whole molecule, and coincide. It holds fewer when two prefix masses sum to the total, since a prefix is then equal to a suffix and the two collide as well, which happens for about one tryptic peptide of the chicken proteome in sixteen. Twins are used twice below, once to fold a real spectrum onto half its coordinates and once to explain why a peptide and its reversal can never be told apart.

The Spectrum Graph

Reconstruction starts from the difference between two masses. If the spectrum holds $s_i$ and $s_j$ with $s_j - s_i$ equal to the mass of some amino acid $a$, then a peptide explaining the spectrum may have $a$ sitting between the cut at $s_i$ and the cut at $s_j$. Making a node for every mass, adding a node 0 for the empty prefix, and drawing an edge labelled $a$ from $s_i$ to $s_j$ whenever the difference is the mass of $a$ gives the spectrum graph. It is a directed acyclic graph, because every edge increases the mass.

Construct the Graph of a Spectrum Problem. Given a spectrum, build its spectrum graph. 

def spectrum_graph(spectrum, mass=MASS):
    alphabet = distinct_masses(mass)
    nodes = [0] + list(spectrum)
    graph = {node: [] for node in nodes}
    for i, source in enumerate(nodes):
        for target in nodes[i + 1:]:
            for amino, weight in sorted(alphabet.items()):
                if target - source == weight:
                    graph[source].append((target, amino))
    return graph

The construction costs $O(m^2)$ for a spectrum of $m$ masses, the inner loop over eighteen masses being a constant. On the spectrum $57, 71, 154, 185, 301, 332, 415, 429, 486$ it produces ten nodes and twelve edges.

Decoding an Ideal Spectrum

A path from node 0 to the node holding the largest mass spells a peptide: read the edge labels in order. Every peptide that explains the spectrum appears as such a path, since its prefix masses are all in the spectrum and consecutive prefixes differ by one amino acid. The converse fails. A path uses only the masses it happens to step on, and says nothing about the masses it skips, so the peptide it spells may have an ideal spectrum that is missing some of the given masses or contains masses that were not given.

Decoding an Ideal Spectrum Problem. Given a spectrum, find a peptide whose ideal spectrum equals it.

The fix is to check. Walk the graph from the source, and at the sink compare the ideal spectrum of the peptide just spelled against the input, accepting only on equality. The search is a depth-first walk with backtracking, and each check costs $O(n \log n)$ for a peptide of $n$ amino acids. 

def decode_ideal_spectrum(spectrum, mass=MASS):
    graph = spectrum_graph(spectrum, mass)
    sink = spectrum[-1]

    def extend(node, peptide):
        if node == sink:
            if ideal_spectrum(peptide, mass) == list(spectrum):
                return peptide
            return None
        for target, amino in graph[node]:
            found = extend(target, peptide + amino)
            if found is not None:
                return found
        return None

    return extend(0, '')

The sample spectrum has four paths from source to sink, and only two of them survive the check. GPFKG and GKFPG both step from 57 to 185 or from 301 to 429 with a lysine, which skips the masses 71 and 415, and their ideal spectra are missing exactly those two. GPFNA and ANFPG both pass.

Theorem. A peptide and its reversal have the same ideal spectrum, so no spectrum ever determines a peptide uniquely.

The proof is one line about twins. Reversing a peptide turns each of its prefixes into a suffix of the same mass and each of its suffixes into a prefix of the same mass, so the two sets that make up the ideal spectrum are exchanged and their union is unchanged. GPFNA and ANFPG in the figure are one such pair. Nothing later in the chapter removes this ambiguity, because nothing later measures anything but mass; what removes it in practice is knowing which end of the peptide the instrument charged, and that is a property of the chemistry rather than of the arithmetic.

Reconstruction from an ideal spectrum is a solved problem, and it is also the wrong problem. Every step above assumed that the input holds every mass of the ideal spectrum and nothing else.

What a Real Spectrum Looks Like

Nothing measures a whole protein this way. A protein of a thousand residues has a mass in the hundred thousands, far above the range in which an instrument can resolve single daltons, and it does not fragment cleanly. The sample is therefore digested first with a protease, an enzyme that cuts a protein chain at particular residues. Trypsin is the usual one: it cuts after every lysine and every arginine, which over the chicken proteome produces pieces of median length 6 and mean length 8.5, short enough that a spectrometer resolves single daltons across the whole fragment. Each peptide is then broken again, one bond at a time, and the pieces are weighed.

The output is a list of masses with an intensity attached to each, the intensity being how many fragments of that mass were counted. It differs from an ideal spectrum in three ways. Some fragments are never detected, so masses are missing. Some peaks come from contaminants, from fragments that broke in more than one place, or from the instrument itself, so masses are present that belong to no prefix and no suffix. And the measured mass is not exactly the fragment mass, though rounding to the nearest dalton hides that here.

One missing mass breaks every path through the spectrum graph that needed it, and the check in decode_ideal_spectrum then rejects every candidate, so the algorithm returns nothing at all. One extra mass adds edges that spell peptides that were never in the sample. Exact reconstruction is the wrong goal for data of this kind, and the rest of the chapter replaces it with scoring: rank every candidate peptide by how well it agrees with the spectrum, and return the best.

The simplest score counts agreements. The shared peak count of a peptide and a spectrum is the number of masses they have in common, and the intensity count adds the intensities of those masses instead of counting them. Both are usable and neither is convenient: they are defined on two sets of integers, which is an awkward object to run a dynamic program over. The next two sections turn both the peptide and the spectrum into vectors of fixed length, after which the score is a dot product and the search for the best peptide is a longest path.

Peptide Vectors

Fix the total mass $m$. The peptide vector of a peptide of mass $m$ is the vector of $m$ coordinates, indexed by the masses $1$ to $m$, holding 1 at each prefix mass and 0 everywhere else. A peptide of $n$ amino acids contributes exactly $n$ ones, the last of them at coordinate $m$. Suffix masses are not recorded, which loses nothing: the suffix of mass $m - i$ exists precisely when the prefix of mass $i$ does, so the prefix coordinates already determine the whole ideal spectrum.

Converting a Peptide into a Peptide Vector Problem. Given a peptide, compute its peptide vector. 

def peptide_vector(peptide, mass=MASS):
    prefix = prefix_masses(peptide, mass)
    vector = [0] * prefix[-1]
    for p in prefix[1:]:
        vector[p - 1] = 1
    return vector

The map is invertible. Reading the positions of the ones in order gives the prefix masses, and the differences between consecutive prefix masses are the amino acid masses, so the peptide is recovered one letter at a time. A binary vector comes from a peptide exactly when every one of those differences is the mass of some amino acid, and when it is not, no peptide has that vector.

Converting a Peptide Vector into a Peptide Problem. Given a binary vector, find a peptide whose peptide vector it is. 

def peptide_from_vector(vector, mass=MASS):
    inverse = {weight: amino for amino, weight in distinct_masses(mass).items()}
    prefix = [0] + [i + 1 for i, bit in enumerate(vector) if bit == 1]
    peptide = ''
    for previous, current in zip(prefix, prefix[1:]):
        if current - previous not in inverse:
            return None
        peptide += inverse[current - previous]
    return peptide

Both directions run in $O(m)$. The examples that follow use two imaginary amino acids, X of mass 4 and Z of mass 5, because a real peptide of five residues has a mass near 600 and a vector of 600 coordinates is not a thing to draw. Over that alphabet XZZXX has prefix masses 4, 9, 14, 18 and 22, and its peptide vector is the 22 coordinates with ones in those five places.

Spectral Vectors

The spectrum is turned into a vector of the same length. The spectral vector of a spectrum whose parent peptide has mass $m$ is the vector $s_1, \dots, s_m$ in which $s_i$ is an amplitude measuring the evidence that some prefix of the peptide weighs $i$. A coordinate carrying a peak gets a positive amplitude and one carrying no peak gets a negative one; where the two numbers come from is the next section.

Only prefix coordinates exist in this vector, and the spectrum reports suffix fragments as well. Twins fix that. A peak at mass $j$ is evidence for a prefix of mass $j$ if it is a prefix fragment, and evidence for a prefix of mass $m - j$ if it is a suffix fragment, and nothing in the measurement says which. Coordinate $i$ therefore counts as carrying a peak when the spectrum has a peak at $i$ or at $m - i$, which folds the spectrum onto the prefix coordinates and doubles the evidence available at each one. 

def fold_twins(peaks, total):
    return {i for i in range(1, total + 1) if i in peaks or total - i in peaks}

With both objects vectors of length $m$, the score of a peptide against a spectrum is their dot product. Since the peptide vector is binary, the dot product is the sum of the amplitudes at the peptide's prefix masses, one term per amino acid. 

def score_peptide(peptide, spectrum, mass=MASS):
    prefix = prefix_masses(peptide, mass)
    if prefix[-1] != len(spectrum):
        return None
    return sum(spectrum[p - 1] for p in prefix[1:])

Two peptides are comparable under this score only if they have the same mass, since otherwise their vectors have different lengths. That is not a restriction in practice: the instrument measures the mass of the undissociated peptide along with the masses of its fragments, so $m$ is known before the search starts, and the candidates are exactly the peptides of that mass.

From Peaks to Amplitudes

The amplitudes were left undefined above, and choosing them arbitrarily would make the score arbitrary. They follow from a model of what the instrument does. Write $b_i = 1$ if the spectrum has a peak at coordinate $i$ and $b_i = 0$ otherwise, and let $p_i = 1$ if the peptide really does have a prefix of mass $i$. The instrument detects a real fragment with probability $\theta$, and reports a peak where there is no fragment with probability $\rho$, independently at each coordinate. Both numbers are properties of the machine and are measured once from data of known composition.

Most Likely Peptide Vector Problem. Given the observed vector $b$, find the peptide vector $p$ maximising $\Pr(p \mid b)$.

The proof that this is the dot product of the previous section is three steps of algebra. By Bayes, $\Pr(p \mid b) \propto \Pr(b \mid p)\Pr(p)$, and taking every peptide of mass $m$ to be equally likely a priori makes $\Pr(p)$ a constant that does not affect the maximum. The coordinates are independent given $p$, so $\Pr(b \mid p) = \prod_i \Pr(b_i \mid p_i)$, and taking logarithms turns the product into $\sum_i \log \Pr(b_i \mid p_i)$. Adding and subtracting $\sum_i \log \Pr(b_i \mid p_i = 0)$, which does not depend on $p$ at all, leaves $\sum_{i : p_i = 1} \log \frac{\Pr(b_i \mid p_i = 1)}{\Pr(b_i \mid p_i = 0)}$ plus a constant. That sum is the dot product of $p$ with the vector whose coordinates are those log odds, so maximising the score is maximising the posterior probability.

The two amplitudes are therefore $\log(\theta/\rho)$ at a coordinate carrying a peak and $\log\frac{1-\theta}{1-\rho}$ at one that does not. The first is positive and the second negative whenever $\theta > \rho$, which is the only case in which the instrument is worth using. A peptide is rewarded for putting a prefix where there is a peak and punished for putting one where there is not, and the ratio of reward to punishment is fixed by the machine rather than chosen.

Multiplying every amplitude by the same positive constant changes no comparison between peptides, so the pair can be rescaled to whatever is convenient. At $\theta = 0.7$ and $\rho = 0.02$ the two values are $+3.5553$ and $-1.1838$, whose ratio is $3.0034$, so dividing by $1.1838$ gives $+3$ and $-1$ to within a third of a per cent. Integer amplitudes are used from here on, which matters in the dynamic programs of the sections on dictionaries, where the score has to index an array.

Sequencing a Peptide

Peptide Sequencing Problem. Given a spectral vector, find a peptide of maximum score against it.

Build a graph on the masses $0, 1, \dots, m$, with an edge from $i$ to $j$ labelled $a$ whenever $j - i$ is the mass of the amino acid $a$, and give node $i$ the weight $s_i$, with node 0 weight zero. A path from 0 to $m$ visits exactly the prefix masses of the peptide it spells, so the sum of the weights of the nodes it visits is that peptide's score. Every peptide of mass $m$ is such a path and every such path is a peptide of mass $m$, so the problem is to find a maximum-weight path from source to sink in a node-weighted directed acyclic graph.

The nodes are already in topological order, since every edge goes from a smaller mass to a larger one, so a single sweep from 0 to $m$ suffices. Let $\textit{best}[i]$ be the largest score of any path from 0 to $i$. Then $\textit{best}[i]$ is $s_i$ plus the largest $\textit{best}[i - |a|]$ over the eighteen masses $|a|$, and a node with no predecessor reachable from 0 is unreachable and stays empty. Recording which predecessor won each maximum allows the peptide to be read back from the sink. 

def sequence_peptide(spectrum, mass=MASS):
    alphabet = distinct_masses(mass)
    weight = [0] + list(spectrum)
    m = len(spectrum)
    best = [None] * (m + 1)
    back = [None] * (m + 1)
    best[0] = 0
    for i in range(1, m + 1):
        for amino, step in sorted(alphabet.items()):
            if i - step >= 0 and best[i - step] is not None:
                score = best[i - step] + weight[i]
                if best[i] is None or score > best[i]:
                    best[i] = score
                    back[i] = (i - step, amino)
    if best[m] is None:
        return None, None
    peptide = ''
    node = m
    while node > 0:
        node, amino = back[node]
        peptide = amino + peptide
    return peptide, best[m]

The graph has $m + 1$ nodes and at most $18(m + 1)$ edges, so the sweep costs $O(m)$ with a constant of eighteen. For a tryptic peptide of mass around 2000 that is some tens of thousands of operations, which is nothing. On the sample spectral vector of length 22 over the toy alphabet the algorithm returns XZZXX with score 22, and exhaustive enumeration of all peptides of mass 22 confirms that no other reaches it.

Identifying a Peptide in a Proteome

Sequencing returns the best peptide over all peptides of mass $m$, and there are a great many of those: counting them with the same one-line recurrence gives $6.5 \times 10^{30}$ peptides of mass 2760, and almost all of them are strings that no organism makes. When the species is known, the candidates should instead be the substrings of its proteome. This is a smaller and far better set, and searching it is peptide identification rather than sequencing.

Peptide Identification Problem. Given a spectral vector and a proteome, find the substring of the proteome with maximum score against the spectrum.

A substring can be scored at all only if its mass is exactly $m$. Extending each start one residue at a time, accumulating the mass and the score together, and stopping as soon as the mass exceeds $m$ gives the straightforward algorithm. The inner loop runs at most $m/57$ times, that being the longest a peptide of mass $m$ can be, so the cost is $O(nm/57)$ for a proteome of $n$ residues. 

def identify_peptide(spectrum, proteome, mass=MASS):
    m = len(spectrum)
    best, best_score = None, None
    for start in range(len(proteome)):
        total = 0
        score = 0
        for end in range(start, len(proteome)):
            total += mass[proteome[end]]
            if total > m:
                break
            score += spectrum[total - 1]
            if total == m and (best_score is None or score > best_score):
                best, best_score = proteome[start:end + 1], score
    return best, best_score

A run of the instrument produces thousands of spectra, one per peptide it happened to isolate, and each is searched against the same proteome. A pair of a peptide and a spectrum whose score reaches a threshold is a peptide-spectrum match, written PSM, and collecting them is the search that a proteomics experiment actually performs.

PSM Search Problem. Given a set of spectral vectors, a proteome and a threshold, find every peptide-spectrum match scoring at least the threshold. 

def psm_search(spectra, proteome, threshold, mass=MASS):
    matches = set()
    for spectrum in spectra:
        peptide, score = identify_peptide(spectrum, proteome, mass)
        if peptide is not None and score >= threshold:
            matches.add(peptide)
    return matches

Filtering by Mass

The chicken proteome is 27,302,528 residues, and a peptide of mass 2760 can be up to 48 residues long, so the loop above performs on the order of a billion additions for a single spectrum. Almost all of that work is spent on substrings whose mass will never equal $m$. Write $\textit{prefix}[i]$ for the mass of the first $i$ residues of the whole proteome. This array is strictly increasing, and the substring from $i$ to $j$ has mass $\textit{prefix}[j] - \textit{prefix}[i]$, so the only $j$ that can end a candidate starting at $i$ is the one with $\textit{prefix}[j] = \textit{prefix}[i] + m$, and binary search finds it or proves it absent in $O(\log n)$.

The number of starts that survive is the number of times the value $\textit{prefix}[i] + m$ happens to be one of the prefix masses, which is about $n$ divided by the average amino acid mass, near 110. Only those starts are ever scored, at $O(m/57)$ each. The array of prefix masses is built once for the whole proteome and reused for every spectrum in the run. 

import bisect


def identify_peptide_filtered(spectrum, proteome, mass=MASS):
    m = len(spectrum)
    prefix = [0]
    for amino in proteome:
        prefix.append(prefix[-1] + mass[amino])
    best, best_score, candidates = None, None, 0
    for start in range(len(proteome)):
        target = prefix[start] + m
        end = bisect.bisect_left(prefix, target, start + 1)
        if end > len(proteome) or prefix[end] != target:
            continue
        candidates += 1
        score = 0
        for t in range(start + 1, end + 1):
            score += spectrum[prefix[t] - prefix[start] - 1]
        if best_score is None or score > best_score:
            best, best_score = proteome[start:end], score
    return best, best_score, candidates

The two versions return the same score on 60 random proteomes with random spectra, and the count of surviving starts equals the count of substrings of mass exactly $m$ found by brute force on 20 more. On the real chicken proteome the filtered search takes six seconds per spectrum, and about 240,000 substrings out of 27 million starts have the right mass, which is the predicted factor of a hundred or so.

The Monkey and the Typewriter

A search over 27 million residues returns a best-scoring peptide whether or not the spectrum came from that organism at all, so a score on its own says nothing. What is needed is the score that chance alone would produce. The classical statement of the problem is the infinite monkey theorem: a monkey typing at random will eventually type any given text, and the quantity worth computing is not whether it happens but how often.

Monkey and Typewriter Problem. Given a dictionary of strings and a random text of length $n$ over an alphabet of $k$ letters, find the expected number of occurrences of dictionary words in the text.

The proof is one application of linearity of expectation. For each of the $n$ positions and each word $w$ of the dictionary, let $X_{i,w}$ be 1 if $w$ starts at position $i$. The letters are independent and uniform, so $\Pr(X_{i,w} = 1) = k^{-|w|}$, and the expected total is $\sum_i \sum_w k^{-|w|} = n \sum_w k^{-|w|}$. The sum $\sum_w k^{-|w|}$ depends on the dictionary alone and is called its probability, so the expected count is the length of the text times the probability of the dictionary, however the words overlap and whatever their lengths.

A proteome is not random text, so the theorem gives an estimate rather than a fact about it. The empirical version of the same idea is a decoy: a second database, the same size as the real one, built so that it contains no true answers. Searching both and comparing the score distributions gives the false discovery rate, the proportion of accepted matches that a database with nothing in it would also have produced.

Theorem. A peptide and its reversal have the same score against any spectral vector obtained by folding twins, so reversing the proteins of a proteome does not produce a usable decoy.

The proof is the twin identity again. Folding sets coordinate $i$ to the positive amplitude exactly when the spectrum has a peak at $i$ or at $m - i$, and that condition is unchanged by swapping $i$ with $m - i$, so $s_i = s_{m-i}$ for every $i$. The prefix masses of the reversal of a peptide are the numbers $m - p$ for $p$ a prefix mass of the peptide, so the reversal's score is $\sum_p s_{m-p} = \sum_p s_p$, which is the peptide's own score. Reversing every protein of the chicken proteome does indeed leave the best score of all five T. rex spectra exactly unchanged. Shuffling the residues of each protein instead destroys the correspondence, and every decoy below is shuffled.

Spectral Dictionaries

The decoy gives an empirical answer at the cost of a second full search. The monkey theorem gives an analytic one, and to apply it the dictionary has to be named. The spectral dictionary of a spectral vector at a threshold is the set of all peptides scoring at least the threshold against it. Its probability, in the sense of the previous section, is $\sum_P 20^{-|P|}$ over the peptides $P$ it contains, and multiplying that by the number of positions in the proteome gives the expected number of matches that good from a database of that size and nothing in it. That expected number is the E-value of the match.

The dictionary is far too large to enumerate, and neither its size nor its probability needs it. Both are sums over peptides of mass $m$ grouped by score, and both satisfy the same recurrence. Let $\textit{Size}(i, t)$ be the number of peptides of mass $i$ scoring exactly $t$ against the first $i$ coordinates of the spectrum. Any such peptide ends in some amino acid $a$, whose prefix contributes $s_i$ to the score, so removing $a$ leaves a peptide of mass $i - |a|$ scoring $t - s_i$.

Size of a Spectral Dictionary Problem. Given a spectral vector and a threshold, find the number of peptides in the spectral dictionary.

Hence $\textit{Size}(i, t) = \sum_a \textit{Size}(i - |a|, t - s_i)$ with $\textit{Size}(0, 0) = 1$ and everything else zero, and the answer is $\sum_{t \geq \textit{threshold}} \textit{Size}(m, t)$. The scores that can occur are bounded: a peptide of mass $m$ has at most $m/57$ amino acids and each contributes one amplitude, so with amplitudes $+3$ and $-1$ the score lies between $-m/57$ and $3m/57$, and the table has $O(m^2/57)$ cells. Since negative scores index an array, the implementation stores the row offset by a fixed amount. 

def dictionary_table(spectrum, max_score, mass=MASS):
    m = len(spectrum)
    low = min(0, min(spectrum) * m)
    size = [[0] * (max_score - low + 1) for _ in range(m + 1)]
    size[0][-low] = 1
    for i in range(1, m + 1):
        for t in range(len(size[i])):
            previous = t - spectrum[i - 1]
            if 0 <= previous < len(size[i]):
                for step in mass.values():
                    if i - step >= 0:
                        size[i][t] += size[i - step][previous]
    return size, low


def dictionary_size(spectrum, threshold, max_score, mass=MASS):
    size, low = dictionary_table(spectrum, max_score, mass)
    return sum(size[len(spectrum)][t - low]
               for t in range(threshold, max_score + 1))

Probability of a Spectral Dictionary Problem. Given a spectral vector and a threshold, find the probability of the spectral dictionary.

The probability satisfies the same recurrence with each term weighted by the chance of drawing that amino acid. Writing $\Pr(i, t)$ for $\sum_P 20^{-|P|}$ over peptides $P$ of mass $i$ scoring $t$, appending one amino acid multiplies the weight of each peptide by $1/20$, so $\Pr(i, t) = \frac{1}{20}\sum_a \Pr(i - |a|, t - s_i)$ with $\Pr(0, 0) = 1$. The sum runs over all twenty amino acids rather than the eighteen distinct masses, because the model draws letters and not masses. 

def dictionary_probability(spectrum, threshold, max_score, mass=MASS):
    m = len(spectrum)
    low = min(0, min(spectrum) * m)
    probability = [[0.0] * (max_score - low + 1) for _ in range(m + 1)]
    probability[0][-low] = 1.0
    share = 1 / len(mass)
    for i in range(1, m + 1):
        for t in range(len(probability[i])):
            previous = t - spectrum[i - 1]
            if 0 <= previous < len(probability[i]):
                for step in mass.values():
                    if i - step >= 0:
                        probability[i][t] += share * probability[i - step][previous]
    return sum(probability[m][t - low]
               for t in range(threshold, max_score + 1))

Both dynamic programs cost $O(m^2)$ with a constant of twenty. On the sample spectral vector of length 14 over the two-letter alphabet, with threshold 1, the dictionary holds three peptides and has probability $0.375$; enumerating every peptide of mass 14 confirms that all three peptides of that mass score at least 1 and that $3 \cdot 2^{-3} = 0.375$.

The estimate can be checked against the decoy it was meant to replace. Taking the spectrum of the twelve-residue T. rex peptide and counting how many peptides of a shuffled chicken proteome reach each score gives an observed tail, and multiplying the dictionary probability at that threshold by the 27,302,528 positions gives a predicted one. At threshold 8 the counts are 1592 observed and 1572 predicted, at 12 they are 225 and 181, at 16 they are 25 and 16, and at 20 they are 5 and 1. The analytic E-value tracks the empirical decoy over four orders of magnitude, which is what makes it usable in place of a second search.

Modified Peptides

A cell alters its proteins after building them. Phosphate groups are attached to serine, threonine and tyrosine, sugars to asparagine, methyl groups to lysine, and in collagen many prolines carry an extra oxygen. Each such post-translational modification changes the mass of one residue by a fixed amount $\delta$, positive or negative. Molecules that have sat in the ground for a long time accumulate further changes of the same kind, chemical rather than enzymatic. A search that assumes the standard masses misses every peptide that carries one.

A modification of mass $\delta$ at position $k$ leaves every prefix mass before $k$ alone and shifts every prefix mass from $k$ onwards by $\delta$. In the peptide vector this moves a whole block of coordinates, inserting $\delta$ zeros if $\delta$ is positive and deleting $-\delta$ of them if it is negative. Modified variants of a peptide are therefore exactly the vectors reachable from its own by such block insertions and deletions, which is the operation the next algorithm searches over.

Spectral Alignment Problem. Given a peptide and a spectral vector, find the variant of the peptide with at most $k$ modifications that scores highest against the vector.

Place the peptide's amino acids on one axis and the masses $0$ to $m$ on the other, and add a third axis counting modifications used. A node $(i, j, t)$ means that the first $i$ amino acids have been placed, they occupy mass $j$, and $t$ modifications have been spent. Leaving amino acid $i$ unmodified moves from $(i-1, j - |a_i|, t)$ to $(i, j, t)$. Modifying it moves from $(i-1, j', t-1)$ for any $j' \lt j$, since a modification may change the mass by any amount. Both moves collect the amplitude $s_j$ of the coordinate they land on, and the answer is the heaviest path from $(0, 0, 0)$ to $(n, m, t)$ over all $t \leq k$.

Written directly the second move scans every earlier mass, giving $O(n m^2 k)$. It does not need to. The quantity being scanned is the maximum of $\textit{score}(i-1, j', t-1)$ over all $j' \lt j$, and that maximum over a growing prefix is maintained in constant time per cell by carrying it along as $j$ increases. The cost drops to $O(nmk)$, and for the 33-residue peptide and mass 2760 of the last section it is a tenth of a second. 

def spectral_alignment(peptide, spectrum, k, mass=MASS):
    weights = [mass[amino] for amino in peptide]
    n, m = len(peptide), len(spectrum)
    NEG = float('-inf')
    score = [[[NEG] * (k + 1) for _ in range(m + 1)] for _ in range(n + 1)]
    back = [[[None] * (k + 1) for _ in range(m + 1)] for _ in range(n + 1)]
    score[0][0][0] = 0
    reachable = [[[(NEG, None)] * (k + 1) for _ in range(m + 1)] for _ in range(n + 1)]

    def running_max(i):
        for t in range(k + 1):
            for j in range(m + 1):
                best = (score[i][j][t], j)
                if j and reachable[i][j - 1][t][0] >= best[0]:
                    best = reachable[i][j - 1][t]
                reachable[i][j][t] = best

    running_max(0)
    for i in range(1, n + 1):
        for j in range(1, m + 1):
            for t in range(k + 1):
                previous = j - weights[i - 1]
                if previous >= 0 and score[i - 1][previous][t] > NEG:
                    score[i][j][t] = score[i - 1][previous][t] + spectrum[j - 1]
                    back[i][j][t] = (i - 1, previous, t)
                if t > 0:
                    value, source = reachable[i - 1][j - 1][t - 1]
                    if value > NEG and value + spectrum[j - 1] > score[i][j][t]:
                        score[i][j][t] = value + spectrum[j - 1]
                        back[i][j][t] = (i - 1, source, t - 1)
        running_max(i)

    best_t = max(range(k + 1), key=lambda t: score[n][m][t])
    if score[n][m][best_t] == NEG:
        return None, None
    pieces = []
    i, j, t = n, m, best_t
    while i > 0:
        pi, pj, pt = back[i][j][t]
        shift = (j - pj) - weights[i - 1]
        pieces.append(peptide[i - 1] if shift == 0
                      else '%s(%+d)' % (peptide[i - 1], shift))
        i, j, t = pi, pj, pt
    return ''.join(reversed(pieces)), score[n][m][best_t]

On the sample peptide XXZ, of mass 13, against a spectral vector of length 14 with two modifications allowed, the algorithm returns XX(-1)Z(+2): the second X is lightened by one and the Z is made heavier by two, which brings the total to 14. Against exhaustive enumeration of every placement of every allowed number of modifications, on 200 random peptides and spectra, the score returned is the maximum every time.

Modification Search Problem. Given a spectrum and a proteome, find the modified peptide from the proteome, with at most $k$ modifications, of maximum score.

Running spectral alignment on every substring of a proteome is not affordable, and it is not necessary either. Modifications change the mass by a bounded amount in practice, so only substrings whose mass lies within that bound of $m$ can be candidates, and the prefix-mass array of the previous section finds them by binary search on each end of the window instead of on a single value. The search that follows is narrower still: the unmodified search has already named the protein, and only that protein is scanned.

Application: Was T. rex Just a Big Chicken?

The peptides reported from the Tyrannosaurus rex femur were deposited as two protein records, one for the collagen alpha-1(I) chain and one for alpha-2(I). Swiss-Prot has since deleted both, and the REST interface returns the reason as a field: the entries P0C2W2 and P0C2W4 are marked inactive with deleted reason "Deleted from Swiss-Prot". The archived records are still served by NCBI, and every sequence used below is fetched rather than typed. In the alpha-1 record, 570 positions long, every residue that was not sequenced is written X, and the five stretches that carry letters are the data. 

import re
import urllib.request

def fetch(url):
    with urllib.request.urlopen(url) as response:
        text = response.read().decode()
    return ''.join(line for line in text.splitlines() if not line.startswith('>'))


NCBI = ('https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi'
        '?db=protein&id=%s&rettype=fasta&retmode=text')
UNIPROT = 'https://rest.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/%s.fasta'

trex = {'a1': fetch(NCBI % 'P0C2W2.2'), 'a2': fetch(NCBI % 'P0C2W4.1')}
peptides = [(chain, match.group())
            for chain in ('a1', 'a2')

The five peptides are GATGAPGIAGAPGFPGARGAPGPQGPSGAPGPK at 2760 Da, GVQGPPGPQGPR at 1127, GSAGPPGATGFPGAAGR at 1408, GVVGLPGQR at 863, and GLPGESGAVGPAGPIGSR at 1559. All five begin with glycine and are 21 per cent proline, which is what collagen looks like: its helical region is a repeat of glycine followed by two other residues, and the chicken alpha-1 chain is 26.6 per cent glycine and 18.7 per cent proline, against 6.4 per cent and 5.8 per cent averaged over the whole chicken proteome. The raw spectra from 2007 are not public, so the spectra used below are simulated from these sequences with the instrument model of the section on amplitudes, at $\theta = 0.7$ and $\rho = 0.02$. What that tests is the search and the statistics, not the chemistry: the question being asked by computation is whether the published sequence beats every other candidate in a real proteome, not whether the fossil really produced it.

Where the Peptides Land

The chicken reference proteome from UniProt holds 41,396 sequences, of which three use letters outside the twenty standard amino acids and are dropped, leaving 41,393 proteins and 27,302,528 residues. Each simulated spectrum is searched against all of it with identify_peptide_filtered, in six seconds per spectrum, and against a decoy in which every protein has had its residues shuffled.

Four of the five peptides come back exactly. GVQGPPGPQGPR, GSAGPPGATGFPGAAGR and GVVGLPGQR are each the highest-scoring substring of the entire proteome for their spectrum, and each is found inside chicken collagen alpha-1(I); GLPGESGAVGPAGPIGSR is likewise the best in the proteome and is found in alpha-2(I). Their scores are 36, 39, 27 and 50, and the best score any shuffled decoy reaches for the same spectra is 22, 19, 23 and 24. Between about 208,000 and 255,000 substrings of the proteome have exactly the right mass in each case, so the right answer is being picked out of a quarter of a million candidates of the correct mass and 27 million starting positions.

Searching the peptides as strings, without any spectrum, sharpens the picture. GVVGLPGQR occurs in chicken, ostrich, human and mouse collagen alike, so finding it establishes only that the molecule is collagen. GSAGPPGATGFPGAAGR occurs in chicken, ostrich and human but not mouse. GVQGPPGPQGPR occurs in chicken and ostrich and in neither mammal, and GLPGESGAVGPAGPIGSR occurs exactly in chicken alone among the four. The peptides that carry information about which animal this was are the ones that are not universal.

What the Scores Are Worth

The E-value of each match is the probability of its spectral dictionary at its own score, times the 27,302,528 positions of the database. For GLPGESGAVGPAGPIGSR at score 50 the dictionary probability is $5.1 \times 10^{-19}$ and the E-value is $1.4 \times 10^{-11}$; for GSAGPPGATGFPGAAGR at 39 it is $1.4 \times 10^{-11}$ as well; for GVQGPPGPQGPR at 36 it is $6.5 \times 10^{-7}$. A database of this size would be expected to throw up a match that good by chance once in $10^{6}$ to $10^{11}$ experiments.

GVVGLPGQR is the exception. It is nine residues long, its score is 27, and its E-value is $3.6 \times 10^{-2}$: a database of this size produces a match that good by chance about once in thirty. That is not evidence of anything, and the reason is visible in the arithmetic rather than in the biology. A short peptide has few prefix masses, so it collects few amplitudes, so its score has a small range and the dictionary at any achievable threshold is large. The same calculation that certifies the long peptides refuses to certify the short one, and the published re-analyses of this dataset reached the same conclusion about this particular peptide by other means.

The Peptide That Needs a Modification

The 33-residue peptide is not found at all. No substring of the chicken proteome of mass exactly 2760 scores better than 20 against its spectrum, while the peptide itself scores 83. The corresponding stretch of chicken collagen alpha-1(I) is GATGAPGIAGAPGFPGARGPSGPQGPSGAPGPK, which differs at two positions and weighs 2776 Da, sixteen more, so it cannot be scored against a vector of length 2760 at all. This is the case the section on modifications was built for.

Running spectral_alignment on the chicken peptide against the T. rex spectrum returns nothing at $k = 0$, as it must. At $k = 1$ it finds a variant scoring 79, and at $k = 2$ one scoring 87, after which more modifications buy nothing. The two-modification answer scores higher than the true peptide's own 83, which is the price of the extra freedom: allowing arbitrary mass shifts lets a peptide chase noise peaks, so scores from a modification search are not comparable with scores from an unmodified one and need a null model of their own.

The Tree

Placing T. rex among modern animals uses only the 89 residues that were sequenced. For each of the five peptides and each species, the homologous stretch of that species' chain is located by a fitting alignment, which is Chapter 5's alignment with the mode set to 'fitting', so that the whole peptide is aligned into the long chain and the best window wins. Reading one residue of the chain per residue of the peptide and concatenating over the five peptides gives every species a string of 89 characters in register with the fossil. 

def homologous_window(peptide, chain, score):
    _, top, bottom = alignment(chain, peptide, score, 1, 'fitting')
    return ''.join(a for a, b in zip(top, bottom) if b != '-')

Counting the columns at which two of the strings differ gives a distance matrix, exactly as in Chapter 7. T. rex differs from chicken at one column out of 89, from ostrich at two, and from human and from mouse at eight each; chicken and ostrich differ from each other at one. Feeding the matrix to that chapter's neighbor_joining and rooting the result at the midpoint of its longest leaf-to-leaf path produces the tree below.

T. rex falls inside the birds, next to chicken, with ostrich outside the two of them and the mammals on the far side of the root. This is the answer to the chapter's question, and it is computed from fetched sequences by the chapter's own code rather than quoted. The limb leading to chicken has length zero, which is what neighbour-joining does when a leaf sits on the path between its neighbours: on these 89 columns the fossil sequence and the chicken sequence are one substitution apart, and chicken is not distinguishable from the common ancestor of chicken and the fossil.

What This Does and Does Not Establish

The computation shows that the published sequences are collagen, that four of them are identified unambiguously in a real proteome by their spectra, that three of those four carry E-values small enough to act on and the fourth does not, and that treating the 89 residues as a character matrix places the fossil among birds rather than mammals. Every one of those statements is about the sequences as data.

None of it establishes that the peptides came from a dinosaur. A tree built from 89 residues can distinguish birds from mammals, since the fossil differs from both mammals at eight of those columns and from both birds at no more than two, but it cannot resolve anything finer, and it cannot tell a 68-million-year-old bird-line collagen from a modern one: the fossil sequence is one substitution from chicken, so any recent bird would sit in the same place. That is the shape of the contamination argument that has been made against this dataset. A later study reported that bone from modern ostrich, handled in a laboratory, yields spectra matching the same peptides, and re-analyses of the original spectra assigned some of them to haemoglobin rather than collagen and argued that the shorter peptides did not clear the threshold. Two of the six sequences originally deposited have since been withdrawn, and Swiss-Prot's deletion of both records is the visible end of that argument.

The algorithms cannot settle it, and knowing why they cannot is the useful part. A spectral dictionary answers one question exactly: how often a database of this size would produce a score this good from nothing. It does not answer whether the sample was contaminated, because a contaminant is a real peptide and scores like one. The statistics of this chapter separate signal from noise, and contamination is signal from the wrong source.

That birds descend from theropod dinosaurs is not in doubt on other grounds, and the fossil record for it is far stronger than 89 residues of collagen. What this chapter's machinery contributes is the observation that if ancient protein can be recovered at all, the tools to read it, place it, and put a number on the placement already exist, and they run in seconds on a proteome of 27 million residues.

Every algorithm on this page was checked against the sample dataset published on the Rosalind problem page it corresponds to, from BA11A through BA11J, and each dynamic program was checked again against exhaustive enumeration on random inputs over the two-letter alphabet: 400 random spectra for peptide sequencing, 300 for identification, 300 for the size and probability of a spectral dictionary, 200 for spectral alignment, and 500 random peptides for the ideal spectrum and peptide vector round trips. The filtered proteome search was checked against the plain one on 60 further random cases.
