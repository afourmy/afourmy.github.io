Chapter 4: How Do We Sequence Antibiotics?

The first three chapters treated DNA as the only string. This chapter follows the information in a gene out to the protein it codes for, then reverses direction. The input becomes a physical measurement of a molecule, a list of masses, and the output is the sequence of the molecule that produced it.

The Central Dogma

A gene is a stretch of DNA that codes for a protein. Two steps separate one from the other, and together they are called the central dogma of molecular biology: DNA makes RNA, and RNA makes protein.

Transcription copies a gene into a strand of RNA. RNA is a string over four ribonucleotides, written A, C, G and U, and the transcript of a gene is its DNA sequence with every T replaced by U.

Translation turns the transcript into a protein. The RNA is cut into non-overlapping 3-mers called codons, and each codon is replaced by one of 20 amino acids. The result is an amino acid string, also called a peptide. The molecular machine that performs translation is the ribosome.

There are $4^3 = 64$ codons and 20 amino acids. Three codons, UAA, UAG and UGA, are stop codons: they code for no amino acid and end the translation. The remaining 61 are shared among the 20 amino acids, so most amino acids are coded by several codons. This assignment of codons to amino acids is the genetic code, and it is nearly identical in every organism.

Each amino acid has a one-letter symbol and a three-letter symbol. This chapter uses the one-letter symbols, so the peptide Val-Lys-Leu is written VKL.

Translating RNA

Protein Translation Problem. Translate an RNA string into an amino acid string.

Ordering the bases U, C, A, G and varying the third base fastest lists the 64 codons in a fixed order, so the genetic code is one string of 64 symbols, the codon $abc$ sitting at index $16i + 4j + k$ for the positions $i$, $j$, $k$ of its three bases. A stop codon is written *. 

BASES = 'UCAG'
AMINO = ('FFLLSSSSYY**CC*W'
         'LLLLPPPPHHQQRRRR'
         'IIIMTTTTNNKKSSRR'
         'VVVVAAAADDEEGGGG')

CODON = {a + b + c: AMINO[16 * i + 4 * j + k]
         for i, a in enumerate(BASES)
         for j, b in enumerate(BASES)
         for k, c in enumerate(BASES)}

def protein_translation(rna):
    protein = []
    for i in range(0, len(rna) - len(rna) % 3, 3):
        amino = CODON[rna[i:i + 3]]
        if amino == '*':
            break
        protein.append(amino)
    return ''.join(protein)

Translation reads the string once, so it costs $O(n)$ for an RNA string of length $n$. Applied to AUGGCCAUGGCGCCCAGAACUGAGAUCAAUAGUACCCGUAUUAACGGGUGA it returns MAMAPRTEINSTRING, the closing UGA ending it.

Finding the Gene Behind a Peptide

The reverse direction locates the gene that produced a known protein. A peptide of $k$ amino acids is coded by $3k$ nucleotides, and two things about that stretch are unknown: where it starts, and which of the two strands carries it.

Peptide Encoding Problem. Given a DNA string Text and an amino acid string Peptide, find every substring of Text encoding Peptide.

A substring encodes the peptide when translating it gives the peptide, or when translating its reverse complement does. Sliding a window of length $3k$ across the text covers every starting position, and so covers all three reading frames of both strands. reverse_complement comes from Chapter 1. 

def peptide_encoding(text, peptide):
    n = 3 * len(peptide)
    substrings = []
    for i in range(len(text) - n + 1):
        window = text[i:i + n]
        for strand in (window, reverse_complement(window)):
            if protein_translation(strand.replace('T', 'U')) == peptide:
                substrings.append(window)
                break
    return substrings

Each of the $n$ windows is translated at most twice at a cost of $O(k)$, so the search costs $O(nk)$. On the text ATGGCCATGGCCCCCAGAACTGAGATCAATAGTACCCGTATTAACGGGTGA and the peptide MA it returns ATGGCC, GGCCAT and ATGGCC. The middle one encodes MA on the reverse strand, since the reverse complement of GGCCAT is ATGGCC.

Tyrocidine B1 Is Not in the Genome

Tyrocidine B1 is an antibiotic produced by the bacterium Bacillus brevis. It is a peptide of ten amino acids, VKLFPWFNQY, and it is cyclic: its two ends are joined, so the molecule is a ring with no first and no last amino acid.

Running peptide_encoding on the B. brevis genome with this peptide returns nothing. No stretch of 30 nucleotides on either strand translates to VKLFPWFNQY, and widening the search to peptides one amino acid away from it returns nothing either.

Tyrocidine B1 is a non-ribosomal peptide, or NRP. It is assembled by an enzyme called an NRP synthetase, which adds one amino acid at a time in an order fixed by the structure of the enzyme itself. No RNA is read and no ribosome is involved, so no gene spells the peptide out.

Bacteria and fungi produce NRPs to kill competing organisms, and many NRPs are used as antibiotics and other drugs. They are frequently cyclic, and they contain amino acids outside the standard 20.

The sequence of an NRP therefore has to be read off the molecule rather than off the genome.

Mass Spectrometry

A mass spectrometer takes many copies of a molecule, breaks them into fragments, and weighs the fragments. Mass is measured in daltons (Da), one dalton being roughly the mass of a single proton or neutron. Rounding each amino acid mass to the nearest dalton makes every mass an integer.

The 20 amino acids have only 18 distinct integer masses. Leucine and isoleucine both weigh 113, and lysine and glutamine both weigh 128, so no measurement of mass separates either pair. Everything below works with those 18 masses, and a peptide is represented as a list of integers rather than a string of letters. 

MASS = {'G': 57, 'A': 71, 'S': 87, 'P': 97, 'V': 99, 'T': 101, 'C': 103,
        'I': 113, 'L': 113, 'N': 114, 'D': 115, 'K': 128, 'Q': 128,
        'E': 129, 'M': 131, 'H': 137, 'F': 147, 'R': 156, 'Y': 163, 'W': 186}

MASSES = sorted(set(MASS.values()))

def masses_of(peptide):
    return [MASS[amino] for amino in peptide]

The Theoretical Spectrum

A subpeptide of a cyclic peptide is a contiguous stretch of it, and the stretch may run past the join. A cyclic peptide of $n$ amino acids has $n$ subpeptides of each length from 1 to $n - 1$, one starting at each position, so $n(n - 1)$ in all.

The theoretical spectrum of a cyclic peptide, its cyclospectrum, is the sorted list of the masses of all its subpeptides, together with 0 and the mass of the whole peptide. It has $n(n - 1) + 2$ entries, and it repeats a mass whenever two different subpeptides weigh the same.

Generating Theoretical Spectrum Problem. Given a cyclic peptide, compute its cyclospectrum.

Summing each subpeptide from scratch costs $O(n)$ per subpeptide, $O(n^3)$ in total. Prefix masses remove the inner sum. With $\text{prefix}[i]$ the mass of the first $i$ amino acids, the subpeptide running from $i$ to $j$ weighs $\text{prefix}[j] - \text{prefix}[i]$, and a subpeptide that wraps past the join weighs the total mass minus the mass of the stretch it leaves out. 

def prefix_masses(peptide):
    prefix = [0]
    for mass in peptide:
        prefix.append(prefix[-1] + mass)
    return prefix

def linear_spectrum(peptide):
    prefix = prefix_masses(peptide)
    spectrum = [0]
    for i in range(len(peptide)):
        for j in range(i + 1, len(peptide) + 1):
            spectrum.append(prefix[j] - prefix[i])
    return sorted(spectrum)

def cyclic_spectrum(peptide):
    prefix = prefix_masses(peptide)
    total = prefix[-1]
    spectrum = [0]
    for i in range(len(peptide)):
        for j in range(i + 1, len(peptide) + 1):
            spectrum.append(prefix[j] - prefix[i])
            if i > 0 and j < len(peptide):
                spectrum.append(total - (prefix[j] - prefix[i]))
    return sorted(spectrum)

Both cost $O(n^2)$, which is the size of their output. A linear peptide has no subpeptides running past a join, so its spectrum holds $n(n + 1)/2 + 1$ masses rather than $n(n - 1) + 2$.

Sequencing a Cyclic Peptide

Cyclopeptide Sequencing Problem. Given an ideal spectrum, find every cyclic peptide whose cyclospectrum equals it.

The answer is never a single peptide. Rotating a cyclic peptide changes no subpeptide, and neither does reading it backwards, so all $2n$ rotations and reversals share one spectrum. With the collisions at 113 and 128, this is the limit of what a spectrum can determine.

The largest mass in a spectrum is the mass of the whole peptide, the parent mass. Brute force generates every peptide of that mass and keeps those whose cyclospectrum matches the spectrum. The number of candidates decides whether this is possible.

Counting Peptides with Given Mass Problem. Given an integer $m$, count the linear peptides of total mass $m$.

A peptide of mass $m$ ends in an amino acid of some mass $w$, and deleting that amino acid leaves a peptide of mass $m - w$. Summing over the 18 masses gives $C(m) = \sum_w C(m - w)$, with $C(0) = 1$ and $C(m) = 0$ for $m < 0$. 

def count_peptides(m):
    counts = [1] + [0] * m
    for mass in range(1, m + 1):
        counts[mass] = sum(counts[mass - w] for w in MASSES if w <= mass)
    return counts[m]

The table has $m$ entries and each costs 18 additions, so counting costs $O(m)$. count_peptides(1024) is 14712706211.

Tyrocidine B1 has parent mass 1322, and about $5.2 \times 10^{13}$ peptides share that mass. Generating them all is out of reach, so the candidates have to be built up and discarded before they are complete.

Branch and Bound

Candidates grow one amino acid at a time from the empty peptide, and a candidate is dropped as soon as it cannot be a stretch of the answer.

A partial candidate is a linear peptide, so the test uses its linear spectrum. A candidate is consistent with the experimental spectrum when every mass in its linear spectrum occurs in the experimental spectrum at least as many times. When a subpeptide of a candidate has a mass the spectrum does not contain, that subpeptide survives in every extension of the candidate, so no extension can be correct. 

from collections import Counter

def expand(peptides, masses=MASSES):
    return [peptide + [mass] for peptide in peptides for mass in masses]

def consistent(peptide, spectrum):
    available = Counter(spectrum)
    for mass in linear_spectrum(peptide):
        if available[mass] == 0:
            return False
        available[mass] -= 1
    return True

def cyclopeptide_sequencing(spectrum):
    parent, target = max(spectrum), sorted(spectrum)
    peptides, results = [[]], []
    while peptides:
        survivors = []
        for peptide in expand(peptides):
            if sum(peptide) == parent:
                if cyclic_spectrum(peptide) == target:
                    results.append(peptide)
            elif consistent(peptide, spectrum):
                survivors.append(peptide)
        peptides = survivors
    return results

On the spectrum 0 113 128 186 241 299 314 427 it returns 186-128-113, 186-113-128, 128-186-113, 128-113-186, 113-186-128 and 113-128-186, the six rotations and reversals of one cyclic peptide.

The pruning carries no worst-case guarantee, and the number of consistent candidates can still grow exponentially. On an ideal spectrum it is fast, because most extensions add an amino acid whose own mass is absent from the spectrum and die at the first test.

Spectra with Errors

A real spectrum is not ideal. It contains false masses, peaks matching no subpeptide, and it lacks missing masses, subpeptides that went undetected.

A single missing mass breaks cyclopeptide_sequencing. The true peptide then has a subpeptide whose mass is absent from the spectrum, so the true peptide fails the consistency test, is discarded, and the algorithm returns nothing at all.

Cyclopeptide Scoring Problem. Given a peptide and a spectrum, compute the number of masses the cyclospectrum of the peptide shares with the spectrum, counting multiplicity. 

def score(peptide, spectrum, cyclic=True):
    theoretical = cyclic_spectrum(peptide) if cyclic else linear_spectrum(peptide)
    available = Counter(spectrum)
    matched = 0
    for mass in theoretical:
        if available[mass] > 0:
            available[mass] -= 1
            matched += 1
    return matched

Scoring costs $O(n^2)$, the size of the theoretical spectrum. For NQEL against 0 99 113 114 128 227 257 299 355 356 370 371 484 the cyclic score is 11 and the linear score is 8.

The Leaderboard

Maximizing the score removes the grounds for discarding a candidate, because a low score no longer proves a candidate wrong. The bound becomes a fixed budget instead: keep the $N$ highest scoring candidates of each round and drop the others.

Candidates are linear, so they are ranked by linear score. Every peptide tied with the one at the cutoff is kept, so the leaderboard can hold more than $N$ peptides. 

def trim(leaderboard, spectrum, n):
    ranked = sorted(leaderboard, key=lambda p: score(p, spectrum, False), reverse=True)
    if len(ranked) <= n:
        return ranked
    cutoff = score(ranked[n - 1], spectrum, False)
    return [p for p in ranked if score(p, spectrum, False) >= cutoff]

def leaderboard_cyclopeptide_sequencing(spectrum, n, masses=MASSES):
    parent = max(spectrum)
    leaderboard, leader = [[]], []
    while leaderboard:
        survivors = []
        for peptide in expand(leaderboard, masses):
            if sum(peptide) == parent:
                if score(peptide, spectrum) > score(leader, spectrum):
                    leader = peptide
                survivors.append(peptide)
            elif sum(peptide) < parent:
                survivors.append(peptide)
        leaderboard = trim(survivors, spectrum, n)
    return leader

A round expands the leaderboard by a factor of 18 and scores every candidate at $O(n^2)$, so it costs $O(Nn^2)$, and a peptide of $n$ amino acids takes $n$ rounds, $O(Nn^3)$ in all. With $N = 10$ on 0 71 113 129 147 200 218 260 313 331 347 389 460 it returns 71-147-113-129, a rotation of the reversal of 113-147-71-129, and both score 13.

The result is a heuristic. A prefix of the highest scoring cyclic peptide is a linear peptide, and its linear score can be low enough to be trimmed in an early round, after which the algorithm can no longer reach that peptide. Raising $N$ makes this less likely at a cost linear in $N$.

Spectral Convolution

expand draws on the 18 masses of the standard amino acids, which assumes the peptide is built from them. NRPs contain amino acids outside the standard 20, whose masses are not in the table, and for those peptides the correct answer is not reachable at all.

The spectrum itself supplies the masses. Two subpeptides differing by one amino acid have masses differing by the mass of that amino acid, and a spectrum contains many such pairs, so the masses of the amino acids in the peptide occur often among the differences between masses.

Spectral Convolution Problem. Given a spectrum, return the positive differences between all pairs of its masses, in decreasing order of multiplicity. 

def spectral_convolution(spectrum):
    differences = Counter(a - b for a in spectrum for b in spectrum if a > b)
    return [d for d, k in differences.most_common() for _ in range(k)]

A spectrum of $s$ masses has a convolution of $O(s^2)$ elements, computed in $O(s^2)$. For 0 137 186 323 it returns 137 and 186 twice each, then 49 and 323 once each; elements of equal multiplicity may come out in any order.

Sequencing with the Convolution

The convolution supplies the alphabet the search expands over. Differences below 57 or above 200 fall outside the range of amino acid masses and are dropped, and the $M$ most frequent of the rest, ties included, become the masses the search may use. 

def convolution_cyclopeptide_sequencing(spectrum, m, n):
    counts = Counter(d for d in spectral_convolution(spectrum) if 57 <= d <= 200)
    ranked = counts.most_common()
    cutoff = ranked[min(m, len(ranked)) - 1][1]
    masses = [d for d, k in ranked if k >= cutoff]
    return leaderboard_cyclopeptide_sequencing(spectrum, n, masses)

With $M = 20$ and $N = 60$ on 57 57 71 99 129 137 170 186 194 208 228 265 285 299 307 323 356 364 394 422 493 it returns 99-71-137-57-57-72, scoring 21. The published answer 99-71-137-57-72-57 is a different cyclic peptide with the same score. Ties at the maximum are normal, since the score is the only thing separating candidates.

Sequencing Tyrocidine B1

Tyrocidine B1 is VKLFPWFNQY, so its masses are 99, 128, 113, 147, 97, 186, 147, 114, 128, 163 and its parent mass is 1322. Its cyclospectrum holds $10 \times 9 + 2 = 92$ masses, which is the entire input the algorithm is given.

convolution_cyclopeptide_sequencing on that spectrum with $M = 20$ and $N = 1000$ returns 147-97-186-147-114-128-163-99-128-113. It scores 92, matching the spectrum in full. Rotating it to begin at 99 gives 99-128-113-147-97-186-147-114-128-163, the masses of tyrocidine B1 in order.

The convolution found the alphabet as well as the peptide: the masses it selected from the spectrum are the ones the molecule is built from. Reading them back through the mass table gives VKLFPWFNQY, with 113 standing for either L or I and each 128 for either K or Q.

A search of the B. brevis genome finds no gene for this peptide, and 92 numbers produced by weighing its fragments determine it up to rotation, reversal, and the two pairs of amino acids that share a mass.
