Chapter 10: Why Have Biologists Still Not Developed an HIV Vaccine?

Every chapter so far has compared sequences to each other. This one builds a model of what a family of sequences looks like, and then asks of a new sequence how well the model explains it. The model is a machine that emits symbols while moving between states that cannot be seen, and the algorithms in this chapter recover what the machine was doing from what it produced.

HIV and the Limits of Alignment

HIV is a retrovirus that infects the immune cells meant to clear it. It carries only nine genes, and the one that matters here is env, which codes for the envelope protein on the surface of the virus. That protein is what an antibody would have to recognise, and it is the hardest possible target. The virus copies its genome with an error-prone enzyme and produces new generations in days, so env accumulates changes at one to two percent per nucleotide per year, faster than an immune response can be raised against any one version of it. The protein is also coated in sugars added by the host cell, a covering that hides much of the surface an antibody would otherwise bind.

Different versions of env behave differently in a patient. Some strains make infected cells fuse into large clumps in culture and are called syncytium-inducing, or SI; others do not and are called non-syncytium-inducing, NSI. The distinction matters because it tracks which cells the virus can enter and how fast the disease progresses, and the difference is carried in a short stretch of env called the V3 loop, about 35 amino acids long. Given the V3 loop of a new isolate, the question is which kind it is.

Chapter 5 compared two sequences and returned a score. That is the wrong shape of answer here. A score says how similar two sequences are as wholes, and what separates SI from NSI is which positions of the loop are fixed across the family and which are free, and what each position prefers. Chapter 5's multiple alignment does produce columns, but it aligns exactly three sequences and its cost grows exponentially with the number of them, so it cannot look at a family of fifty. A single alignment also has no way to say that a position usually holds arginine, sometimes lysine, and never proline. That statement is a probability distribution per column, and a model made of those distributions is what this chapter builds.

The Crooked Dealer

Strip the biology away and the problem has a simpler form. A dealer flips a coin repeatedly and announces the outcomes. Most of the time the coin is fair, but sometimes the dealer switches to a biased coin that comes up heads three times in four, and later switches back. The sequence of outcomes is seen; the sequence of coins is not.

Casino Problem. Given a string $x = x_1 \dots x_n$ of outcomes, find the string $\pi = \pi_1 \dots \pi_n$ saying which coin produced each outcome.

The obvious attack is a sliding window: at each position, count the heads in a window around it and call the window biased when the share is closer to $0.75$ than to $0.5$. The trouble is the width. A short window is dominated by chance, since a fair coin easily gives four heads in six flips, so it reports biased stretches that are not there. A long window averages the noise away but also averages the biased stretch together with the fair flips on either side of it, so a short stretch is flattened until it disappears and the ends of a long one are placed wherever the window happens to cross the threshold.

The width is doing work that the data should be doing. Choosing it is a statement about how long the dealer keeps a coin before switching, and that is exactly the kind of statement a model can hold explicitly and a measurement cannot.

CG-Islands

The same problem appears in a genome. Reading a chromosome as a string over $\{$A, C, G, T$\}$, the dinucleotide CG is far rarer than the letter frequencies predict. The cause is chemical. A C followed by a G is the site at which the cell attaches a methyl group, a small chemical tag used to switch genes off, and a methylated C changes into a T comparatively easily, so over evolutionary time CG pairs have been eroded into TG pairs. In the real window of human chromosome X used later in this chapter, CG occurs at $2.5689$ percent of positions where the C and G counts alone predict $5.9536$ percent, which is a rate of $0.431$ of what composition would give.

The erosion is not uniform. Around the start of many genes the methylation machinery is kept away, the CG pairs there are not eroded, and the result is a short stretch with much more CG than its surroundings. These are CG-islands, and finding them locates gene starts. The naive method is again a sliding window over CG density, and it fails for the same reason with the same freedom in its width.

Hidden Markov Models

A hidden Markov model is four things. An alphabet of symbols that can be emitted. A set of states. A transition matrix whose entry $\textit{transition}(k, l)$ is the probability that a chain in state $k$ moves to state $l$ next. An emission matrix whose entry $\textit{emission}(k, b)$ is the probability that state $k$ emits symbol $b$. The chain starts in a state chosen uniformly, and at every step it emits one symbol and then moves.

A run of the machine produces two strings of the same length: the hidden path $\pi$ of states it passed through, and the string $x$ of symbols it emitted. Only $x$ is seen. The probability of the path alone is the product of the transitions along it, and the probability of the emissions given the path is the product of the emissions, so the two together give $\Pr(x, \pi) = \Pr(\pi) \Pr(x \mid \pi)$. 

def path_probability(path, states, transition):
    probability = 1 / len(states)
    for previous, current in zip(path, path[1:]):
        probability *= transition[previous, current]
    return probability


def outcome_given_path(x, path, emission):
    probability = 1
    for symbol, state in zip(x, path):
        probability *= emission[state, symbol]
    return probability

An HMM is written here as four plain Python objects: a list of state names, a list of alphabet symbols, and two dictionaries keyed by pairs. Nothing else in the chapter needs more than that. The casino problem now has a precise statement: among all $|\textit{States}|^n$ hidden paths, find the one that makes $\Pr(x, \pi)$ largest.

Decoding

Decoding Problem. Given a string $x$ emitted by an HMM, find a hidden path $\pi$ maximising $\Pr(x, \pi)$ over all hidden paths.

Lay the states out as rows and the positions of $x$ as columns, and draw an edge from every state in one column to every state in the next. This is the Viterbi graph. Give the edge from $(k, i-1)$ to $(l, i)$ the weight $\textit{transition}(k, l) \cdot \textit{emission}(l, x_i)$, which is exactly the factor that a path contributes by making that move and emitting that symbol.

Theorem. The probability $\Pr(x, \pi)$ of a hidden path is the product of the weights of the edges along the corresponding path through the Viterbi graph, so a most likely hidden path is a longest path in a directed acyclic graph.

The proof is the definition rearranged. $\Pr(x, \pi) = \Pr(\pi)\Pr(x \mid \pi)$ expands to $\frac{1}{|\textit{States}|}\prod_{i=2}^{n} \textit{transition}(\pi_{i-1}, \pi_i) \cdot \prod_{i=1}^{n} \textit{emission}(\pi_i, x_i)$. Pairing each transition factor with the emission factor of the position it moves into groups the product into exactly the edge weights defined above, with the first emission and the uniform start left over as the weight of entering the first column. Taking logarithms turns the product into a sum, and because the logarithm is increasing, the path maximising the product is the path maximising the sum. The graph has an edge only from column $i-1$ to column $i$, so it is acyclic, and the longest path problem in a weighted directed acyclic graph is the one Chapter 5 solved.

That reduction also gives the size of the problem. The graph has $|\textit{States}|$ nodes per column and $n$ columns, and a full set of edges between consecutive columns, so it has $|\textit{States}|^2 (n-1)$ edges between columns plus $2|\textit{States}|$ joining the source and sink, and the longest path costs one step per edge.

Theorem. If a longest path into $(l, i)$ arrives along the edge from $(k, i-1)$, then its initial part is a longest path into $(k, i-1)$.

The proof is an exchange. Suppose some path into $(k, i-1)$ were heavier than the initial part of the path in question. Appending the same final edge to it would give a path into $(l, i)$ heavier than the original, since both differ only in that final factor, contradicting that the original was longest. So the best score at a node depends only on the best scores one column to its left, and one pass from left to right computes them all. 

def safe_log(p):
    return log(p) if p > 0 else -inf


def viterbi(x, states, transition, emission):
    """The most likely hidden path: the longest path in the Viterbi graph."""
    best = {k: safe_log(emission[k, x[0]] / len(states)) for k in states}
    back = []
    for symbol in x[1:]:
        step = {l: max(states, key=lambda k: best[k] + safe_log(transition[k, l]))
                for l in states}
        best = {l: best[step[l]] + safe_log(transition[step[l], l])
                + safe_log(emission[l, symbol]) for l in states}
        back.append(step)
    state = max(states, key=best.__getitem__)
    path = [state]
    for step in reversed(back):
        state = step[state]
        path.append(state)
    return list(reversed(path))

The scores are kept as logarithms throughout. A path of a few hundred steps has a probability below the smallest positive number a machine can hold, so multiplying the weights directly would give zero for every path and the comparison would be meaningless. Adding logarithms costs nothing extra and the answer is the same path.

Application: CG-Islands in Chromosome X

The window used here is positions $226{,}352$ to $1{,}000{,}000$ of human chromosome X in assembly GRCh38, fetched from NCBI as record NC_000023.11. It is $773{,}649$ nucleotides long and is the longest stretch of the first megabase of that chromosome carrying no ambiguous nucleotide. Its GC content is $48.80$ percent, and its CG rate of $0.431$ of what composition predicts is the depletion described earlier.

A model over the four nucleotides cannot express what a CG-island is, because a state's emission distribution would only say how often each single letter appears, which is GC content rather than CG frequency. The sequence is therefore read as a string over the sixteen dinucleotides. Consecutive pairs are taken end to end rather than overlapping, because an overlapping pair shares a nucleotide with its neighbour and an HMM assumes each emission depends on nothing but its own state. That gives $386{,}824$ observations over an alphabet of sixteen, and two states, one for island and one for everything else.

Viterbi needs parameters before it can run, and the simplest way to get them is to count on a stretch where the answer is already known.

HMM Parameter Estimation Problem. Given a string $x$ emitted by an HMM with unknown matrices, together with the hidden path $\pi$ that produced it, find the transition and emission matrices that maximise $\Pr(x, \pi)$.

The answer is counting. The likelihood is a product of one factor per step, each factor an entry of one of the two matrices, so it factorises into independent problems, one per row. Maximising a row subject to its entries summing to one puts each entry at the share of the transitions out of that state, or the emissions from it, that took that option. A state the path never leaves gives no evidence at all, and its row is left flat. 

def normalise(counts, rows, columns, allowed=None):
    """Turn a table of counts into rows that sum to one. A row with no counts
    in it stays zero, and entries the row is not allowed to use stay zero."""
    table = {}
    for row in rows:
        options = columns if allowed is None else allowed(row)
        total = sum(counts[row, column] for column in options)
        for column in columns:
            table[row, column] = (counts[row, column] / total
                                  if total and column in options else 0.0)
    return table


def flatten_empty_rows(counts, rows, columns):
    """A state the path never uses leaves no evidence behind, so its row is
    made flat rather than left at zero."""
    for row in rows:
        if not any(counts[row, column] for column in columns):
            for column in columns:
                counts[row, column] = 1


def estimate_parameters(x, path, states, alphabet):
    transition = {(k, l): 0.0 for k in states for l in states}
    emission = {(k, symbol): 0.0 for k in states for symbol in alphabet}
    for previous, current in zip(path, path[1:]):
        transition[previous, current] += 1
    for state, symbol in zip(path, x):
        emission[state, symbol] += 1
    flatten_empty_rows(transition, states, states)
    flatten_empty_rows(emission, states, alphabet)
    return (normalise(transition, states, states),
            normalise(emission, states, alphabet))

The labels come from the published compositional definition of a CG-island, applied to a $300$ nucleotide window around each position: GC content above $55$ percent and observed CG at least $0.65$ of what the C and G counts predict. That marks $6.49$ percent of the sequence. Counting on the first half of the window gives a model in which an island emits CG with probability $0.0774$ against $0.0258$ outside, an enrichment of $3.0$ times, and in which both states are far more likely to persist than to switch.

Viterbi then decodes the whole window in $0.68$ seconds. Runs of at least $200$ nucleotides marked island number $80$, covering $10.99$ percent of the sequence with a median length of $724$ nucleotides. Their mean GC content is $66.5$ percent against $48.8$ percent for the window as a whole, and their mean observed CG rate is $0.63$ of what composition predicts, against $0.431$ for the window.

$44$ of the $80$ stretches pass the published test outright, needing GC above $50$ percent and a CG rate of at least $0.6$ along their whole length. The rest are GC-rich without being CG-rich, which is the limit of a model with two states: one state has to describe everything that is not ordinary sequence, and a region that is unusual in its letter composition alone is enough to reach it.

Nothing in that calculation used a gene. The window carries $8$ annotated genes, and $5$ of them start within $500$ nucleotides of a decoded island, against $19.5$ percent for positions drawn at random from the same window, which would happen by chance with probability $0.0093$. The islands are sitting where genes start, which is what the biology says they should do and what nothing in the estimation told them to do.

The width that the sliding window could not choose has become a transition probability that the counting supplied. Applying the same compositional test through a sliding window instead, and varying only its width, gives $501$ regions at a width of $100$, $125$ at $300$, $30$ at $1000$ and $8$ at $3000$: the answer moves by a factor of $63$ with a parameter that nothing in the data fixes.

The Likelihood of an Outcome

Viterbi returns one path. A different question is how well the model explains the string at all, summing over every path it could have taken.

Outcome Likelihood Problem. Given a string $x$ and an HMM, find $\Pr(x)$, the probability that the model emits $x$.

Summing $|\textit{States}|^n$ terms directly is impossible, and it is also unnecessary. The same Viterbi graph carries the answer: if $\textit{forward}(k, i)$ is the total weight of all paths from the source to node $(k, i)$, then every path into $(l, i)$ passes through exactly one node of column $i-1$, so the totals obey the same recurrence as before with the maximum replaced by a sum, and $\Pr(x)$ is the total over the last column. 

def forward(x, states, transition, emission):
    """Forward probabilities, each column rescaled to sum to one. The scale
    factors are returned as well, and their product is Pr(x)."""
    column = {k: emission[k, x[0]] / len(states) for k in states}
    total = sum(column.values())
    columns, scales = [{k: v / total for k, v in column.items()}], [total]
    for symbol in x[1:]:
        column = {l: emission[l, symbol] * sum(columns[-1][k] * transition[k, l]
                                               for k in states) for l in states}
        total = sum(column.values())
        columns.append({k: v / total for k, v in column.items()})
        scales.append(total)
    return columns, scales


def outcome_probability(x, states, transition, emission):
    _, scales = forward(x, states, transition, emission)
    probability = 1.0
    for scale in scales:
        probability *= scale
    return probability


def log_likelihood(x, states, transition, emission):
    _, scales = forward(x, states, transition, emission)
    return sum(safe_log(scale) for scale in scales)

Logarithms cannot be used here, because the recurrence adds probabilities rather than multiplying them and the logarithm of a sum is not a sum of logarithms. Each column is divided by its own total instead, which keeps the numbers near one, and the totals are kept so that their product is $\Pr(x)$ and the sum of their logarithms is $\log \Pr(x)$. The rescaling cancels out of every ratio taken later, which is why the same columns can be reused without correction.

The Most Likely Outcome

Turning the question around asks not which path explains a string but which string the model is most likely to produce. Two different questions hide behind that. Maximising $\Pr(x)$, summed over all paths, does not decompose along the graph, because the sum over paths of one prefix cannot be combined with the sum over paths of a suffix without knowing which state joins them. Maximising $\Pr(x, \pi)$ over strings and paths together does decompose, and it is the question solved here.

Most Likely Outcome Problem. Given an HMM and a length $m$, find a string $x$ of length $m$ and a path $\pi$ maximising $\Pr(x, \pi)$.

The Viterbi graph gains a third dimension, one axis for the symbol emitted at each step, so a node becomes a triple of state, position and symbol. It collapses again immediately: the symbol emitted at a step appears in exactly one factor of the product, $\textit{emission}(l, b)$, and that factor is independent of everything else in the path. The best symbol for a state can therefore be chosen once, before any path is considered, and the search returns to the ordinary Viterbi graph with each state's emission factor fixed at its largest value. 

def most_likely_outcome(length, states, transition, emission, alphabet):
    """The string and path maximising Pr(x, pi) together. At every step the
    symbol is chosen after the state, so the choice is one lookup per state."""
    pick = {k: max(alphabet, key=lambda s: emission[k, s]) for k in states}
    best = {k: safe_log(emission[k, pick[k]] / len(states)) for k in states}
    back = []
    for _ in range(length - 1):
        step = {l: max(states, key=lambda k: best[k] + safe_log(transition[k, l]))
                for l in states}
        best = {l: best[step[l]] + safe_log(transition[step[l], l])
                + safe_log(emission[l, pick[l]]) for l in states}
        back.append(step)
    state = max(states, key=best.__getitem__)
    path = [state]
    for step in reversed(back):
        state = step[state]
        path.append(state)
    path.reverse()
    return ''.join(pick[state] for state in path), path

Profile HMMs

Everything so far has taken the model as given. For a family of sequences the model has to be built from the family itself, and the structure that does it is a profile HMM.

The input is a multiple alignment of sequences already known to belong together, called a seed alignment. Some of its columns are filled in nearly every sequence and describe positions the family shares; others are mostly gaps and describe material that only a few members carry. A threshold $\theta$ separates them: a column whose share of gaps reaches $\theta$ is dropped, and the rest are kept.

The model is then built in three stages. One match state per kept column, chained in order, each emitting with the distribution of residues in its column. One insertion state between consecutive match states and one at each end, which absorb the residues of the dropped columns and can be visited repeatedly, so a sequence carrying extra material can still be explained. One deletion state under each match state, which emits nothing at all and exists so that a sequence missing a column the family has can skip it, and a run of them can skip several.

A deletion state is silent. That is not a detail: it means a path can move between states without consuming a symbol, so the graph is no longer one column per position and the decoding has to handle moves that stay in the same column.

The probabilities come from the seed alignment by counting, exactly as in the estimation problem earlier, with the path of each sequence through the model read off the alignment: a residue in a kept column is a match, a gap in a kept column is a deletion, and a residue in a dropped column is an insertion. 

def profile_states(n):
    states = ['S', 'I0']
    for i in range(1, n + 1):
        states += ['M%d' % i, 'D%d' % i, 'I%d' % i]
    return states + ['E']


def level(state, n):
    return 0 if state == 'S' else n + 1 if state == 'E' else int(state[1:])


def successors(state, n):
    """The three states a profile HMM can move to, or two at the last column."""
    i = level(state, n)
    if state == 'E':
        return []
    if i < n:
        return ['I%d' % i, 'M%d' % (i + 1), 'D%d' % (i + 1)]
    return ['I%d' % i, 'E']


def profile_path(row, kept):
    """The states one row of the alignment walks through, with what it emits."""
    steps, matched = [('S', '')], 0
    for j, symbol in enumerate(row):
        if j in kept:
            matched += 1
            steps.append(('M%d' % matched, symbol) if symbol != '-'
                         else ('D%d' % matched, ''))
        elif symbol != '-':
            steps.append(('I%d' % matched, symbol))
    return steps + [('E', '')]

Counting alone leaves a column that happens to hold one residue in every sequence assigning probability zero to all the others, so a single unexpected residue would make an entire sequence impossible rather than merely unusual. A pseudocount $\sigma$ fixes that by being added to every entry the model is allowed to use before the row is renormalised. It is added to the probability rather than to the count, so how much a column is softened does not depend on how many sequences the alignment happens to hold. 

def profile_hmm(alignment, alphabet, theta, sigma=0.0):
    gaps = [column.count('-') / len(column) for column in zip(*alignment)]
    kept = {j for j, share in enumerate(gaps) if share < theta}
    n = len(kept)
    states = profile_states(n)
    transition = {(k, l): 0.0 for k in states for l in states}
    emission = {(k, symbol): 0.0 for k in states for symbol in alphabet}
    for row in alignment:
        steps = profile_path(row, kept)
        for (k, _), (l, _) in zip(steps, steps[1:]):
            transition[k, l] += 1
        for state, symbol in steps:
            if symbol:
                emission[state, symbol] += 1

    def emits(state):
        return alphabet if state[0] in 'MI' else []

    def leaves(state):
        return successors(state, n)

    transition = normalise(transition, states, states, leaves)
    emission = normalise(emission, states, alphabet, emits)
    if sigma:
        for state in states:
            for l in leaves(state):
                transition[state, l] += sigma
            for symbol in emits(state):
                emission[state, symbol] += sigma
        transition = normalise(transition, states, states, leaves)
        emission = normalise(emission, states, alphabet, emits)
    return states, transition, emission

Aligning a new sequence to the family is now decoding: find the most likely path through the profile HMM that emits it. The silent states are what makes this more than a copy of Viterbi. Emitting states are reached from the column to the left, as before, but a deletion state is reached within the same column, and a run of deletions chains through several of them before any symbol is consumed. Taking the deletion states in order of their column lets each one see the finished value of the one before it. 

def profile_alignment(text, states, transition, emission):
    """Viterbi through a profile HMM, whose deletion states emit nothing."""
    n = max(level(state, 0) for state in states if state[0] == 'M')
    silent = [state for state in states if state[0] == 'D'] + ['E']
    parent = {}

    def enter_silent(score, column):
        """Deletion states emit nothing, so they are reached within a column.
        Taking them in order of level lets a run of deletions chain."""
        for l in silent:
            for k in states:
                weight = score[k] + safe_log(transition[k, l])
                if weight > score[l]:
                    score[l], parent[l, column] = weight, k

    score = {state: -inf for state in states}
    score['S'] = 0.0
    enter_silent(score, 0)
    columns = [score]
    for i, symbol in enumerate(text, start=1):
        score = {state: -inf for state in states}
        for l in states:
            if l[0] not in 'MI':
                continue
            for k in states:
                weight = columns[-1][k] + safe_log(transition[k, l]) \
                    + safe_log(emission[l, symbol])
                if weight > score[l]:
                    score[l], parent[l, i] = weight, k
        enter_silent(score, i)
        columns.append(score)

    path, state, i = [], 'E', len(text)
    while state != 'S':
        if state != 'E':
            path.append(state)
        previous = parent[state, i]
        if state[0] in 'MI':
            i -= 1
        state = previous
    return list(reversed(path))

The score of that path is what turns the model into a classifier. On its own the number means little, because a longer sequence has a smaller probability whatever it is. Comparing it against a background model that emits residues independently at their usual frequencies removes the length and leaves how much better the family explains the sequence than chance does. 

def path_score(path, text, transition, emission):
    """The log probability of one path through a profile HMM, and of what it emits."""
    total, state, i = 0.0, 'S', 0
    for l in path:
        total += safe_log(transition[state, l])
        if l[0] in 'MI':
            total += safe_log(emission[l, text[i]])
            i += 1
        state = l
    return total + safe_log(transition[state, 'E'])

Learning Without a Path

Estimating parameters from a known path was easy. The situation that matters is the one where the path is not known, which is every situation where the model is being fitted to raw data.

HMM Parameter Learning Problem. Given a string $x$ emitted by an HMM with unknown matrices, find the matrices and the hidden path maximising $\Pr(x, \pi)$.

The two unknowns depend on each other, and each is easy given the other. That suggests alternating: decode with the current parameters, re-estimate from the decoded path, repeat. 

def viterbi_learning(x, states, alphabet, transition, emission, rounds):
    for _ in range(rounds):
        path = viterbi(x, states, transition, emission)
        transition, emission = estimate_parameters(x, path, states, alphabet)
    return transition, emission

What this throws away is everything the model knew except its single best guess. A position that the current parameters place in one state with probability $0.51$ contributes to the counts exactly as much as one placed there with probability $0.999$, and the doubt is gone by the next round. Recovering it means asking, for each position separately, how much of the total probability passes through each state.

Soft Decoding Problem. Given a string $x$ and an HMM, find $\Pr(\pi_i = k \mid x)$ for every position $i$ and state $k$.

Theorem. $\Pr(\pi_i = k \mid x) = \textit{forward}(k, i) \cdot \textit{backward}(k, i) / \Pr(x)$, where $\textit{backward}(k, i)$ is the total weight of paths from $(k, i)$ to the end.

The proof is a bijection. Any path through $(k, i)$ splits at that node into a prefix ending there and a suffix starting there, its weight is the product of the two parts, and every choice of a prefix with a suffix is again a path through that node. Summing the products over all pairs therefore factorises into the sum over prefixes times the sum over suffixes, which are the forward and backward values. Dividing by $\Pr(x)$, the total weight of all paths, turns the weight into a conditional probability. 

def backward(x, states, transition, emission):
    """Backward probabilities, rescaled column by column in the same way."""
    columns = [{k: 1.0 for k in states}]
    for symbol in reversed(x[1:]):
        ahead = columns[0]
        column = {k: sum(transition[k, l] * emission[l, symbol] * ahead[l]
                         for l in states) for k in states}
        total = sum(column.values())
        columns.insert(0, {k: v / total for k, v in column.items()})
    return columns


def soft_decoding(x, states, transition, emission):
    ahead, _ = forward(x, states, transition, emission)
    behind = backward(x, states, transition, emission)
    profile = []
    for i in range(len(x)):
        weight = {k: ahead[i][k] * behind[i][k] for k in states}
        total = sum(weight.values())
        profile.append({k: weight[k] / total for k in states})
    return profile

Baum-Welch learning is Viterbi learning with those shares in place of the hard decision. Instead of counting one visit to the state the best path chose, it counts a fraction of a visit to every state, weighted by how much probability passes through it, and it does the same for the edges. The counts are no longer integers, and nothing else changes. 

def baum_welch(x, states, alphabet, transition, emission, rounds):
    for _ in range(rounds):
        ahead, _ = forward(x, states, transition, emission)
        behind = backward(x, states, transition, emission)
        node = soft_decoding(x, states, transition, emission)
        edge = {(k, l): 0.0 for k in states for l in states}
        for i in range(len(x) - 1):
            weight = {(k, l): ahead[i][k] * transition[k, l]
                      * emission[l, x[i + 1]] * behind[i + 1][l]
                      for k in states for l in states}
            total = sum(weight.values())
            for pair in weight:
                edge[pair] += weight[pair] / total
        seen = {(k, symbol): sum(node[i][k] for i in range(len(x)) if x[i] == symbol)
                for k in states for symbol in alphabet}
        transition = normalise(edge, states, states)
        emission = normalise(seen, states, alphabet)
    return transition, emission

This is expectation maximization, the procedure Chapter 8 used for soft clustering, with hidden states in place of hidden cluster memberships. Each round computes the expected counts under the current parameters and then maximises with those counts held fixed, and the standard argument for expectation maximization shows that a round can never lower $\Pr(x)$. The argument turns on the fact that the expected log likelihood is a lower bound on the true one which touches it at the current parameters, so improving the bound cannot worsen what it bounds. The full proof is not reproduced here.

Rising likelihood is not the same as a better answer, and the chromosome X sequence shows the difference plainly. Started from the counted parameters of the earlier section and run for eight rounds on the same $386{,}824$ observations, Baum-Welch raises the log likelihood at every round, from $-1{,}059{,}094$ to $-1{,}045{,}258$. Over the same eight rounds the share of the window decoded as island climbs from $11.43$ percent to $63.99$ percent. The model is fitting better and describing something else: broad variation in GC content across hundreds of thousands of nucleotides, which is real structure in a chromosome and is not what a CG-island is. Maximum likelihood optimises the fit, not the interpretation, and when the two part company it is the fit that wins.

Application: Classifying an HIV Sequence

The sequences are real. Fetching the $120$ GenBank records for HIV-1 whose titles name the V3 loop, and keeping the translated peptides of the canonical length written over the twenty standard amino acids, gives $72$ loops of between $34$ and $36$ residues. Two thirds of them, $48$ sequences, build the model, and the remaining $24$ are held back.

A profile HMM needs a seed alignment and Chapter 5 stopped at three sequences, so the alignment is built progressively: each sequence in turn is aligned against the consensus of what has been aligned so far, using Chapter 5's alignment as the engine, and every gap the consensus picks up is opened in all the rows already placed. The substitution scores are BLOSUM62, read from the file published by NCBI rather than typed in. The result is $37$ columns wide and $5.6$ percent gaps.

With $\theta = 0.35$ and a pseudocount of $0.01$ the model has $35$ match states and $108$ states in all. Reading the most probable residue off each match state in order gives CTRPNNNTRKSVRIGPGQTFYATGDIIGDIRQAHC. That string was never supplied to the program. It is the known consensus of the V3 loop, and its landmarks are the model's most confident columns: the cysteine at the first match state and the cysteine at the last, which in the folded protein join to close the loop, and the glycine-proline-glycine at match states $15$ to $17$, the tip of the loop.

The test is whether the model recognises V3 loops it has not seen. Each held-out sequence is aligned to the profile and scored against a background model built from the amino acid composition of HIV-1 Gag, a real protein of the same virus from a different family. Three controls are scored the same way: the held-out loops with their residues shuffled, which have the same composition and the same length but no order; $35$ residue windows of Gag; and $35$ residue windows of the human KAT6B protein of Chapter 9.

The held-out V3 loops score between $45.6$ and $90.9$. All $104$ controls score below $-19.7$. The two groups do not overlap, and any threshold in the gap between them classifies every one of the $24$ real loops correctly with no false positives. The shuffled control is the one that matters: it holds composition and length fixed, so the $65.3$ units separating it from the real loops are entirely the order of the residues, which is what the profile encodes and a single alignment score cannot.

What this establishes is that a profile HMM built by the chapter's own code from real sequences recognises further real members of the same family and rejects sequences that merely look like them in composition. It does not establish the SI and NSI classification the chapter opened with. That needs sequences labelled by what the virus did in culture, and those labels are not carried by the public sequence records, so the phenotype question is quoted here rather than computed: the distinction is known to turn on the charge of a few positions of this same loop. The machinery that would answer it is what has just been built. What is missing is the labels.
