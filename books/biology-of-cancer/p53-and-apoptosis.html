Chapter 9: p53 and Apoptosis

A Protein Found Bound to a Viral Oncoprotein

In 1979, researchers used antibodies from animals with tumors caused by SV40 to study the proteins of cells transformed by SV40. The antibodies caught the viral large T antigen and, bound to it, a protein of the cell that weighs about 53 to 54 kilodaltons. This protein became known as p53. Its gene was first cloned from tumor cells. This version of the gene helped ras and myc to transform cells, so p53 was first thought to be an oncogene.

A copy of the gene cloned from normal cells did the opposite: it stopped transformation. The two versions differed by a single point mutation, and the version from tumor cells was a mutant. The p53 gene is in fact a tumor suppressor gene. Of all the genes examined in human cancers, it is the one mutated most often, and it is mutated in more than 90% of some types of cancer.

Mice that lack both copies of p53 develop normally, so p53 is not needed to control normal growth. But these mice die of cancer, mostly lymphomas, and all of them are dead by about 250 days of age. Mice with one copy of p53 begin to develop tumors at about that age. Normal mice show almost no deaths until almost 500 days of age. In humans, inherited mutant copies of p53 cause Li-Fraumeni syndrome. In families with this syndrome, many people develop cancers of many kinds, often early in life. Mutant p53 copies are passed on in about 70% of these families.

One Mutant Copy Disables Most p53

Most mutations of APC shorten the protein. Most p53 mutations, in contrast, replace one amino acid with another, and 95.1% of them fall in the part of the protein that binds DNA. The p53 protein works as a tetramer, a group of four subunits. One defective subunit can disable the whole tetramer.

Consider a cell with one mutant copy and one normal copy of p53, both making the same amount of protein. Each tetramer takes its four subunits at random, so only one tetramer in 16 is made entirely of normal subunits. A mutant allele that works in this way, by disabling the protein made by the normal allele, is called dominant-negative. Tumors usually lose the remaining normal copy later, by LOH.

Mdm2 Keeps p53 Low Until the Cell Is Damaged

Normal cells make p53 all the time, but they destroy it almost at once. When the making of new proteins is blocked, p53 disappears with a half-life of only 20 minutes. The cell can therefore raise its level of p53 quickly, simply by stopping its destruction. It does not need to make more p53 RNA.

X-rays, ultraviolet light, drugs that damage DNA and drugs that block DNA synthesis all raise p53 within minutes. Later work found other triggers: low oxygen, a shortage of nucleotides and too much signaling by oncogenes. One experiment used thymocytes, white blood cells from the thymus. In normal mice, 80% of these cells died in the 25 hours after X-rays. In mice with one p53 copy, almost as many died. In mice without p53, fewer than 5% died.

The protein that destroys p53 is Mdm2. Mdm2 attaches chains of a small protein called ubiquitin to p53, which marks p53 for destruction. In turn, p53 switches on the gene for Mdm2. This negative feedback loop brings p53 back down once the damage is repaired. Damage and stress break this loop. DNA breaks activate the kinases ATM and Chk2, and stalled DNA copying activates the kinases ATR and Chk1. These kinases add phosphate to p53 near the site where Mdm2 binds, and this prevents the binding. Oncogene signals act through another protein, called ARF.

ARF Links the pRb and p53 Pathways

ARF is encoded in the same stretch of DNA as p16INK4A, but it is read in a different frame and from its own promoter. ARF binds Mdm2 and holds it in the nucleolus, a region of the nucleus, away from p53. The p53 protein then builds up. The gene for ARF is switched on by E2F. In a cell whose pRb pathway is broken, free E2F drives the cell into the S phase (Chapter 8). This E2F also makes the cell produce ARF, which raises p53, and p53 can then kill the cell.

Too much Myc has the same effect. In lymphoid tissue, Myc makes cells divide, but it does not increase their number, unless p53 or ARF is also lost. The stretch of DNA that encodes both p16INK4A and ARF is inactivated in about half of all human tumors. A single deletion there disables both the pRb pathway and the p53 pathway.

What p53 Does

The p53 protein is a transcription factor. One of its target genes is p21Cip1, whose protein blocks CDKs and stops the cell cycle. Other targets are genes for DNA repair and genes for proteins that trigger apoptosis, such as PUMA, Noxa and Bax. A cell with damaged DNA can therefore stop and repair the damage, stop dividing for good (a state called senescence), or kill itself.

An experiment showed how much this matters for the genome. Pregnant mice with one p53 copy, mated with males of the same kind, were treated with a chemical that causes mutations, ethylnitrosourea. Among the young mice that lacked p53, 70% developed brain tumors. Among those with one copy, 3.6% did, and among those with two copies, none did. Without p53, cells with damaged DNA survived and gave rise to tumors.

The p53 protein also protects the skin. When ultraviolet-B light damages the DNA of skin cells, p53 triggers their death. This is why skin peels several days after a sunburn. Ultraviolet light also causes p53 mutations, which are found in skin carcinomas.

Tumors that keep a normal p53 gene disable the pathway in other ways. They can lose ARF. They can amplify Mdm2, as some sarcomas do. Or viral proteins can disable p53: the E6 protein of HPV marks p53 for destruction, and the large T antigen of SV40 binds it.

Apoptosis

Apoptosis is a program of cell suicide that almost all cells carry. It removes cells during normal development. For example, more than half of the nerve cells first formed in the brain die by apoptosis. A cell in apoptosis forms bulges on its surface. Its nucleus shrinks and breaks up, and its DNA is cut into small pieces. Within about an hour, the cell falls apart into fragments, which neighboring cells or macrophages swallow. Unlike necrosis, in which cells burst, apoptosis causes no inflammation. Damage, oncogene signals and lack of oxygen can all trigger apoptosis, so it is a constant threat to a cell on its way to becoming a cancer cell.

In the intrinsic pathway, the decision is made at the mitochondria, by the Bcl-2 family of proteins. Bax and Bak can open pores in the outer membrane of the mitochondria. Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL prevent this. Sensor proteins, such as PUMA, Noxa, Bim and Bid, block Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL. The p53 protein switches on the genes for some of these sensors. When the sensors win, the mitochondria release cytochrome c. Cytochrome c binds the protein Apaf-1, and seven Apaf-1 molecules form a wheel-shaped complex, the apoptosome. The apoptosome activates caspase 9. Caspase 9 activates caspases 3, 6 and 7, which cut hundreds of proteins and take the cell apart.

In the extrinsic pathway, other cells display or release ligands such as FasL, TNF and TRAIL. These ligands bind death receptors on the cell surface. The receptors activate caspase 8, which then activates caspases 3, 6 and 7. Caspase 8 also cuts Bid, which acts on the mitochondria. Other proteins, called IAPs, block caspases directly.

Cancer cells block apoptosis at many points. At least half of human tumors show raised levels of Bcl-2. Bax is mutated in some colon cancers, and caspase 8 is lost in some neuroblastomas. Activation of the PI3K-Akt pathway (Chapter 6) also blocks several proteins that promote apoptosis.

Blocking apoptosis and driving cell division work together. Mice that carry an extra Bcl-2 gene active in their B cells show no increase in deaths. Mice that carry an extra, overactive Myc gene almost all die of lymphomas by 100 days of age. Mice that carry both die of lymphomas by 50 days. Bcl-2 lets cells survive, and Myc makes them divide, so together they produce far more tumor cells than either one alone.
