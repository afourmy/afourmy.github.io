Chapter 4: Cellular Oncogenes

Most Human Tumors Carry No Virus

In the 1970s, the tumor viruses of Chapter 3 suggested a simple theory of human cancer: common viruses infect human tissues and transform some of their cells. The facts did not support this theory. Most human cancers do not spread from person to person, and groups of cases in one place are hard to find. Attempts during the 1970s to find viruses in most kinds of human tumors failed. More than one hundred types of tumor are seen in the clinic. Of these, only two common ones in the Western world, liver carcinomas and cervical carcinomas, were known or suspected to be caused by viruses. Another idea was that carcinogens wake up inactive viral genomes carried in our own DNA. No experiment supported it.

The remaining theory combined two facts from Chapters 2 and 3. Carcinogens cause mutations, and the normal genome contains proto-oncogenes that can be changed into oncogenes. A carcinogen might therefore mutate a proto-oncogene where it lies on its chromosome, without any virus. The mutant gene would then make the cell that carries it grow, together with all its descendants. This theory made a prediction: the DNA of cells transformed by chemicals, and the DNA of human tumor cells, should contain mutant genes able to transform other cells. The difficulty was to find a few such genes among the tens of thousands of genes of a cell.

Tumor DNA Transforms Normal Cells

The test was to put the DNA of cancer cells into normal cells and look for foci of transformed cells, as Temin had done with RSV. In 1972, a method called transfection made this possible. DNA mixed with calcium phosphate forms small crystals that cells take up. Some of the cells then insert pieces of the foreign DNA into their own chromosomes. A line of mouse cells called NIH 3T3 turned out to be especially good at this.

The donor DNA came from mouse cells of a line called C3H10T1/2. These cells had been transformed by repeated treatment with 3-methylcholanthrene, a carcinogen found in coal tar, and they carried no trace of any virus. In 1978 and 1979, the DNA of these cells produced many foci in cultures of NIH 3T3 cells after several weeks. The cells of the foci grew without anchorage and formed tumors in mice. DNA from untransformed C3H10T1/2 cells produced no foci. The carcinogen had therefore created, in the donor cells, DNA able to transform other cells. DNA from human bladder, lung and colon carcinomas, and from a human leukemia, also transformed the mouse cells. These oncogenes therefore work across species and across tissues.

Each recipient cell takes up only about 0.1% of the donor genome. The chance that it takes up two particular genes that lie far apart in the donor genome is then about $10^{-3} \times 10^{-3} = 10^{-6}$, which is very unlikely. A single donor gene is therefore enough to transform an NIH 3T3 cell. The carcinogen had created one mutant gene that could transform the recipient cells on its own.

The Same Genes the Retroviruses Captured

The genes found by transfection were compared with the oncogenes of retroviruses. The comparison used the Southern blot, a method in which a labeled probe shows which DNA fragments carry a matching sequence. A probe for H-ras, the oncogene of Harvey sarcoma virus, detected the oncogene of a human bladder carcinoma. A probe for K-ras, the oncogene of Kirsten sarcoma virus, detected the oncogene of a human colon carcinoma.

Other links came from extra copies of known oncogenes in tumor DNA. A human leukemia cell line carried about 10 to 20 copies of myc per cell instead of two. The gene erbB, the oncogene of avian erythroblastosis virus, was present in extra copies in human stomach, breast and brain tumors. The same proto-oncogenes can therefore be activated by a retrovirus in animals or by a mutation in human cells. The c-src gene, the first proto-oncogene found, is an exception. Repeated searches for mutant versions of it in human tumors have failed.

Two Ways to Activate a Proto-Oncogene

A mutation can turn a proto-oncogene into an oncogene in two ways. It can change the structure of the protein, so that the protein works when it should not. Or it can change the amount of protein that the cell makes, so that a normal protein is present in excess and at all times. Normally, the promoter of a proto-oncogene responds to signals from outside the cell. For example, the c-myc gene is switched on and off as the growth factors around the cell change (Chapter 5). In a retrovirus, the captured gene is transcribed from the viral promoter at a high and constant level, whatever signals the cell receives. Both kinds of change free the gene from the control of the cell. The result is an oncogene that pushes the cell to grow all the time.

A Single Base Change in H-ras

The bladder carcinoma had only one copy of the H-ras oncogene, so extra copies could not explain its effect. Once cloned, the oncogene was found on a piece of DNA 6.6 thousand bases long. Normal human DNA carried a piece that looked identical, holding the normal H-ras gene. Restriction enzymes cut DNA at specific short sequences, and both pieces were cut at the same positions. Yet only the oncogene transformed NIH 3T3 cells. Swapping parts between the two cloned pieces narrowed the important difference down to a segment of 350 bases. Sequencing this segment in both showed a single difference: a G in the normal gene had become a T in the oncogene.

This discovery was made in 1982 by three laboratories at the same time. It was the first specific mutation shown to cause a human cancer. The mutation was somatic, and it was one base out of the 3.2 billion of the genome. The patient was a man who had smoked for four decades. Carcinogens from tobacco smoke pass from the lungs into the blood, and the kidneys remove them into the urine. In the bladder, some of them enter the cells of the bladder lining and damage their DNA. The H-ras mutation arose in one such cell. Years later, after further mutations described in Chapter 11, its descendants formed the tumor.

Mutations of ras Hit Three Codons

Mammals have three ras genes: H-ras, K-ras and N-ras. Within ten years, many human tumors were found to carry a point mutation in one of them. The mutation is almost always in codon 12, less often in codon 13 or 61. Nearly 10,000 mutant K-ras genes from human tumors have been sequenced. Among them, codon 13 is mutated only about 12% as often as codon 12, and codon 61 rarely. Yet the gene has 189 codons, and all of them suffer mutations at similar rates.

Mutations in the other codons either have no effect or harm the cell. Only cells mutated at these three positions gain an advantage, grow and form tumors. Chapter 5 explains why, from the structure of the Ras protein. In a survey of 40,000 human tumors, 22% carried an activating mutation in K-ras, 8.2% in N-ras and 3.7% in H-ras.

The frequency of ras mutations varies widely between tissues, from 98% of pancreatic carcinomas to 10% of bladder carcinomas. In one experiment, the protein-coding part of the K-ras gene of mice was replaced with that of H-ras. A chemical carcinogen then caused lung tumors with H-ras mutations, instead of the K-ras mutations found in normal mice. The DNA that controls when and where a ras gene is transcribed therefore decides which ras gene is found mutated in a tissue. The protein that the gene encodes does not decide it.

The two ways of activating a proto-oncogene can also combine. The H-ras gene of Harvey sarcoma virus carries a point mutation, and it is also overexpressed from the viral promoter. In many tumors, a mutant ras gene is later amplified, which makes the signal even stronger.

Extra Copies of a Gene: Amplification

Gene amplification is the repeated copying of one region of a chromosome, called the amplicon. The rest of the chromosome is not copied. An amplicon usually spans 0.5 to 10 million bases, far more than the gene it carries, so amplified regions can often be seen in the microscope. The copies may stay in the chromosome as a long series of repeats, called a homogeneously staining region. They may also break away as small separate pieces of DNA, called double minutes (Chapter 1).

N-myc, a relative of c-myc, is amplified in about 30% of advanced neuroblastomas. Neuroblastomas are tumors of the nerve tissue outside the brain in children. Children whose tumors have amplified N-myc have a much worse outlook.

In 1987, the gene HER2 was found amplified in many breast cancers. HER2 is also called erbB2 or neu, and it is related to erbB. Patients whose tumors carried more than five copies of HER2 relapsed more often after treatment. In a large group of patients, those whose tumors made normal amounts of the HER2 protein lived for a median of 6 to 7 years after diagnosis. Those whose tumors made too much of it lived for a median of 3 years.

A 2010 survey listed 77 genes that are amplified again and again in human cancers and probably help the tumors grow. An amplicon also carries neighboring genes that play no role. The gene whose amplification helps the tumor is called the driver, and the other genes are called passengers. Amplification does not always raise expression: only 40 to 60% of amplified genes show a matching increase in their RNA.

Translocations That Raise Expression

Burkitt's lymphoma is a cancer of lymphocytes. It is common among young children in East and Central Africa, where long-term infection with Epstein-Barr virus and with the parasites of malaria both contribute to it. Its cells almost always carry a translocation that joins part of chromosome 8 to chromosome 14, 2 or 22. In 1982, the c-myc gene was found on chromosome 8, right at the point of exchange. On the other side of the junction lies one of three groups of antibody genes. The heavy-chain genes on chromosome 14 are involved in 75% of cases. The lambda light-chain genes on chromosome 22 are involved in 16%, and the kappa light-chain genes on chromosome 2 in 9%. During the development of the immune system, enzymes cut and rejoin antibody genes (Chapter 15). Sometimes they join an antibody gene to myc by mistake.

The translocation separates myc from its own promoter. It places myc under the control of the enhancer of the antibody gene, a DNA element that makes transcription strong and constant in the lymphocytes that make antibodies. The myc gene then makes a normal Myc protein, but in large amounts and whatever signals the cell receives. This makes the cell divide. Myc can thus be activated in three ways: by a provirus (Chapter 3), by amplification or by translocation. All three lead to the same result, a high and constant level of Myc outside the control of the cell.

More than 300 recurring translocations have been listed in human tumors, most of them in cancers of blood cells. In 2005, about 70% of prostate carcinomas were found to carry translocations that deregulate one of two genes for transcription factors, ERG or ETV1.

Proteins Changed by Deletion or Fusion

Other mutations change the structure of a protein more than a point mutation does. The receptor for epidermal growth factor (EGF) crosses the cell membrane. Its outer part binds EGF, and its inner part then sends a signal into the cell. In about one-third of glioblastomas, a type of brain tumor, the receptor lacks most of its outer part. It then sends growth signals even when no EGF is present. Chapter 5 shows how such changes work in a family of about 60 similar receptors.

More than 95% of chronic myelogenous leukemias (CML), a cancer of white blood cells, carry the Philadelphia chromosome. This is a small chromosome 22 that results from an exchange with chromosome 9, written t(9;22)(q34;q11). The exchange joins most of the gene bcr, on chromosome 22, to most of the gene abl, on chromosome 9. The abl gene is the one captured by the Abelson leukemia virus. The joined gene makes a single fused protein. Abl is a tyrosine kinase, an enzyme that attaches phosphate groups to the amino acid tyrosine in other proteins to pass on a signal. In the fused protein, the Abl kinase is active all the time. The Burkitt translocation changes the amount of a normal protein. This translocation, by contrast, creates a protein with a new structure.

Dozens of other translocations create fused proteins, and most of them are each found in a small group of blood cancers. The gene MLL is joined to at least 50 different partner genes in different leukemias. Fusions also occur in solid tumors. In 80% of alveolar rhabdomyosarcomas, childhood tumors of muscle precursor cells, a translocation joins two genes for transcription factors, PAX3 or PAX7 with FKHR. The fused protein activates its target genes far more strongly than either normal protein. Each of these rearrangements is a very rare accident. Yet it is found in all the cancer cells of the tumor that carries it. This shows that the cell in which it happened grew faster than its neighbors.

Infections and Viral Oncogenes

About one-fifth of cancer deaths worldwide are linked to infections. Stomach cancer causes 9% of cancer deaths in the world, and most of these are linked to long-term infection by Helicobacter pylori. Liver carcinomas cause 6%, and almost all of them are linked to hepatitis B and C viruses. Cervical carcinomas cause 5%, and almost all of them are due to human papillomaviruses.

Most viruses that cause cancer carry their own oncogenes. Examples are HPV, Epstein-Barr virus and human herpesvirus 8. These viral genes have been part of the viral genomes for millions of years. They help the virus by making the infected cell a better place for the virus to multiply, and the cancer is a side effect. Chapters 8 and 9 describe how several of these genes act. Chapter 11 describes how the hepatitis viruses cause cancer without oncogenes of their own.
