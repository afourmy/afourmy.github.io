Chapter 13: Stroma and Angiogenesis

A Tumor Contains Many Normal Cells

Carcinomas make up more than 80% of the human cancer burden. A carcinoma is not only a mass of cancer cells. It also contains normal cells recruited from the surrounding tissue, which together form the stroma. The stroma includes fibroblasts, myofibroblasts, the endothelial cells that line blood vessels, pericytes, fat cells, macrophages, lymphocytes and mast cells. In common carcinomas of the breast, colon, stomach and pancreas, stromal cells can make up as much as 90% of the cells of the tumor. In Hodgkin's lymphoma, up to 99% of the cells are normal lymphocytes around a few cancer cells.

The space between the cells is filled by the extracellular matrix (ECM), a network of proteins such as collagens. The ECM can make up as much as 25% of the mass of a carcinoma. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas, it can make up as much as 90%. The ECM also holds growth factors in an inactive, bound form. Enzymes that cut the ECM release these factors.

Cancer Cells and Stromal Cells Exchange Signals

Signals between different cell types are called heterotypic signals. In normal tissues, epithelial cells and stromal cells control each other's growth in this way. Tumors keep this exchange. The cancer cells of many carcinomas release PDGF, and fibroblasts and macrophages in the stroma carry the PDGF receptor. The stromal cells answer by releasing IGF-1, which helps the cancer cells survive and grow. In melanomas, PDGF from the cancer cells makes fibroblasts release IGF-2. Stromal cells of breast cancers release the growth factor HGF and the chemokine SDF-1, both of which stimulate the cancer cells.

Blood vessels are built by the same kind of exchange. Endothelial cells release PDGF and HB-EGF, which attract pericytes and smooth muscle cells to the outside of the vessel. The pericytes answer with VEGF and angiopoietin-1, which keep the endothelial cells alive.

Carcinoma cells need their stroma. In 1961, basal cell carcinomas were removed from patients and put back at another site on the same patients' skin. Implants that included stroma grew well. Implants of cancer cells without stroma failed to grow when examined 1 to 5 weeks later. Most carcinoma cells are therefore hard to grow in culture. The cancer cells that do grow in culture have been selected for their ability to divide without stroma.

Tumors Use the Program of Wound Healing

In 1986, Harold Dvorak described tumors as "wounds that do not heal." When the skin is wounded, blood platelets clump and release PDGF and TGF-β. Fibrinogen leaks from damaged vessels and forms a fibrin clot. PDGF attracts fibroblasts and makes them divide. TGF-β turns them into myofibroblasts, which contract the wound. Myofibroblasts and macrophages release matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), enzymes that cut the ECM and free the growth factors bound to it.

The freed factors, together with VEGF, make new blood vessels grow into the wound. Epithelial cells at the edge of the wound take on the shape and mobility of fibroblasts. This change is called the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). The cells move into the wound and divide. They then return to the epithelial state and rebuild the epithelium.

Tumors switch on the same program but never switch it off. Carcinoma cells of the breast, prostate, colon and lung release PDGF continuously, while platelets in a wound release it in one brief burst. Tumor capillaries leak all the time, so fibrin forms in the tumor as in a wound. Fibroblasts near the cancer cells become myofibroblasts and lay down collagen. In many carcinomas, a high ratio of stromal cells to cancer cells predicts a worse outcome.

Experiments show that stromal cells make tumors grow faster. Transformed human breast epithelial cells took more than two months to form a tumor in mice, and formed one in only about half of them. Mixed with fibroblasts from a normal human breast, they formed tumors in a third of the time and in every mouse. The added fibroblasts spared the cancer cells the weeks needed to recruit fibroblasts from the host. In another experiment, immortalized human prostate epithelial cells alone did not form tumors. Mixed with fibroblasts from a prostate carcinoma, they formed tumors with a median weight 10 times larger. The same cells mixed with normal prostate fibroblasts formed growths of about 7 mg.

Macrophages Help Tumors Progress

Cancer cells attract monocytes from the blood with factors such as MCP-1 and CSF-1. In the tumor, the monocytes become macrophages. Macrophages release VEGF and interleukin-8, which make blood vessels grow, and MMPs, which cut the ECM. In advanced breast cancers, macrophages are the main source of EGF for the cancer cells. A high density of macrophages predicts a poor outcome in gliomas and in carcinomas of the breast, ovary, prostate, cervix, bladder and lung.

In one experiment, mice developed breast tumors driven by an oncogene. In mice that could not make CSF-1, the tumors contained almost no macrophages. The primary tumors grew normally, but they rarely became invasive or metastatic. Macrophages are therefore needed for the later steps of progression.

Tumors Need Blood Vessels

Oxygen diffuses only about 0.2 mm through living tissue. Tumor cells more than about 0.2 mm from a blood vessel stop growing, and cells farther away die. In sections of tumors, dead tissue begins as close as 85 micrometers from a capillary in a melanoma and 110 micrometers in a prostate carcinoma. A tumor therefore cannot grow beyond a very small size without new blood vessels. The growth of new blood vessels is called angiogenesis.

Tumors attract blood vessels. Small pieces of tumor implanted in the cornea or the ear of rabbits drew dense networks of new capillaries within days. The main signal is VEGF. Other factors also make vessels grow, such as FGF2, interleukin-8, angiopoietin, TGF-β and PDGF.

Tumor vessels are built badly. Capillaries in tumors are typically three times wider than normal ones. The vessels form a chaotic network with dead ends and loops. The endothelial cells leave gaps between them, often several micrometers wide, and pericytes are loosely attached. The walls of tumor capillaries are about 10 times more permeable than normal walls. VEGF causes much of this leakiness. It was first described in 1979 as a vascular permeability factor.

Fluid that leaks from the capillaries accumulates in the tumor. In normal tissues, lymphatic vessels drain this fluid. In solid tumors, the growing mass of cells compresses the lymphatic vessels until they collapse. Myofibroblasts, stimulated by PDGF, also contract and squeeze fluid out. The pressure of the fluid inside a tumor becomes high. Drugs then move poorly from the blood into the tumor, because they need a pressure drop from the vessel to the tissue.

Vessel permeability can also help treatment. WNT-medulloblastomas are brain tumors of children that release inhibitors of Wnt signaling. Brain capillaries need Wnt signaling to form properly. The vessels of these tumors therefore have large gaps, which increase drug transport into the tumor up to 300-fold. These tumors are almost always curable with low doses of drugs.

The Angiogenic Switch

Many tumors cannot recruit blood vessels at first and must acquire this ability. The clearest model is the Rip-Tag mouse. It carries the large T and small t genes of SV40 under the control of the insulin gene promoter, so they are made in the insulin-producing cells of the pancreas. These cells form about 400 small clusters called islets. By 10 weeks of age, as many as half of the islets become hyperplastic. About 10% of the hyperplastic islets then become angiogenic. By 12 to 14 weeks, about 3% of the hyperplastic islets have become carcinomas.

Hyperplastic islets grow to a diameter of about 0.1 to 0.2 mm and then stop. Their cells keep dividing, but an equal number die by apoptosis because they lack oxygen. At some point, a few islets suddenly start to attract new capillaries and then grow quickly. This event is called the angiogenic switch. It is a separate step of tumor progression.

The islet cells make VEGF all along, as normal islets do. The VEGF stays bound to the ECM and is inactive. The switch happens when mast cells and macrophages arrive and release MMP-9. This enzyme cuts the ECM and frees the VEGF. In Rip-Tag mice that lack mast cells, the islet tumors start normally but never become angiogenic, and they stay 0.1 to 0.2 mm wide. A bone marrow transplant that restores mast cells also restores angiogenesis, and large tumors then appear.

In human tumors, a high density of small vessels predicts a worse outcome. Breast cancer patients whose tumors had many microvessels had a lower chance of remaining free of disease over 20 years. One study followed breast cancer patients whose lymph nodes contained cancer cells. When their tumors made low levels of both HER2 and VEGF, more than 80% were alive after 8 years. When their tumors made high levels of both, only about 35% were alive.

Blocking Angiogenesis

Drugs against tumor vessels had an expected advantage. Tumors usually become resistant to drugs because their unstable genomes produce resistant variants (Chapter 12). The endothelial cells of tumor vessels are normal cells with stable genomes, so they should not become resistant. They also divide much more often than normal endothelial cells. In tumors they may live only a week, while normal endothelial cells live hundreds of days, some as long as seven years.

Avastin (bevacizumab) is an antibody that binds and neutralizes VEGF-A. Patients with metastatic colon carcinoma who received Avastin with the drug 5-fluorouracil lived on average four months longer than patients who received 5-fluorouracil alone. In non-small-cell lung carcinoma, Avastin added to chemotherapy extended survival by about two months. By 2020, 3.5 million patients had received Avastin. In many cancers, it slows the progression of the disease but does not greatly increase overall survival.

Experiments in Rip-Tag mice show why one drug is not enough. A drug that blocks the VEGF receptor, SU5416, prevented the angiogenic switch in 90% of early islets. It had no effect on established tumors. A drug that blocks the PDGF receptor, SU6668, acts on pericytes, which carry this receptor and support the endothelial cells. Given together, the two drugs reduced the volume of established tumors by about 85%.

Tumors also find ways around these drugs. In Rip-Tag mice treated with an antibody against the VEGF receptor, the tumors first shrank by more than half. The surviving cells then made more FGFs, which replaced VEGF as the signal for new vessels, and the tumors grew again. In slowly growing tumors, vessels may mature over years and become as resistant as normal vessels. In some tumors, cancer cells themselves line vessel-like channels. Cancer cells that have lost p53 can also survive with less oxygen.
