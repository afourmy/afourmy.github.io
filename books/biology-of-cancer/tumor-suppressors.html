Chapter 7: Tumor Suppressor Genes

Genes Whose Loss Causes Cancer

Oncogenes are dominant: one mutant copy is enough to change the cell. A second kind of cancer gene works the other way. Researchers fused cancer cells with normal cells to make hybrid cells. When the cancer cells came from most human tumors not caused by viruses, or from rodent tumors caused by chemicals, the hybrid cells could not form tumors. The normal cell therefore supplied something that stopped the cancer, and the cancer cell had lost it. When the cancer cells came from tumors caused by viruses, the hybrid cells usually still formed tumors, as expected from dominant viral oncogenes. Genes whose loss contributes to cancer are called tumor suppressor genes.

It is easy to inactivate a gene. A point mutation at many different places can do it, and so can a small insertion or deletion that shifts the reading frame, or the deletion of the whole gene. A ras gene, by contrast, is activated only by substitutions at codons 12, 13 or 61. But a cell has two copies of every tumor suppressor gene, and one remaining good copy is enough to do the job. Suppose one copy is inactivated with a probability of about $10^{-6}$ per cell generation. Two independent mutations in the same cell then have a probability of about $10^{-12}$, which seemed too rare for tumors to form. The answer to this puzzle came from a rare eye tumor of children.

Retinoblastoma and Knudson's Two Hits

Retinoblastoma is a tumor of the retina. It arises in the cells that would become the light-sensing cells of the eye. It affects about 1 in 20,000 children and is diagnosed between birth and the ages of 6 to 8 years, rarely later.

The disease has two forms. Children from families with no history of the disease have a single tumor in one eye. This form is called unilateral retinoblastoma, and once the tumor is removed, the child has no further risk. In the family form, the child of a parent who had the disease has several tumors in both eyes. This is called bilateral retinoblastoma. These children also have a risk of bone cancer in their teens that is more than 500 times the normal risk. Half of their own children develop the disease.

In 1971, Alfred Knudson studied the ages at which the two forms are diagnosed. The family form appeared as if one random event were enough to cause a tumor. The other form appeared as if two random events were needed. Knudson's explanation was that both events inactivate the same gene, later named Rb. A child who inherits one defective copy carries it in every cell of the retina. Any one of these cells then needs only one more event. A child born with two good copies needs both copies to be lost in the same cell.

Dominant in Families, Recessive in Cells

Inside a cell, a mutant Rb allele is recessive: a cell with one mutant copy and one normal copy behaves normally. In a family, however, it behaves like a dominant allele, because a carrier almost always develops the disease. A simple estimate explains this. Suppose the developing retina contains $5 \times 10^6$ cells that can form a tumor, and each of them loses its remaining good copy with probability $10^{-6}$. A carrier then has, on average, 5 cells per eye that lose all Rb function, and each of these cells can grow into a tumor.

With this average, the number of tumors in one eye follows a Poisson distribution. The chance that an eye has no tumor is $e^{-5}$, about 0.7%. The chance that both eyes are affected is about 98.7%.

Losing the Second Copy

The second copy is usually not lost by a second, independent mutation. When a cell divides, its two copies of a chromosome can exchange pieces. This is called mitotic recombination. It happens at a rate of $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-4}$ per cell generation, far more often than a mutation in a given gene. After such an exchange, one daughter cell can end up with two copies of the piece that carries the mutant Rb, so the mutation is now on both chromosomes.

Other events have the same result. In gene conversion, one copy of a sequence is overwritten by the other. A cell can also lose the chromosome that carries the good copy and then duplicate the chromosome that remains. Finally, the good copy can be removed by a deletion.

All these events change a whole region of the chromosome, not only the Rb gene. At many places near Rb, a person often carries two different versions of the DNA, one on each chromosome. The person is then said to be heterozygous at these places. After the event, the tumor carries only one version. It has lost heterozygosity, which is abbreviated LOH. This loss marks the region where a tumor suppressor gene lies.

The gene for the enzyme esterase D lies near Rb on chromosome 13, and its two versions can be told apart in the laboratory. In retinoblastomas of children who carried both versions, one version was often lost. LOH at other DNA markers then narrowed down the region, and Rb was cloned in 1986.

The Rb gene spans about 190,000 bases. Some retinoblastomas had lost both copies of parts of it. One osteosarcoma had lost a piece that lay entirely inside the gene. This confirmed that the Rb gene itself, and not a neighboring gene, is the target.

Searching tumors for regions that often lose heterozygosity became a general way to find tumor suppressor genes, and more than 30 were cloned this way. Large-scale sequencing has now identified more than 200 human genes that meet the criteria for tumor suppressor genes. One example is TP53, which is inherited in mutant form in Li-Fraumeni syndrome. Others are APC, inherited in familial adenomatous polyposis, PTEN, and NF1, inherited in neurofibromatosis.

Silencing by Methylation

A tumor suppressor gene can also be silenced without any change in its sequence, by methylation of its promoter (Chapter 1). Some tumor suppressor genes are inactivated by inherited mutations in family cancer syndromes. More than half of these genes are silenced by promoter methylation in cancers that do not run in families. Often, one copy is methylated first, and LOH then copies the methylated version onto the other chromosome.

One study examined the gene p16INK4A in normal-looking lung epithelial cells. The gene was methylated in 44% of samples from current and former smokers, and in none of the samples from people who had never smoked. Methylation of genes that control growth can therefore happen early, long before a tissue looks abnormal under the microscope.

NF1: a Tumor Suppressor That Switches Off Ras

Neurofibromatosis type 1 affects about 1 in 3,500 people worldwide. Its main feature is neurofibromas, benign tumors of the sheaths around nerves, and some of these tumors become malignant. The NF1 gene encodes a protein called neurofibromin, which is a GAP for Ras (Chapter 5). It inserts an arginine, the amino acid at position 1276 of neurofibromin, into the active site of Ras, and this speeds up the cutting of GTP. When this arginine is replaced by another amino acid, the GAP activity falls 1000-fold.

A cell that has lost both copies of NF1 keeps more of its Ras bound to GTP. Here, the loss of a tumor suppressor gene has the same effect as an activated ras oncogene.

APC and the Colonic Crypt

People with familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) develop hundreds or thousands of polyps in the colon at a young age, and some of the polyps become carcinomas. FAP causes a little less than 1% of colon cancers in the West. The inherited gene, APC, was cloned in 1991. It is mutated in about 90% of colon cancers that do not run in families. In these cancers, both copies of APC are lost in two steps, as for Rb.

The APC protein works with axin and the kinase GSK-3 beta to mark beta-catenin for destruction (Chapter 6). Without working APC, beta-catenin builds up and keeps switching on its target genes.

The lining of the colon has many crypts, small pits in its wall. At the bottom of each crypt, cells of the stroma release Wnt factors. The Wnt factors keep beta-catenin high in the cells at the bottom, and this keeps these cells in a stem cell state. The daughters of the stem cells move up the wall of the crypt. On the way, they stop dividing and mature. At the surface they die and are shed into the gut. In the human colon, this whole journey takes only 3 to 4 days.

A cell that acquires a mutation on its way up is therefore lost within days. A cell that loses APC is different. Its beta-catenin stays high, as if Wnt were present, so the cell and its descendants stay in the crypt and keep dividing. They then have time to acquire further mutations. This is why the loss of APC is usually the first step in colon cancer.

Why Inherited Cancer Genes Are Mostly Suppressors

Most inherited cancer syndromes are caused by defective copies of tumor suppressor genes or of the DNA repair genes of Chapter 12. An allele that makes an oncoprotein active all the time acts in every cell of the embryo, and it usually disrupts development. A defective copy of a tumor suppressor gene, in contrast, does no harm as long as the good copy remains.

A carrier has the defective copy in each of about $10^{13}$ cells, and LOH happens at a rate of $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-4}$ per cell generation. Yet carriers do not develop millions of tumors. Losing a tumor suppressor gene is therefore a necessary step for most cancers, but not a sufficient one, and further changes are needed (Chapter 11). Retinoblastoma is an exception: in a precursor cell of the retina, the loss of Rb is almost enough.
