Chapter 11: Multi-Step Tumorigenesis

Cancer Incidence Rises Steeply With Age

In the United States, a 70-year-old man is up to 1000 times more likely to die of colon cancer than a 10-year-old boy. The incidence of a cancer is the rate at which new cases are diagnosed. For many cancers, the incidence grows as a power of age. Depending on the cancer type, it grows as $a^4$ to $a^7$, where $a$ is the age at diagnosis. For carcinomas as a whole, the death rate grows roughly as the fifth or sixth power of age.

A power law of this kind follows from a chain of rare, independent events. Suppose a tumor forms only after $k$ such events, each with a small and constant probability per year. The probability that all of them have happened by age $a$ is then proportional to $a^k$. The incidence is proportional to $a^{k-1}$, which is a straight line of slope $k-1$ on logarithmic axes. In 1953, C.O. Nordling plotted male cancer death rates against age in this way for four countries. The slopes pointed to five or six rate-limiting events. They were similar in all four countries. A slope of 5, for example, means six events.

Each event is rare. Colon cancer becomes frequent only in the seventh and eighth decades of life. Each event probably happens once every 10 to 15 years on average, and the whole series usually takes 40 to 60 years. The true number of steps is probably higher. Some steps happen quickly, and these do not change the slope. Autopsies show that 60 to 70% of people carry undiagnosed tumors by the end of their lives.

Tumors Develop Through Visible Stages

The stages of tumor development are clearest in the colon. The inner surface of the intestine is an epithelium that is only one cell thick in many places. Each minute, 20 to 50 million of these cells die and are replaced in the duodenum, the first part of the small intestine. The colon replaces a tenth as many. Cancers of the intestine arise from these epithelial cells, not from the tissue beneath them.

Pathologists see a series of lesions that become more and more abnormal. In hyperplasia, the cells look normal but divide too often, and the epithelium becomes several cells thick. In dysplasia, the cells look abnormal and no longer form an ordered layer. A larger growth of dysplastic cells is called a polyp or an adenoma. An adenoma is benign: it has not broken through the basement membrane, the thin sheet of protein under the epithelium. A carcinoma has broken through this membrane and invades the tissue below it. The last stage is a metastasis, for example in the liver.

Three observations show that carcinomas grow out of adenomas. First, carcinomas are sometimes seen growing directly out of an adenoma. Second, removing polyps during colonoscopy lowers the number of later colorectal cancers by more than 80%, or by 60% in a more cautious estimate. At least 60% of colon carcinomas therefore come from adenomas that can be detected. Third, some lesions can be followed over time. In one patient, three early lesions were found in the lungs. After 19 months, one of them had become a carcinoma and the other two had disappeared.

People with familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) inherit a mutant copy of the APC gene (Chapter 7). They develop from dozens to more than a thousand polyps in the colon. Each polyp turns into a carcinoma at a rate of about 2.5 per 1000 polyps per year. Similar series of stages exist in other organs, such as the cervix, the breast and the prostate.

Each Stage Carries New Mutations

In 1989, Vogelstein and his colleagues matched the stages of colon cancer with genetic changes. Early adenomas often showed loss of heterozygosity on the long arm of chromosome 5, where the APC gene lies. Loss of heterozygosity is the loss of the remaining good copy of a gene (Chapter 7). Almost half of slightly larger adenomas also carried a mutant K-ras gene. Even larger adenomas often showed loss of heterozygosity on the long arm of chromosome 18. About half of the carcinomas also showed it on the short arm of chromosome 17, where p53 lies.

The fastest route to a carcinoma is a mutation in APC, then in K-ras, then in Smad4, and finally in p53. Each of these genes stands for a signaling pathway, and the pathway is what matters. A mutation in the gene for β-catenin can replace the loss of APC. A mutation in B-Raf or in PI3 kinase can replace a mutant Ras. Silencing the gene for the type I TGF-β receptor can replace the loss of Smad4. Mutations in p53 are found in carcinomas but not in adenomas, so this step comes late.

Loss of APC starts almost all colon carcinomas. People with Li-Fraumeni syndrome inherit a mutant copy of p53 (Chapter 9). They develop tumors in many organs but rarely in the colon. This shows that colon cancer has to begin with the APC step.

Tumor Progression Is Darwinian Evolution Among Cells

Tumor progression follows the rules of Darwinian evolution, with cells in place of organisms. A mutation gives one cell an advantage in growth or survival. Its descendants outgrow their neighbors and form a large clone, the set of descendants of one cell. When the clone reaches about $10^6$ cells, a second rare mutation becomes likely. Such a mutation strikes about once per $10^6$ cell generations. The descendants of the doubly mutant cell form a second clone. This clone grows inside the first one and replaces it. This repeated replacement is called clonal succession.

Each new mutation must add a new advantage. In colon cancer, a K-ras mutation adds to the effect of the lost APC rather than repeating it. A second mutation in the same pathway gives no further advantage. A tumor suppressor gene needs two hits, one for each copy. The number of changes can therefore reach twice the number of genes involved.

The steps are far apart in time. In colon cancer, a decade or more may separate one step from the next. The step from polyp to invasive cancer has been estimated to take up to 17 years. In many people, the whole process would stretch over a century. Certain foods may shorten the intervals to 5 years or less, instead of the usual 10 or 20. The whole process then takes 30 or 40 years. Colon cancer incidence varies up to twentyfold between countries. Differences in food, not in genes, cause this variation.

Inherited Mutations and Fields of Mutant Cells

In people with FAP, every cell of the colon already carries one mutant copy of APC. The first rate-limiting step has therefore already happened in every cell. This is why these people develop so many polyps. Many inherited cancer syndromes work in the same way: they speed up one of the rate-limiting steps.

Sometimes several tumors appear at the same time in one tissue, many centimeters apart. This is called field cancerization. It happens when a large clone of mutant cells spreads through the tissue while still looking normal under the microscope. Several tumors can then grow out of this shared field. They carry the mutations of the field plus their own. In sun-exposed human skin, normal-looking patches of cells with a mutant p53 can contain up to 3000 cells. In the lungs of one patient, cells in widely separated areas carried the same rare p53 mutation.

Cells Within a Tumor Differ

Early in progression, selection removes the less fit cells, and each clone is made of nearly identical cells. This is called purifying selection. Late in progression, the genome becomes unstable, and new variants arise faster than selection removes them. The tumor then splits into several subclones that live side by side. Most mutations in a tumor are passenger mutations, which give no advantage and are carried along with the driver mutations. Passenger mutations far outnumber driver mutations.

Sequencing of different regions of one renal cell carcinoma showed this branching. A mutation in the VHL gene came first and was present in every tumor cell. The tumor then split into two main branches, and only one of them gave rise to metastases. Mutations in the SETD2 and KDM5C genes arose several times, independently, in different regions. In one pancreatic carcinoma, each subclone contained at least 100 million cells, and several subclones seeded their own metastases.

Cancer Stem Cells

Cells within a tumor also differ in ways that do not depend on mutations. In acute myelogenous leukemia (AML), a minority of the cancer cells, less than 1%, can be separated from the rest. As few as 5000 of these rare cells produced leukemia when injected into mice. As many as 500,000 cells of the majority did not. The majority cells resembled mature white blood cells and divided only a limited number of times.

Breast cancers show the same pattern. A minority of about 2% of the cancer cells can seed tumors. Only 200 of these cells formed a tumor in a mouse, while 20,000 of the other cells did not. The two groups of cells were genetically identical. The rare cells are called cancer stem cells. Like normal stem cells, they renew themselves and give rise to cells that divide for a while and then stop.

Cancer stem cells are often more resistant to chemotherapy, radiotherapy and immunotherapy than the other cancer cells. They can survive a treatment that removes the bulk of the tumor and then form new tumors. The cells that seed metastases must also be able to act as cancer stem cells (Chapter 14).

The Hallmarks of Cancer

Another way to describe cancer lists the traits that all cancer cells share. These traits are called the hallmarks of cancer. Cancer cells depend less on growth factors, resist signals that stop division, divide without limit and resist apoptosis. Cells of solid tumors also cause new blood vessels to grow, invade tissues and spread to distant sites. Two further traits are escape from the immune system and a changed use of glucose. Two processes help cells gain these traits: a high mutation rate (Chapter 12) and inflammation.

Genes and hallmarks do not match one to one. By blocking p53, the large T antigen of SV40 produces at least three traits: resistance to growth inhibition, escape from apoptosis and immortality. Ras affects apoptosis, the need for growth factors, the growth of blood vessels and invasion. The hTERT gene affects only immortality. About 95% of tumors keep traits of the tissue they came from. This lets pathologists classify them.

One Oncogene Does Not Transform a Normal Cell

A single mutant ras gene transformed NIH 3T3 mouse cells (Chapter 4). A simple calculation shows that one oncogene cannot be enough in the body. About $3 \times 10^{11}$ cell divisions happen in the human body each day. From known mutation rates, several thousand new ras oncogenes should arise by accident every day. The body should then carry millions of cells with a ras oncogene. People do not develop new tumors every day.

The explanation lies in the cells that were used. NIH 3T3 cells are a cell line: they had adapted to life in culture and could divide without limit. Cells taken directly from rat or hamster embryos, called primary cells, were not transformed by ras alone. They made the Ras protein but did not become tumor cells. In the body, too, a single oncogene is not enough. A ras oncogene put into the colon cells of mice produces only hyperplasia. Mutant H-ras cells appear in mouse skin within a week of a mutagen, yet the skin looks normal for a year.

A ras oncogene together with a myc oncogene transformed primary rat embryo fibroblasts. Neither gene did this alone, and the E1A gene of adenovirus could replace myc. The two genes supply different traits. Ras let the cells grow without anchorage and lose contact inhibition. Myc made them immortal and less dependent on growth factors. Many other pairs of oncogenes cooperate in this way. Often one gene drives division and the other blocks a defense that the first one triggers. For example, Myc drives both division and apoptosis, and Bcl-2 blocks the apoptosis.

The same cooperation happens in mice. Mice that carried a ras or a myc oncogene in all breast epithelial cells showed at most hyperplasia. They developed breast tumors only after a long delay. Mice that carried both oncogenes developed tumors much faster, but still only after a delay. Even two oncogenes needed at least one more random event. Rat cells transformed by ras and myc in culture usually go on to lose the p53 pathway, which may be this extra event.

Transforming Human Cells Takes Five Changes

Human cells are harder to transform than rodent cells. Mouse cells in culture often become immortal on their own, but human cells rarely if ever do. For 15 years, attempts to transform human cells with oncogenes failed. Three genes introduced together finally turned several types of normal human cells into tumor cells. They were hTERT, the early region of SV40 and a ras oncogene.

The early region of SV40 makes two proteins, large T and small t. Large T blocks both pRb and p53. Small t disturbs PP2A, an enzyme that removes phosphate groups from proteins. The three genes therefore change five pathways: Ras, pRb, p53, telomerase and PP2A. Four of these changes are common in human tumors. In a survey of glioblastomas, 74% had changes in the pRb, p53 and Ras-MAPK pathways. Nearly all of them also made high levels of hTERT.

Sequencing of 2658 tumors of 38 types of human cancer found four to five driver mutations per tumor on average. Human colon stem cells can grow in culture into small three-dimensional crypts, called organoids. Four mutations, in APC, p53, K-ras and Smad4, turned these cells into invasive carcinoma cells. Each mutation replaced one of the four growth factors that the normal cells needed: Wnt, R-spondin, EGF and Noggin. Human tumors thus appear to need about half a dozen rate-limiting changes.

Large Animals Need Extra Defenses

The cells of a mouse go through about $10^{11}$ divisions in its lifetime. The cells of a human go through about $10^{16}$. These numbers come from body mass multiplied by lifespan. Each division is a chance for a mutation, yet humans are not more prone to cancer than mice. This is known as Peto's paradox. Bowhead whales can weigh 100 tons and live 200 years. By the same count, their risk of cancer would be 10,000 times ours. A 2021 study found fast evolution of tumor suppressor genes in whales.

At least 10 orders of mammals descend from a common ancestor of about 25 grams. Each of them evolved a large body independently over the last 100 million years. Each must also have evolved its own defenses against cancer. Many of these defenses are built into individual cells. Human cells need more changes to become tumor cells than mouse cells do. Among rodents, the species with larger bodies have less telomerase in their normal cells.

Most Carcinogens Are Not Mutagens

Few human cancers can be traced to a specific mutagen. The exceptions are tobacco smoke, ultraviolet light, aflatoxin B1 and alcohol. The liver turns alcohol into acetaldehyde, which is a mutagen. In one study, 20 chemicals known or suspected to cause human cancer were tested in mice. All 20 caused tumors. Sequencing of 188 of these tumors showed that only 3 of the chemicals raised the number of mutations. The tumors caused by the other 17 carried no extra mutations.

Initiation and Promotion

Experiments on mouse skin in the early 1940s separated two kinds of carcinogen. A single painting of the skin with DMBA, a mutagen found in coal tar, causes no tumors. Repeated paintings with TPA, an irritant from the seeds of the croton plant, cause no tumors either. One painting with DMBA followed by weekly paintings with TPA produces papillomas after 4 to 8 weeks, depending on the strain of mice. A papilloma is a benign growth of the skin, similar to an adenoma of the colon. DMBA is called the initiator and TPA the promoter.

Variations of the experiment show what each agent does. The skin can rest for a year between DMBA and TPA and still form papillomas. DMBA therefore leaves a permanent change, which is a mutation. TPA painted on a nearby patch that DMBA did not reach gives no papillomas. If TPA is stopped soon after the papillomas appear, they regress, so the effect of TPA is reversible and not genetic. If TPA continues for months, some papillomas no longer need it. A second painting of DMBA on a papilloma can produce a carcinoma.

TPA acts by switching on protein kinase C. A promoter makes the initiated cells divide and form a large clone. In a large clone, the next rare mutation becomes likely. Random mutations accumulate at similar rates in tissues whose cancer rates differ greatly. The pace of promotion, not the rate of mutation, therefore often decides how fast a tumor forms.

Hormones as Promoters

Hormones that make cells divide can act as promoters. In each menstrual cycle, the cells lining the ducts of the breast divide and then regress. The more cycles a woman has in her life, the higher her risk of breast cancer. By one estimate, the lifetime risk falls by 20% for each year that the first menstruation is delayed. In pairs of identical twins who both developed breast cancer, the twin who menstruated first was 5.4 times more likely to be diagnosed first. Women who stop menstruating before age 45 have about half the risk of women who continue to age 55 or later.

The ovaries are the main source of estrogen, and removing them lowers the risk sharply. In a Dutch study, women whose menopause started before age 36 had a 90% lower risk of breast cancer. Their early menopause was caused by chemotherapy for Hodgkin's lymphoma. After menopause, women with breast cancer have on average 15% more estrogen in their blood than women without it.

Inflammation as a Promoter

TPA is an irritant, and it causes inflammation. Inflammation is the response of the body to injury or infection, in which immune cells move into the tissue. Wounding the skin promotes papillomas at least as well as TPA does. Drugs that block inflammation also block promotion by TPA. Many human cancers arise at sites of long-lasting inflammation. Examples are the liver after hepatitis B or C infection, the stomach after infection by Helicobacter pylori, the colon in inflammatory bowel disease and the gallbladder after years of gallstones.

An initiator and a promoter together can multiply each other's effects. In a study in Shanghai, infection with hepatitis B virus raised the risk of liver cancer about 7-fold. Food contaminated with aflatoxin B1 raised it about 3-fold. People exposed to both had about 60 times the risk. If the extra risks simply added up, the combined risk would be 9 times. If the two factors multiplied, it would be 21 times.

In mouse skin, TPA makes the skin cells release the protein TNF-α, which draws immune cells into the tissue. Mice that lack the TNF-α gene develop only 5 to 10% as many skin carcinomas under DMBA and TPA. The signal runs from TPA through protein kinase C to the transcription factor NF-κB, which switches on TNF-α. NF-κB also switches on genes for survival, such as Bcl-xL, and genes for division, such as Myc and cyclin D1. It also switches on COX-2. This enzyme makes prostaglandin E2, a small molecule that drives inflammation.

Aspirin and other non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) block COX-2. They also lower the risk of several cancers when taken daily for several years, usually five. In one study, users were 46% less likely to develop pancreatic carcinomas. Other studies found about 30% fewer colorectal carcinomas and 25% fewer breast cancers. Regular aspirin lowered the risk of stomach cancer by about 40% in people infected with Helicobacter pylori, but not in uninfected people. NSAIDs also cause bleeding in the stomach and gut, which killed 16,550 people in the United States in 1998.

Cancer Cells Use Glucose Differently

In 1924, Otto Warburg found that many cancer cells break down glucose mostly by glycolysis, even when oxygen is plentiful. This is called aerobic glycolysis, or the Warburg effect. Glycolysis makes 2 molecules of ATP, the energy carrier of the cell, from one molecule of glucose. When its product is further broken down in the mitochondria, one glucose yields 33 to 36 ATP. Most normal cells have oxygen and use this complete route. As many as 80% of cancer cells break down most of their glucose by glycolysis. Warburg thought that their mitochondria had stopped working, but this is not the case.

Cancer cells also take up large amounts of glucose through the transporter GLUT1. The oncogenes myc, ras and src raise the level of GLUT1. PET scans locate tumors through this uptake, using a radioactive form of glucose. The intermediate products of glycolysis are also raw material for nucleotides and lipids. Normal cells use more than 30% of their imported glucose to make ATP, while cancer cells use only about 1%. Normal cells that divide quickly also use aerobic glycolysis. It is part of the program of cell growth, not an invention of cancer cells.
