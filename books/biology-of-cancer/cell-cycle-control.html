Chapter 8: pRb and the Cell Cycle Clock

The Phases of the Cell Cycle

A dividing cell goes through a fixed series of phases. After a cell is born, it often spends 12 to 15 hours in the G1 phase, the first gap. During G1, the cell decides whether to divide again, to rest or to become a specialized cell. In the S phase, the cell copies its DNA, about $6.4 \times 10^9$ base pairs in a human cell. This takes 6 to 8 hours in many mammalian cells grown in a dish. Most cells then spend 3 to 5 hours in the G2 phase, preparing to divide. Division itself, the M phase or mitosis, usually takes about an hour. A cell that stops dividing leaves the cycle during G1 and enters a resting state called G0.

The cycle also has checkpoints, which stop it when something is wrong. A cell does not start the S phase with damaged DNA. It does not enter the M phase until all its DNA is copied. It does not separate its chromosomes until all of them are attached to the spindle, the structure that pulls them apart. Cancer cells often disable these checkpoints, and this lets mutations build up faster (Chapter 12). Cancer cells do not usually divide faster than normal cells. Instead, they keep starting new cycles when normal cells would stop.

The Restriction Point

Suppose growth factors are removed from cells grown in a dish. If this happens before the cells have completed 80 to 90% of G1, the cells stop and return to G0. If it happens during the last 10 to 20% of G1, the cells complete the cycle anyway. The point between these two parts of G1 is called the restriction point, or R point.

Before the R point, the cell responds to signals from outside. Growth factors push it forward, and inhibitors such as TGF-beta hold it back. After the R point, the cell goes through the S, G2 and M phases on its own. The signals of Chapters 5 and 6 include some that promote growth and some that inhibit it. All of them lead to a single decision, taken late in G1: whether to pass the R point.

Cyclins and Cyclin-Dependent Kinases

The cycle is driven by a set of kinases called cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs). A CDK is active only when it is bound to a partner protein called a cyclin. For example, the binding of cyclin A to CDK2 makes CDK2 400,000 times more active. Different pairs of cyclin and CDK act one after another. D-type cyclins work with CDK4 or CDK6 in early and middle G1. Cyclin E works with CDK2 at the end of G1. Cyclin A works with CDK2 during the S phase, and cyclin B works with CDK1 in mitosis. Each cyclin builds up slowly and is then destroyed quickly at a set point. The cycle can therefore only move forward.

The D-type cyclins are different. Their levels follow the growth factors outside the cell. Growth factors act through the Ras-ERK pathway, which switches on the gene for cyclin D1 (Chapter 6). When growth factors are removed, the level of cyclin D falls quickly. Cyclin D is therefore the link between the outside of the cell and the cycle.

Inhibitor proteins work against the CDKs. The INK4 family, which includes p15INK4B and p16INK4A, blocks CDK4 and CDK6. The Cip/Kip family, which includes p21Cip1 and p27Kip1, blocks the complexes that contain CDK2. TGF-beta stops the cycle partly by raising the amount of p15INK4B. In skin cells, it raises the RNA of p15INK4B 30 times.

A Switch Made by Titration

Early in G1, a cell contains a lot of p27. The p27 is bound to the few cyclin E-CDK2 complexes and keeps them inactive. Growth factors raise the level of cyclin D, and new cyclin D-CDK4/6 complexes form. Each of these complexes captures one molecule of p27, and it is not inhibited by it. Less and less p27 is left for cyclin E-CDK2, until some cyclin E-CDK2 is freed.

The freed cyclin E-CDK2 then phosphorylates p27, which marks p27 for destruction. This frees even more cyclin E-CDK2. The timing of the R point is thus set by how fast growth factors make cyclin D-CDK4/6 build up. The positive feedback turns this slow build-up into a sudden switch.

pRb Controls the Restriction Point

The protein made by the Rb gene of Chapter 7, called pRb, controls the R point. At the end of each mitosis, pRb loses all its phosphate groups. During G1, cyclin D-CDK4/6 adds a single phosphate to any one of about 14 sites on pRb. At the R point, cyclin E-CDK2 adds phosphates to at least 12 more sites. The protein then stays heavily phosphorylated until the next mitosis.

In 1988, the oncoprotein E1A of adenovirus was found to bind pRb. The large T antigen of SV40 and the E7 protein of human papillomavirus also bind pRb. These three viral proteins are not built alike. Yet all three bind the active form of pRb, which carries few phosphates, and they disable it. Each of these viruses needs its host cell to enter the S phase, so that the virus can copy its own DNA.

Lightly phosphorylated pRb binds transcription factors of the E2F family. These transcription factors recognize a DNA sequence close to TTTCCCGC in the promoters of their target genes. When pRb is bound to E2F on a promoter, it blocks the gene. It also brings enzymes that pack the DNA more tightly. Heavy phosphorylation makes pRb let go of E2F. E2F then switches on the genes needed for the S phase, such as the genes for DNA polymerase and thymidine kinase. It also switches on the genes for cyclin E and for E2F1 itself.

This creates a second positive feedback loop: cyclin E-CDK2 frees E2F, and E2F makes more cyclin E. Once it has passed the R point, the cell cannot go back. The main job of Ras in G1 is to lead to the inactivation of pRb. A mutant form of Ras that blocks Ras signaling prevents normal cells from entering the S phase. It does not stop cells that lack Rb.

Myc Drives Cells Through G1

More than 70% of human tumors overexpress Myc or one of its relatives, N-Myc and L-Myc. Myc is a transcription factor that works as a pair with another protein, Max. The level of Myc follows the growth factors around the cell, while the level of Max stays constant. The Myc-Max pair raises the expression of many genes. Among them are the genes for cyclin D2, for CDK4 and for proteins that destroy p27. Myc also switches off the genes for the inhibitors p15INK4B and p21Cip1. It also drives the making of ribosomes and the growth of the cell.

Too much Myc therefore pushes cells past the R point. It also makes them resistant to the signal of TGF-beta that normally stops growth. In cells from 12 breast carcinomas, TGF-beta no longer switched off myc in any of them, although its other effects still worked in 11.

Tumors Disable the pRb Pathway

Human tumors disable the pRb pathway in many different ways. Rb itself is lost in retinoblastomas and in many other tumors. Cyclin D1 is overexpressed in most breast cancers that carry the estrogen receptor. Its gene, CCND1, is amplified in many squamous cell carcinomas of the head and neck. Mutant copies of the gene for p16INK4A are inherited in some families with melanoma. In many tumors that do not run in families, this gene is deleted or silenced by methylation (Chapter 7).

A few melanomas, some of them in families, carry a point mutation in CDK4 called R24C. It makes CDK4 resistant to all INK4 inhibitors. In cervical carcinomas, the E7 protein of HPV binds and disables pRb. All these changes have the same effect, and in each type of tumor, most tumors carry at least one of them.

The pathway is therefore a target for drugs. In a study reported in 2016, patients with one kind of breast cancer received an inhibitor of CDK4 and CDK6 in addition to their usual treatment. The time before their disease grew again rose from 14.5 months to 24.8 months. The pRb pathway responds to signals from outside the cell. The protein p53, described in Chapter 9, responds to damage and stress inside the cell.
