Chapter 3: Tumor Viruses

A Virus That Causes Sarcomas

A virus is a particle made of a genome, of DNA or of RNA, wrapped in a protein coat. A virus cannot live on its own. It multiplies only inside a host cell, using the cell's enzymes and building blocks to make hundreds or thousands of new particles. Viruses were first defined by their size: they are infectious agents small enough to pass through a filter that holds back bacteria.

Beginning in 1909, Peyton Rous, at the Rockefeller Institute in New York, studied a sarcoma that had appeared in the breast muscle of a hen. He ground the tumor with sand and passed the extract through such a filter. He then injected the liquid that came through the filter into young chickens, and they developed sarcomas where it was injected. The new tumors could be ground and filtered in turn, and they caused tumors in other chickens. This cycle could be repeated again and again.

The agent was therefore a virus, now called Rous sarcoma virus (RSV). It multiplied in the chicken, because far more virus could be recovered from a tumor than had been injected. RSV caused sarcomas on a predictable timetable, so researchers could produce cancer when they wanted, instead of waiting for tumors to appear. Viruses that cause tumors are called tumor viruses. For decades, few researchers worked on RSV. This changed when cell culture made it possible to study what the virus does to single cells.

Transformation of Cells in a Dish

Howard Temin and Harry Rubin, working in the laboratory of Renato Dulbecco at the California Institute of Technology, infected chicken fibroblasts with RSV. Fibroblasts are cells of connective tissue, and here they grew on the bottom of a Petri dish. Normal fibroblasts spread until they cover the bottom of the dish. They then stop dividing, and they form a layer one cell thick, called a monolayer. This stop in division when cells touch their neighbors is called contact inhibition.

A cell infected by RSV behaves differently. The infected cell and all its descendants become rounded, and they keep dividing after they touch other cells. They pile up into a clump of cells, called a focus, which can be seen with the naked eye. All the cells of a focus descend from the one infected cell, so a focus is a clone. The change of a normal cell into a cell that behaves like a tumor cell is called transformation. RSV showed that transformation can happen in a dish, where each cell can be followed under the microscope.

Transformed cells differ from normal cells in many ways at once. They are often rounded, and they have lost contact inhibition, so they reach a much higher density in a dish. They can grow while floating in a soft gel. Normal fibroblasts and epithelial cells cannot do this: they must be attached to a solid surface. This ability to grow without attachment is called anchorage independence, and it usually predicts that the cells can form tumors. Transformed cells can also divide without limit, while normal cells stop after a certain number of divisions (Chapter 10). They need less of the growth factors that normal cells require (Chapter 5). They take up more glucose, and they break it down by glycolysis even when oxygen is present.

The final test of transformation is whether the cells can form tumors in animals. The cells are injected into mice of the same genetic strain, which do not reject them. They can also be injected into Nude mice, which have no thymus and a weak immune system. Fully transformed cells grow into tumors in these mice.

The Viral Gene Must Keep Working

The descendants of an infected cell keep copies of the RSV genome. This alone does not show whether the virus is still needed. RSV might act only once, change the infected cell for good, and then play no further role. This is called hit-and-run. An experiment done in 1970 at the University of California, Berkeley, answered the question. It used a temperature-sensitive mutant of RSV, a mutant whose protein works at one temperature and fails at another.

This mutant transformed chicken cells at 37 °C but not at 41 °C, the normal body temperature of chickens. Infected cells kept at 37 °C grew as transformed cells for many generations. Weeks later, the culture was moved to 41 °C, and the cells quickly took on the shape and growth of uninfected cells. When the culture was moved back to 37 °C, the cells became transformed again. A viral protein is therefore needed to keep cells transformed, not only to start the transformation, and RSV does not act by hit-and-run. The protein must be made in every descendant of the infected cell, so the viral genome must be passed on at every cell division.

DNA Tumor Viruses

Several families of viruses with DNA genomes also cause tumors. The Shope papillomavirus causes warts on the skin of rabbits, and these warts sometimes become squamous cell carcinomas. More than 100 related human papillomaviruses were found later. The polyomaviruses include mouse polyomavirus and SV40, a virus of monkeys. Human adenovirus, which causes infections of the airways, causes tumors in hamsters. Among the herpesviruses, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) is one of the causes of Burkitt's lymphoma in young children in equatorial Africa and New Guinea. It is also one of the causes of nasopharyngeal carcinoma in Southeast Asia.

Between one-third and two-thirds of the polio vaccines given from 1955 to 1963 contained SV40, and 10 to 30 million people were exposed to the virus through vaccination. Forty years of follow-up showed little or no increase in their risk of cancer.

SV40 multiplies in the kidney cells of several species of monkeys. These cells are called permissive. The infection kills the cell and releases tens of thousands of new virus particles. SV40 cannot multiply in mouse, rat or hamster cells, which are called nonpermissive. In about one infected nonpermissive cell out of thousands, the cell survives and becomes transformed. It then changes shape, loses contact inhibition and can form tumors, like the cells transformed by RSV.

The genomes of papillomaviruses and polyomaviruses are small circles of DNA, 5 to 8 thousand bases long. The human genome is 3.2 billion bases long. Most of the few genes of these viruses are needed to copy the virus and to build its coat. Only a handful of genes can therefore cause transformation, and each of them must change many traits of the cell at once. A gene that changes several traits at once is called pleiotropic.

Viral DNA Joins the Host Chromosome

Cells transformed by SV40 make a viral protein called the T antigen, for tumor antigen. Cells that lose the T antigen also lose the transformed state, so the viral DNA must stay in every transformed cell. In a permissive cell, SV40 copies its DNA as separate circles, apart from the chromosomes. In a nonpermissive cell, however, SV40 cannot copy its DNA at all. A free piece of viral DNA also lacks a centromere, the part of a chromosome that makes sure each daughter cell receives a copy.

In 1968, researchers found that the SV40 DNA of transformed mouse cells stays attached to the chromosomal DNA of the cells. It was found in DNA molecules longer than 50 thousand bases, not in the circles of about 5 thousand bases of free virus. It stayed attached even in alkaline conditions, which break every bond except covalent ones. The viral DNA had therefore been inserted into a chromosome. This insertion is called integration. Once integrated, the viral DNA is copied with the chromosome at every division, like any gene of the cell.

For SV40, integration is a rare accident. It happens in far fewer than one infection in 1,000, at random places in the genome, and often only part of the viral genome is inserted. Transformed cells always keep the early region of the viral genome, which carries the genes needed for transformation. They often lose the late region, which is not needed.

Human cervical carcinomas show the same pattern. More than 99.7% of these tumors carry pieces of the DNA of human papillomavirus (HPV) in their chromosomes. Integration usually breaks the viral gene E2. The E2 protein normally represses two other viral genes, E6 and E7. Without E2, these two genes are expressed at a high level. The E6 and E7 proteins then push the cells toward cancer, by the mechanisms described in Chapters 8 and 9.

Retroviruses Copy Their RNA into DNA

The genome of RSV is single-stranded RNA, and RNA cannot be inserted into chromosomal DNA. In the 1960s, Temin proposed a solution. After infection, RSV makes a double-stranded DNA copy of its RNA genome. This DNA copy, which Temin called the provirus, is inserted into a chromosome of the host cell. The cell then copies the provirus with its own chromosomes at every division. The cell also transcribes the provirus into viral RNA. This RNA serves as the genome of new virus particles and as messenger RNA for making viral proteins.

At the time, information was known to flow only from DNA to RNA to protein, so copying RNA into DNA seemed impossible. In 1970, Temin and David Baltimore independently found that RSV particles carry an enzyme that copies RNA into DNA. It is called reverse transcriptase. RSV and related viruses, including HIV, came to be called retroviruses, because their information flows backward.

For a retrovirus, integration is a normal step of every infection. The viral gene pol encodes the reverse transcriptase and also the integrase, an enzyme whose only job is to insert the whole provirus into a chromosome. The gene gag encodes the proteins of the core of the particle, and the gene env encodes the proteins on its outer membrane. Each end of the provirus carries the same sequence, called a long terminal repeat (LTR). The LTRs contain strong promoters, the DNA sequences where transcription starts.

The genome of RSV is closely related to that of avian leukosis virus (ALV), a common virus of chickens that does not transform cells. RSV differs from ALV by one extra gene near its end, called src (pronounced "sark", for sarcoma). Mutants of RSV that had lost src still multiplied normally but no longer transformed cells. All the transforming power of RSV is therefore in this one gene.

The src Gene Is a Normal Chicken Gene

In 1974, the laboratory of J. Michael Bishop and Harold Varmus in San Francisco set out to make a probe for src. A probe is a piece of DNA that pairs only with a chosen sequence and that can be made radioactive, so it can be detected. They copied the RNA of normal RSV into DNA fragments. They then mixed the fragments with the RNA of a mutant RSV that lacked src. The fragments that paired with the mutant RNA came from gag, pol or env, and they were removed. The fragments that were left came from src.

The plan was to follow src into infected cells, because uninfected cells were expected to have none. Instead, in 1975, the probe found src sequences in the DNA of uninfected chicken cells. Each cell had two copies, one on each of the two chromosomes inherited from the two parents. Most genes of a cell are present in the same way.

The probe also paired with the DNA of other birds and of several mammals. The more distant a species was from the chicken in evolution, the weaker the pairing was. This is the pattern of a gene inherited from a common ancestor, whose sequence slowly changed in each line of descent. It is not the pattern of a gene recently inserted by a virus. The src gene was later found in fruit flies and even in sponges, whose ancestors separated from those of vertebrates about 550 million years ago. The gene must therefore play a vital role in normal cells.

The cellular gene is called c-src, and the viral version is called v-src. An oncogene is a gene that can transform a normal cell into a tumor cell. A normal gene that can be changed into an oncogene is called a proto-oncogene. The c-src gene was the first proto-oncogene to be found.

How RSV Acquired src

The most likely explanation starts from ALV. By chance, an ALV provirus was inserted next to the c-src gene in an infected chicken cell. Transcription that started in the provirus continued into c-src. It produced a single RNA molecule that carried both viral and cellular sequences. The introns of c-src, the stretches between its coding pieces, were then removed from this RNA. The RNA was packed into a virus particle. A later infection added ALV sequence on the other side of src, which gave the genome of RSV. Inside the virus, src then changed into the powerful oncogene v-src. This capture may have happened only once in the history of cancer research.

The discovery had three consequences. First, the normal genome of vertebrates contains genes that can become cancer genes. Mutations might therefore turn a proto-oncogene into an oncogene where it lies on its chromosome, without any virus. Second, the single gene v-src produces all the traits of a transformed cell. A few key genes may therefore be enough to make a cell form tumors, instead of 20 or 30 genes for 20 or 30 traits. Third, other retroviruses that cause tumors might carry other captured proto-oncogenes.

Many Proto-Oncogenes

Within a year, researchers started to look for other captured genes. MC29 is a chicken virus that causes a cancer of blood-forming cells. It carries the oncogene v-myc, which comes from the chicken gene c-myc, and like RSV it descends from ALV. A feline leukemia virus that captured the gene fes became feline sarcoma virus. A rat-mouse leukemia virus captured the genes H-ras and K-ras on two separate occasions, which gave the Harvey and Kirsten sarcoma viruses. Other examples are abl, carried by the Abelson leukemia virus of mice, and sis, carried by simian sarcoma virus. More than fifty vertebrate proto-oncogenes are now known, and thirty of them were first found in a retrovirus that transforms cells.

Each proto-oncogene found in one species is present in all other vertebrates. For example, chickens, mice and humans all have a c-myc gene, and the three versions seem to work the same way. The whole set of proto-oncogenes must therefore have existed in the common ancestor of mammals and birds. The captured gene usually replaces some of the genes that the virus needs to multiply, so most retroviruses that carry an oncogene cannot multiply on their own. RSV is an exception, because it kept gag, pol and env. Retroviruses that carry an oncogene are called acutely transforming viruses, because they cause tumors within days or weeks after injection.

Proviruses That Activate a Gene in Place

ALV and murine leukemia virus (MLV) carry no oncogene. They do not change the shape or growth of the cells they infect in a dish. Yet they cause leukemias in animals, after several months. In 1981, researchers mapped the proviruses in leukemias caused by ALV, each leukemia from a different chicken. A provirus can be inserted at millions of places in the genome. Yet in more than 80% of these leukemias, the provirus sat inside or right next to the c-myc gene.

Most of these proviruses sat between the first exon of c-myc, which codes for no protein, and the second exon, where the protein-coding sequence begins. They also pointed in the same direction as the gene. The promoter in the viral LTR then transcribes c-myc all the time and at a high rate, outside the controls that normally regulate the gene. The resulting excess of Myc protein makes the cell divide.

This mechanism is called insertional mutagenesis. In an infected chicken, the virus spreads through the cells that make blood. There are tens of millions of infections, and each one inserts a provirus at a random place. Perhaps one insertion in 10 million lands next to c-myc. A leukemia can only appear after the virus has infected enough cells for this rare event to happen. This explains the delay of weeks to months. Suppose that each insertion lands next to c-myc with probability $p = 10^{-7}$, independently of the others. The chance that at least one of $N$ insertions does so is then $1 - (1-p)^N$.

The provirus sits next to the gene it activates, so reading the DNA around the proviruses of several tumors reveals the gene. In breast cancers of mice caused by mouse mammary tumor virus, most proviruses clustered at three places, near genes named int-1, int-2 and int-3. The int-1 gene turned out to be the mouse version of the fruit-fly gene wingless, and it was renamed Wnt-1. It was the first of the Wnt genes, whose signaling pathway is overactive in many human cancers (Chapters 5 and 6). In birds and mice, proviruses have been found next to more than 25 different proto-oncogenes. Insertional mutagenesis also caused leukemias in several children treated by gene therapy, in which a retrovirus was used to insert a working copy of a gene into the patients' cells.

A Retrovirus with Its Own Oncogene: HTLV-I

Human T-cell leukemia virus type I (HTLV-I) belongs to neither group. It does not carry a captured oncogene, and it does not act by insertion next to a particular gene. HTLV-I infects about 1% of the people of Kyushu, the southern island of Japan. In Japan as a whole, leukemias caused by HTLV-I kill about 1,000 people a year. A lifelong infection carries a 3 to 4% risk of adult T-cell leukemia. The virus passes mainly from mother to baby through milk.

The best understood of its cancer-causing genes is tax. The virus needs the Tax protein to transcribe its own provirus. Tax also switches on two signaling pathways of the cell (Chapter 6), which make T lymphocytes divide and protect them from cell death. Here the oncogene is a normal part of the viral life cycle, not a captured gene of the cell.

Infections Behind Human Cancers

About one-fifth of the world's cancers are linked to infections. Hepatitis B and C viruses cause about 600,000 cancer deaths. They cause liver cancers that arise after years of chronic inflammation of the liver. Human papillomaviruses cause about 610,000 cancer deaths, through their own oncogenic proteins. EBV is one of the causes of lymphomas and of nasopharyngeal carcinoma. None of these agents is one of the retroviruses or SV40 studied in the laboratory in the 1970s. The value of those laboratory viruses is that they led to the human genes that play central roles in all types of cancer.

A bacterium, Helicobacter pylori, causes most stomach cancers. Almost 1 million new cases of stomach cancer were diagnosed worldwide in 2012, and three-quarters of them were likely to be fatal. At least 75% of these deaths are due to long-term infection by H. pylori. In Japan, 99% of stomach cancers are linked to it. Like the hepatitis viruses, H. pylori causes long-lasting inflammation.

Some strains of H. pylori also carry a 40,000-base stretch of genes for about 30 proteins. These proteins open a channel into the cells that line the stomach, and the bacterium injects its protein CagA through this channel. Inside the cells, CagA disturbs their shape, their junctions with neighboring cells, their signaling and their division. CagA is needed for a tumor to form, but not afterwards. In this it differs from Src, which must keep working. People are usually infected early in life, and the tumors appear after the age of 50, so the delay is more than five decades.
