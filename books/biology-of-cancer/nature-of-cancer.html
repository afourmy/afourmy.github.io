Chapter 2: The Nature of Cancer

Tumors Are Made of the Body's Own Cells

A tumor is a mass of cells that has grown beyond the normal size and order of a tissue. In the nineteenth century, microscopes showed that tumors are made of cells. These cells come from the body's own cells, and the tissue they form is less organized than normal tissue. Cancer is therefore a disease of the body's own cells. It is not an infection by a foreign organism.

A tumor that grows in place and does not invade the tissue around it is called benign. A tumor that invades the tissue around it is called malignant, and the word cancer means a malignant tumor. Cells can leave a malignant tumor, settle in distant parts of the body and grow into new tumors there. These new tumors are called metastases. The tumor where the disease began is called the primary tumor.

Most primary tumors in humans are benign and harmless. A benign tumor causes harm only in rare cases: when it presses on a vital organ, or when it releases too much of a hormone. For example, a benign tumor of the thyroid gland can release too much thyroid hormone. A benign tumor of the pituitary gland can release too much growth hormone, which makes some tissues grow too large, a condition called acromegaly. Deaths from benign tumors are uncommon. Almost all cancer deaths are caused by malignant tumors, and about 90% of them are caused by metastases rather than by the primary tumor.

Tumors Are Classified by Their Tissue of Origin

Most organs are built on the same plan. An epithelium is a sheet of cells that covers a surface or lines a cavity, such as the skin, the gut or the ducts of the breast. Under every epithelium lies a thin layer of proteins called the basement membrane. Under the basement membrane lies the stroma, the supporting tissue. The stroma contains cells such as fibroblasts and fat cells, and protein fibers such as collagen. Tumor cells keep many features of the normal cells they came from. As a result, a pathologist can tell the tissue of origin of more than 95% of tumors by looking at them under the microscope. Tumors are grouped by their tissue of origin into four main classes.

Carcinomas arise from epithelial cells. They cause more than 80% of cancer deaths in the Western world. Some epithelia protect a surface, such as the skin and most of the mouth. Their tumors are called squamous cell carcinomas. Other epithelia release substances into the ducts or cavities they line. Their tumors are called adenocarcinomas, and they are among the common tumors of the lung, colon, breast, pancreas, stomach and prostate.

Sarcomas arise from the cells of connective tissue, such as fibroblasts, fat cells, bone-forming cells and muscle cells. They make up only about 1% of the tumors seen in the clinic. The third class comes from the tissues that make blood cells. In leukemias, the tumor cells circulate one by one in the blood. In lymphomas, they form solid masses, most often in lymph nodes. These blood cancers cause about 7% of cancer deaths in the United States. The fourth class comes from the nervous system and includes gliomas and neuroblastomas. These tumors make up 1.3% of diagnosed cancers and cause about 2.5% of cancer deaths.

In 2 to 5% of cases, the tumor cells have lost almost all the features of their tissue of origin. Such tumors are called anaplastic. Sometimes an anaplastic tumor is found at a distant site and no primary tumor can be found. It is then classified as a cancer of unknown primary. Molecular tests can now identify the tissue of origin of almost 90% of these cancers. They use, for example, the pattern of DNA methylation described in Chapter 1, or the set of genes that the cells use.

Stages from Normal Tissue to Invasive Cancer

Tissue samples from patients show a range of growths between normal tissue and invasive cancer. In a hyperplasia, the tissue contains too many cells. The cells look normal and still form a tissue with a nearly normal structure. In a metaplasia, one type of normal cell layer is replaced by another type of normal cell that does not belong there. This happens most often where two kinds of epithelium meet.

Barrett's esophagus is an example of metaplasia. In this condition, the protective lining of the esophagus is replaced by secretory cells of the kind that line the stomach. The cause is acid that flows back from the stomach for years. People with Barrett's esophagus have a thirtyfold higher risk of a cancer of the esophagus called adenocarcinoma. This cancer develops in about 0.5% of these people each year.

In a dysplasia, the cells themselves look abnormal. Their nuclei vary in size and shape, and each nucleus is large compared with the rest of the cell. More cells than usual are dividing. The cells also lack the features of the mature cells of the tissue.

An adenoma is a growth of dysplastic tissue that is large enough to see with the naked eye. It is called a polyp in the colon, a papilloma in some other epithelia and a wart on the skin. An adenoma is still benign, because its cells stay on their side of the basement membrane. A tumor becomes malignant when its cells break through the basement membrane and invade the stroma below. It is then called an invasive carcinoma. To form metastases, the cells need further abilities, described in Chapter 14. The order of these stages was worked out from samples of different patients, each taken at a single time. The order from hyperplasia to metastasis is therefore likely, but it has not been proven.

A Tumor Descends from One Cell

A clone is a group of cells that all descend from one cell. A tumor is called monoclonal if all its cells descend from a single cell that changed. It is called polyclonal if several cells changed independently and each gave rise to part of the tumor. To tell the two apart, researchers look for a rare marker that two cells almost never acquire independently. A translocation is such a marker. A translocation is an exchange of pieces between two different chromosomes, and each one is a rare accident. In many tumors, every cancer cell carries the same unusual translocation. All the cells of such a tumor must therefore descend from the one cell in which the translocation happened.

A second test uses the X chromosome. Women have two X chromosomes. Early in the embryo, each cell switches off one of its two X chromosomes, chosen at random. All the descendants of that cell keep the same X chromosome switched off. As a result, every tissue of a woman's body is a patchwork: some patches of cells use one X chromosome, and other patches use the other.

The gene for the enzyme G6PD lies on the X chromosome. It exists in variants that can be told apart in the laboratory. More than 30% of African American women carry two different variants, one on each X chromosome. In most of their tissues, half of the cells make one variant of the enzyme and half make the other. In 1965, researchers examined leiomyomas, which are benign tumors of the uterus, from such women. Each tumor made only one of the two variants. Each tumor had therefore grown from a single cell.

A large tumor can be monoclonal even if it began from several cells. Suppose that ten cells became cancer cells at the same time. Each of them would give rise to its own clone. The ten clones would differ slightly in how fast their cells divide. The clone whose cells divide fastest would grow faster than all the others, and its share of the tumor would rise toward 100%. Many tumors develop over decades, which gives one clone plenty of time to take over. A tumor that began polyclonal can therefore end up monoclonal.

After one clone has taken over, its cells keep acquiring new mutations. Each new mutation starts a new subclone, and the subclones of a tumor can be drawn as a family tree. Mutations that were already present in the first cell are shared by all the subclones. They are called truncal mutations, because they sit on the trunk of the tree. In a kidney carcinoma studied in 2012, a mutation in the gene VHL was truncal. In the same tumor, three separate branches had each acquired their own mutation in the gene SETD2. A mutation in SETD2 had therefore given each of these branches a growth advantage, in addition to the truncal mutations.

Cancer Is Rare per Cell Division

An adult body contains about $3 \times 10^{13}$ cells. Other estimates go up to almost $10^{14}$. Cells die and are replaced all the time, almost $10^7$ times per second, and about $10^{16}$ cells are formed over a lifetime. Each division copies the genome, so each division can introduce errors. Suppose that changes in diet and lifestyle halved the risk of dying from cancer in the West. Cancer would then cause about 10% of all deaths. Under this assumption, only one cell division in $10^{17}$ leads to a cancer that is diagnosed. This is the number of cell divisions in the bodies of ten people over their whole lives. The body must therefore have very effective defenses against tumors.

Cancer Rates Differ Between Populations

Cell division works the same way in all human populations. If cell division were the main cause of cancer, cancer rates would be similar everywhere. Some cancers do occur at similar rates across the world, but many others differ greatly between countries. The incidence of a cancer is the rate at which it is diagnosed. It is usually given per 100,000 people per year and adjusted for the age of the population. Skin melanoma is diagnosed 155 times more often in Queensland, Australia, than in Japan. Incidence can be misleading, because it counts only what is diagnosed. For example, mammography finds many small growths in the breast that would have stayed harmless and unnoticed for the rest of a woman's life. In the United States, at least two thirds of diagnosed breast cancers may be of this kind. Such screening raises the incidence of a cancer without changing the number of dangerous cases. Mortality is the death rate from a cancer, adjusted in the same way. It is a more reliable measure of how often dangerous cancers occur. But mortality also depends on how well each country treats the disease. For example, cancer mortality in the United States has fallen by almost 35% over a generation.

Differences between countries could come from genes or from the environment. Studies of people who migrate separate the two. People in Japan get stomach cancer 6 to 8 times as often as Americans. When Japanese people move to the United States, their children get stomach cancer at about the same rate as other Americans, within one generation. Genes do not change in one generation, so the difference must come from the environment. Here, environment includes diet and way of life.

Large differences also exist within one country. Seventh-day Adventists belong to a religious group that discourages smoking, heavy drinking and eating animal protein. Their death rate from cancer is 40% lower for men and 24% lower for women than that of the general American population.

Specific Causes: Soot, Tobacco and Other Exposures

The first specific causes of cancer were found by studying jobs and habits. In 1761, John Hill linked cancer of the nose to heavy use of snuff, a powdered tobacco. In 1775, Percivall Pott, a surgeon in London, reported many skin cancers of the scrotum in young men who had worked as chimney sweeps when they were boys. Within three years, the Danish guild of chimney sweeps told its members to bathe every day, to wash off the material that seemed to cause the cancer. In the early twentieth century, doctors and others who worked with the new X-ray tubes got cancer more often than other people, often at the spot exposed to the rays.

In 1949 and 1950, two groups of researchers reported that heavy smokers had more than twenty times the risk of lung cancer of nonsmokers. The more cigarettes people smoked, the higher their risk. These findings suggested that many cancers have specific causes that can be identified. They also suggested that the chain of events leading from each cause to a tumor could one day be traced.

A study published in 2018 looked at the roughly 587,000 cancer deaths in the United States in 2014. It estimated that about 45% of them, some 265,150 deaths, came from factors that people can change. Less cautious estimates put this share at 50 to 60%. Smoking, obesity and alcohol together account for 39.3% of the deaths, most of that share. The other factors include infections, such as those with human papillomavirus and with the hepatitis B and C viruses.

Carcinogens Mutate DNA

A carcinogen is anything that causes cancer. In 1915, Katsusaburo Yamagiwa produced carcinomas on the ears of rabbits by painting them with coal tar again and again. Coal tar is similar to the soot of chimneys. In one rabbit, the tumor was removed after 660 days of painting. By the late 1930s, chemists had identified the compounds in tar that cause cancer. They are hydrocarbons formed when organic matter burns incompletely. One of them, benzo[a]pyrene, is also found in cigarette smoke.

In 1927, Hermann Muller showed that X-rays cause mutations in fruit flies. By the late 1940s, several chemicals related to the mustard gas of the First World War were found to cause mutations in fruit flies. Some of these chemicals were then shown to cause cancer in laboratory animals. A substance that causes mutations is called a mutagen. These findings led geneticists to propose that cancer is a disease of mutated genes, and that carcinogens cause cancer because they are mutagens.

In 1975, Bruce Ames used bacteria to measure how strongly carcinogens cause mutations. He used a strain of Salmonella with a mutation that prevents it from making the amino acid histidine. These bacteria cannot grow on a dish without histidine. A new mutation that repairs the defect lets a bacterium grow into a colony that can be seen by eye. The number of colonies therefore measures how strongly a chemical causes mutations. Many carcinogens do not cause mutations as they are. They must first be changed by enzymes of the liver, whose normal job is to break down foreign compounds. Ames therefore mixed each chemical with an extract of rat liver before adding it to the bacteria. With this step, many known carcinogens turned out to be mutagens.

Carcinogens differ in strength by up to a million times per unit of weight. For example, in rodents, one microgram of aflatoxin causes as much cancer as 10,000 micrograms of the synthetic compound benzidine. Aflatoxin is made by molds that grow on peanuts and wheat. Across many chemicals, strong mutagens in the Ames test were strong carcinogens, and weak mutagens were weak carcinogens. This relation held over a range of five orders of magnitude of strength. By 1976, Ames and his group had measured 300 compounds, and the Ames test became a way to screen substances for their power to cause cancer.

Carcinogens That Do Not Mutate DNA

Almost all compounds that cause mutations in human cells probably also cause cancer. The reverse is not true. By the 1990s, the Ames test had been used on many compounds. As many as 40% of the compounds known to cause cancer in rodents did not clearly cause mutations in the bacteria. Some carcinogens therefore act by mutating DNA, and others help tumors grow in other ways. Carcinogens of the second kind are called tumor promoters. They include substances that cause inflammation, and they may turn out to be more important causes of human cancer than the mutagens found by the Ames test. Chapter 11 describes them.

If carcinogens act by causing mutations, cancer cells should carry mutated genes. Chapters 3 and 4 describe how the first such genes were found.
