Chapter 6: Signaling Pathways

The Response to Growth Factors

Fibroblasts kept without serum for a few days stop dividing. When fresh serum is added, most of them start to copy their DNA within 9 to 12 hours, and they divide a few hours later. Long before that, within minutes to an hour, they switch on a set of genes called immediate early genes. A survey of 14,824 genes found 229 that behave this way.

The immediate early genes are switched on even when the drug cycloheximide blocks the making of new proteins. The proteins that switch these genes on must therefore already exist in the resting cell. Signals thus travel through proteins that are present before the growth factor arrives, and these proteins change their structure or their location when it arrives. A second group of genes, the delayed early genes, needs new proteins and comes on later.

The myc gene is one of the immediate early genes. Its RNA rises steeply when mitogens are added, and it falls quickly when they are removed. The Myc protein has a half-life of only 25 minutes. The amount of Myc in the cell therefore follows the amount of mitogen around the cell, with a delay of minutes. Myc is controlled by its amount, while Ras and Src are controlled by changes in their structure. These are the same two ways in which proto-oncogenes are activated in tumors (Chapter 4).

Genetics Found the Links

Biochemistry showed what single proteins, such as Ras or a receptor kinase, do on their own. It did not show which protein passes the signal to which. These links were found by the genetics of flies and worms. The eye of the fruit fly is made of many small units, each with seven light-sensing cells: six cells around a seventh in the center. In flies with a mutant gene called sevenless, the central cell does not form. The sevenless gene encodes a receptor tyrosine kinase, related to the human FGF receptor.

A search for genes needed after Sevenless in the chain found a gene named Son of sevenless (Sos). Its protein is a GEF for Ras, the kind of protein that switches Ras on (Chapter 5). Between the receptor and Sos, researchers found adaptor proteins, which have no enzyme activity and only link other proteins together. They are Shc, Grb2 and Crk. Crk is also the oncoprotein of an avian sarcoma virus. This pathway already existed more than 600 million years ago, in the common ancestor of all animals. It has changed so little that the fly proteins and the human proteins work in the same way.

Phosphotyrosines as Docking Sites

Besides its kinase, the Src protein contains two other parts, called SH2 and SH3 domains. An SH2 domain is about 100 amino acids long. It binds a phosphorylated tyrosine together with the 3 to 6 amino acids that follow it in the protein chain. An SH3 domain binds stretches rich in the amino acid proline. The human genome encodes at least 120 SH2 domains, and each one prefers a particular sequence.

A receptor tyrosine kinase phosphorylates the tyrosines on its own tail when it is activated (Chapter 5). Each phosphorylated tyrosine, together with the amino acids next to it, then becomes a docking site for the SH2 domains of particular proteins. For example, the activated PDGF receptor binds Src, PI3K, Ras-GAP, the phosphatase SHP-2 and the enzyme PLC-gamma. The activated EGF receptor binds a different set of proteins, and it therefore starts a different set of pathways.

Docking mostly changes where a protein is, not what it does. The tail of a receptor lies just under the cell membrane. The proteins that dock on it are therefore brought next to molecules that sit in the membrane. For example, Ras-GAP docked on a receptor is brought next to Ras, which is held in the membrane by its fatty tail. PI3K docked on a receptor is brought next to the membrane lipids that it modifies.

Docking can also switch a protein on. Src normally keeps itself inactive. Its own SH2 domain binds the phosphorylated tyrosine at position 527 of the Src protein, and this keeps the active site of the kinase blocked. When an activated receptor offers a better docking site, the SH2 domain binds the receptor instead, and the active site of Src opens.

From the Receptor to Ras

Grb2 is made of one SH2 domain and two SH3 domains, and it has no enzyme activity. Its SH2 domain binds a phosphorylated tyrosine on the tail of many activated receptors. Its SH3 domains bind two proline-rich stretches of Sos. The receptor thus brings Sos to the membrane, next to Ras. Sos then makes Ras release GDP and take up GTP. Shc, the other adaptor found in the genetic searches, also links activated receptors to this chain. The chain from growth factor to active Ras is therefore: growth factor, pair of receptors, phosphorylated tail, Grb2, Sos, Ras.

From Ras to the Nucleus: the MAP Kinase Cascade

Active Ras binds several proteins, called its effectors. They bind tightly to Ras when it holds GTP and hardly at all when it holds GDP. The first effector found was the kinase Raf. When Ras binds GTP, it binds Raf about 1,000 times more strongly. Raf then moves from the cytoplasm to the membrane, where it is activated. Raf phosphorylates and activates the kinase MEK, and MEK phosphorylates and activates the kinases ERK1 and ERK2. A chain of three kinases like this one is called a MAP kinase cascade. Such chains already exist in yeast. Scaffold proteins hold the three kinases together, so that each kinase finds its target at once.

Active ERK moves into the nucleus. There it phosphorylates transcription factors, which switch on genes such as those for the proteins Fos and Jun. A 2017 study counted 2,507 proteins with good evidence of being targets of ERK.

In about 50% of human melanomas, the kinase B-Raf, a close relative of Raf, carries an amino acid substitution. The substitution moves a loop out of the active site of B-Raf and raises its activity about 500 times. The cascade then runs without any signal from Ras. In about one-third of the other melanomas, N-Ras is mutated instead. In human tumors, a mutant ras gene is rarely found together with a mutant B-raf gene. Either one gives the cell what it needs from this pathway.

The PI3K Branch: Survival and Growth

The second effector of Ras is the enzyme phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K). It phosphorylates a lipid of the inner face of the cell membrane, turning PIP2 into PIP3. Proteins that have a PH domain bind PIP3 and are pulled to the membrane. One of them is the kinase Akt, also called PKB.

Akt acts on many proteins. It blocks Bad, a protein that promotes cell death (Chapter 9). It blocks GSK-3 beta, which would otherwise mark growth-promoting proteins such as beta-catenin, cyclin D1 and Myc for destruction. It activates mTOR, a kinase that increases protein synthesis and cell growth, and it increases the uptake of glucose. In mice whose pancreatic beta cells carry an Akt that is always active, these cells grow to more than twice the normal area in cross-section. That is almost 4 times the normal volume.

The phosphatase PTEN removes the phosphate that PI3K adds, and it turns PIP3 back into PIP2. PTEN therefore works against the whole branch. PTEN activity is lost in 30 to 40% of all human cancers. The loss can come from a mutation in its gene, from DNA methylation that silences the gene, or from a microRNA that blocks the making of the protein.

PIK3CA is the gene for the main enzyme part of PI3K. By 2020, activating mutations of PIK3CA had been found in about 10% of all human cancers, and in almost one-third of breast and endometrial cancers. PIK3CA is the second most often mutated oncogene, after K-RAS. In mice, removing the gene for this part of PI3K prevents an activated K-ras oncogene from causing pancreatic cancer.

The Third Branch and the Division of Labor

The third effector of Ras, Ral-GEF, activates two small GTPases, RalA and RalB. Through the proteins Sec5 and Exo84, Ral signaling helps cells transformed by ras to grow without attachment and to form tumors. Ral signaling also controls the delivery of vesicles to the cell surface and the movement of the cell. In a simplified picture, the Raf branch drives cell division, the PI3K branch drives cell survival, cell growth and glucose uptake, and the Ral branch acts on transport and movement. A mutant Ras protein switches on all three branches at once. This explains why one oncogene changes so many traits of a cell.

Anchorage Signals Enter the Same Pathways

Integrins that gather in focal adhesions activate focal adhesion kinase (FAK). FAK is a tyrosine kinase, but it is not a receptor. Phosphorylated FAK provides docking sites for SH2 proteins, including Src, PI3K and the Grb2-Sos pair that activates Ras. Attachment and growth factors therefore act through the same pathways. Normal cells need the signal from their integrins to keep Ras and Src partly active. A Ras oncoprotein supplies this signal without any attachment, and this is how it lets cells grow while floating.

Anoikis also shapes normal tissue. In the breast, small round clusters of epithelial cells form the glands. The cells in the middle of a cluster lose contact with the basement membrane and die by anoikis, which creates the central opening. When this death is blocked in experiments, for example by the protein Bcl-xL, the opening fills with living cells. The result looks like some early breast cancers.

Wnt and Beta-Catenin

Without a Wnt signal, a group of proteins that includes Apc, axin and the kinase GSK-3 beta phosphorylates beta-catenin. This marks beta-catenin for fast destruction. When Wnt binds its Frizzled receptor, GSK-3 beta is blocked. The half-life of beta-catenin then rises from less than 20 minutes to 1 to 2 hours, and its amount rises in proportion. Beta-catenin then enters the nucleus and binds transcription factors of the Tcf/Lef family. Together they switch on genes that promote division, such as the genes for cyclin D1 and Myc.

Mutant forms of beta-catenin that cannot be phosphorylated are found in carcinomas of the liver, colon, endometrium and ovary, and in melanomas. In almost all colon carcinomas, the destruction of beta-catenin fails because Apc is defective (Chapter 7).

Signals That Travel to the Nucleus

Several pathways carry a protein from the cytoplasm directly into the nucleus. In the TGF-beta pathway, the activated receptor phosphorylates Smad2 or Smad3. These proteins bind Smad4 and move into the nucleus. There they bind a DNA sequence four bases long and control genes together with other transcription factors.

The transcription factor NF-kappaB is held in the cytoplasm by a partner protein called I-kappaB. Signals such as those of inflammation activate a kinase that phosphorylates I-kappaB, which marks it for destruction. NF-kappaB is then free to enter the nucleus. There it switches on up to 500 target genes, including genes that block cell death.

The STAT proteins dock on activated cytokine receptors through their SH2 domains. They are then phosphorylated, pair up and move into the nucleus. STAT3 is always active in most breast cancers and in almost all cancers of the head and neck. The Hedgehog pathway is overactive in most basal cell carcinomas of the skin. Inherited mutations of PTCH, the gene for its receptor, cause Gorlin syndrome, in which people develop many basal cell carcinomas.

Feedback Switches Signals Off

Every pathway contains negative feedback that limits the strength and the length of its signal. Ras-GAP docks on activated receptors, next to Ras, and switches Ras off. Phosphatases remove the phosphates added by kinases. Active receptors are pulled into the cell by endocytosis and destroyed. Positive feedback also exists. Soon after a receptor is activated, the cell destroys its supply of NF1, a GAP for Ras, and this lets Ras switch on faster. A cancer cell can therefore gain signaling in two ways: from an overactive protein that stimulates the pathway, or from the loss of a protein that switches the pathway off.

Feedback also decides whether a drug works. Vemurafenib is a drug that blocks mutant B-Raf. It stops the division of melanoma cells that carry the B-Raf mutation. It does not stop colorectal cancer cells that carry the same mutation. In the colorectal cells, active ERK normally holds down the EGF receptor through a negative feedback loop. When the drug shuts down B-Raf, ERK shuts down too, and the feedback stops. The EGF receptor then fires strongly and drives division through another route: Ras, PI3K and Akt. Melanoma cells make little EGF receptor, so they have no such second route.

The Size of the Signaling Network

Signaling proteins are controlled in three ways. The first is a change in activity, such as binding GTP or gaining a phosphate. The second is a change in amount, as for Myc and beta-catenin. The third is a change in location, as for proteins that dock on a receptor or move into the nucleus. The human genome contains 518 genes for protein kinases, 90 of them for tyrosine kinases. It also contains more than 100 phosphatases that remove phosphate from tyrosine. About 200 million pairs of human proteins could in principle interact, and about 64,000 of these interactions had been confirmed by experiment by 2016.

Why particular mutations are found in particular tissues is still not understood. For example, 90% of pancreatic carcinomas carry a mutant K-ras gene, but only about 10% of breast cancers do.
