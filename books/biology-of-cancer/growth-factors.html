Chapter 5: Growth Factors and Their Receptors

Cells Divide When Other Cells Signal Them To

In a tissue, no cell decides alone whether to divide. The number and position of each type of cell must stay constant. A cell therefore divides only when its neighbors send it signals to divide, and it stops when they send signals to stop. Many of these signals are carried by growth factors. A growth factor is a small protein that one cell releases and that travels through the space between cells to other cells. Growth factors that make cells divide are called mitogens.

Normal cells in a dish show this dependence. A liquid medium that contains every nutrient, such as amino acids, vitamins, glucose and salts, is not enough to make them divide. They divide only when serum is added. Serum is the clear fluid left after blood clots. During clotting, the platelets of the blood release a mixture of growth factors that start the healing of a wound. Among them are platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), epidermal growth factor (EGF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), insulin-like growth factor (IGF) and fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF-2).

PDGF attracts fibroblasts into a wound and makes them divide. EGF makes epithelial cells divide. Without PDGF, fibroblasts in a dish stay alive and active for weeks, but they do not divide. Many oncoproteins act on the proteins that detect growth factors. The cell then divides as if growth factors were present, even when there are none.

Src Is a Tyrosine Kinase

The v-src gene encodes a protein of 533 amino acids, with a mass of about 60 kilodaltons. In 1977 and 1978, researchers found that Src adds phosphate groups to other proteins. They used antibodies to catch the Src protein. When the antibodies were mixed with Src and ATP, the molecule that supplies phosphate in the cell, the antibodies themselves gained phosphate groups. Src is therefore a protein kinase: an enzyme that moves a phosphate group from ATP onto a protein. The phosphate changes how that protein works.

More than 50 proteins are now known to be phosphorylated by Src. Each of them then changes other proteins in turn. This explains how one oncoprotein can change many traits of a cell at once. Mutant Src proteins that can no longer phosphorylate other proteins also lose the ability to transform cells.

The kinases known before Src put phosphate on the amino acids serine and threonine. Src puts it on tyrosine. In normal cells, more than 99% of phosphorylated amino acids are serine or threonine, and phosphorylated tyrosine makes up only 0.05 to 0.1%. In cells transformed by v-src, it rises to as much as 1%. In cells transformed by H-ras, it hardly changes. Later work showed that tyrosine phosphorylation is used mainly by the signaling pathways that make cells divide. The kinases that control thousands of other processes act almost only on serine and threonine.

Growth Factor Receptors Are Tyrosine Kinases

EGF was the first growth factor found. It was discovered because it makes newborn mice open their eyes early, and it was later found to make many kinds of epithelial cells divide. EGF binds only to the cells that respond to it. This pointed to a receptor on the cell surface: a protein that recognizes EGF outside the cell and passes the information inside.

The EGF receptor was purified from a human carcinoma cell line that makes up to 100 times the normal amount of it. Its sequence has three parts. The ectodomain, 621 amino acids long, lies outside the cell and binds EGF. The transmembrane domain, 23 amino acids long, crosses the cell membrane. The cytoplasmic domain, 542 amino acids long, lies inside the cell.

Part of the cytoplasmic domain has a sequence similar to that of Src. Proteins with similar sequences usually have similar functions and a common ancestor. Researchers therefore concluded that the receptor works like Src. When the outer part binds EGF, the kinase in the inner part becomes active. It phosphorylates tyrosines on proteins in the cytoplasm, and this leads the cell to divide.

Many other receptors are built on the same plan, each with its own growth factor. They are called receptor tyrosine kinases. Examples are the PDGF receptor, found on fibroblasts and other connective tissue cells, and the VEGF receptor, found on the cells that line blood vessels. Another is Kit, the receptor for stem cell factor, found on cells that make blood. At least 58 of the roughly 20,000 human genes that code for proteins encode receptors of this type. Almost no single-celled organism has such receptors. They appeared shortly before the first animals.

A Receptor Without Its Outer Part

In 1984, the sequence of the EGF receptor was found to be closely related to that of the protein made by erbB, the oncogene of avian erythroblastosis virus. The viral protein, v-ErbB, is the chicken EGF receptor with most of its ectodomain missing. It cannot bind EGF, yet it makes cells divide. Without its ectodomain, the receptor sends signals into the cell all the time, whether EGF is present or not. Shortened EGF receptors of this kind were later found in one-third of human glioblastomas. Shortened forms of HER2, a close relative of the EGF receptor, were found in breast cancers with a very poor outlook.

This explains a long-known trait of cancer cells: in a dish, they need far less growth factor than normal cells. A cell that carries v-ErbB receives from it, all the time, the same signals that EGF would send through a normal receptor. The cell therefore divides whether or not growth factors are present. Other receptor tyrosine kinases are altered in human tumors in the same way. HER2 is overexpressed in about 20% of stomach carcinomas, and in 15 to 30% of breast cancers depending on the estimate. Kit carries amino acid substitutions in gastrointestinal stromal tumors, and the EGF receptor is overexpressed in many carcinomas.

A Cell That Makes Its Own Growth Factor

In 1983, the B chain of PDGF was found to be closely related to the protein made by v-sis, the oncogene of simian sarcoma virus. A cell infected by this virus releases large amounts of a protein similar to PDGF. This protein binds the PDGF receptors of the same cell and activates them without pause.

PDGF normally acts on connective tissue cells, such as fibroblasts, fat cells and smooth muscle cells, which carry the PDGF receptor. Epithelial cells mostly carry the EGF receptor instead. This explains why simian sarcoma virus transforms fibroblasts but not epithelial cells. Only cells that carry the PDGF receptor respond to the growth factor that the virus makes them release.

A signal sent from one cell to a nearby cell of another type is called paracrine. A signal sent through the blood to distant tissues is called endocrine. Normal tissues avoid loops in which a cell stimulates itself. Epithelial cells make PDGF for the fibroblasts beneath them. Fibroblasts make TGF-alpha, a relative of EGF, for the epithelial cells. A cell usually does not carry the receptor for the growth factor it releases.

A cell that makes a growth factor and also carries its receptor stimulates itself. This is called an autocrine loop. Many human tumors form such loops. Some lung cancers even make three growth factors, TGF-alpha, stem cell factor and IGF-1, and carry the receptors for all three.

A Ligand Joins Two Receptors

The molecule that a receptor binds is called its ligand. Here the ligand is the growth factor. Seconds after EGF reaches cells, the protein with the most phosphorylated tyrosines is often the EGF receptor itself. Two facts explain this. Many growth factors are made of two parts, and receptors of this kind move freely sideways in the membrane. A ligand therefore binds two receptors and holds them together as a pair, called a dimer. The kinase of each receptor then phosphorylates tyrosines on the tail of the other receptor. This is called transphosphorylation.

The phosphorylation of one particular tyrosine often moves a loop of the kinase out of its active site, which switches the kinase fully on. The phosphorylated tyrosines on the tail become binding sites for other proteins. Through these proteins, the receptor starts the signaling pathways of Chapter 6.

Receptors can also pair with related receptors. HER2 has no known ligand of its own. It pairs with the EGF receptor or with HER3. The kinase of HER3 does not work, but the kinase of HER2 can phosphorylate the tail of HER3. The signal is later switched off in two ways. First, phosphatases, enzymes that remove phosphate groups, undo the work of the kinases. Humans have 103 phosphatases that act on tyrosine. Second, active receptors are pulled into the cell by endocytosis, and many of them are then destroyed.

Receptors That Fire Without Ligand

Pairing explains why too many copies of a normal receptor can make a cell divide. Receptors present in excess meet one another by chance as they move in the membrane. These chance pairs fire like pairs held by a ligand. In one experiment, the human Met receptor was overexpressed in the liver of mice. This receptor cannot bind the mouse form of its ligand. Yet the mice developed liver carcinomas, so the receptors must have fired through chance pairing. In a survey of 206 glioblastomas, the region that carries the EGF receptor gene was amplified in about 90. Most of the amplified genes encoded a normal receptor.

Extra receptors also make a cell respond to very small amounts of growth factor. Cells can collect extra receptors when the removal of receptors from the surface fails. The protein HIP1 is overexpressed in many human carcinomas. Cells that make too much HIP1 keep extra EGF receptors on their surface. They grow in 0.1% serum, a concentration at which normal cells of the same line stop dividing. The protein GAK helps to remove EGF receptors from the surface. When GAK is blocked, the number of EGF receptors on the surface rises as much as fiftyfold.

In other tumors, a receptor is fused to a protein that pairs with itself. The fused protein holds two receptor kinases together all the time. An example is the fusion of a protein called FIG with the receptor ROS, found in a glioblastoma.

Mutant receptor genes can also be inherited. The gene ret encodes a receptor tyrosine kinase. Inherited mutant copies of ret cause the family cancer syndromes called multiple endocrine neoplasia types 2A and 2B. Inherited mutant copies of met cause hereditary papillary kidney cancer, through receptors that fire without ligand. A Japanese family has passed a mutant Kit receptor through at least four generations, and the family members who carry it develop gastrointestinal stromal tumors.

Such families are rare. An allele that makes an oncoprotein active all the time acts in every cell of the embryo, and it usually disrupts development. Such alleles are therefore rarely passed on. Most inherited cancer syndromes are instead caused by inactive copies of tumor suppressor genes (Chapter 7) or of DNA repair genes (Chapter 12).

Other Receptor Families

Receptor tyrosine kinases are only one family of receptors. Receptors for cytokines, such as erythropoietin, which controls the making of red blood cells, have no kinase of their own. They use separate kinases, called JAKs, attached to their inner part. The receptors for TGF-beta have kinases that phosphorylate serine and threonine. TGF-beta stops the division of normal epithelial cells. But it helps cells that are already transformed to become more malignant (Chapters 8 and 14).

The receptors called Frizzled bind proteins of the Wnt family, and humans make at least 19 Wnt proteins. Without Wnt, the enzyme GSK-3 beta phosphorylates the protein beta-catenin, which marks it for destruction. When Wnt binds its receptor, GSK-3 beta is blocked. Beta-catenin then accumulates and promotes cell division. Notch receptors are activated by ligands on the surface of a neighboring cell. Mutant Notch receptors that fire without ligand are found in half of adult T-cell leukemias. The largest family of receptors, the G-protein-coupled receptors, has more than 800 genes, about 4% of all genes. About 400 of them serve the sense of smell.

Another family of receptors sits inside the cell. These nuclear receptors bind small fat-soluble molecules that cross the membrane, such as the steroid hormones estrogen, progesterone and androgens. The receptor then acts as a transcription factor, a protein that switches genes on or off. Nuclear receptors drive the growth of many carcinomas of the breast, ovary and prostate. The drug tamoxifen binds the estrogen receptor and changes its shape. The receptor then attracts proteins that switch genes off instead of proteins that switch them on. This is why tamoxifen is used to block estrogen signaling in breast cancer.

Anchorage: Integrins

Normal cells also need to be attached in order to divide. Many kinds of cells die when they are detached, by a form of cell suicide called anoikis (Chapter 9). Cells attach to the extracellular matrix, the network of proteins between cells, which includes collagens, laminins and fibronectin. They sense this attachment through receptors called integrins.

Each integrin is a pair made of one alpha and one beta subunit. Humans have at least 18 alpha and 8 beta subunits. They could form 144 pairs, but only 24 pairs are known. Each pair binds particular proteins of the matrix. For example, the pair alpha5 beta1 is the main receptor for fibronectin. Integrins bound to the matrix gather into clusters called focal adhesions. These clusters also anchor the actin fibers that give the cell its shape. Integrins send signals that prevent anoikis.

Integrins matter for tumors too. In one experiment, an oncogene was expressed in the breast tissue of mice whose breast epithelial cells lacked beta1 integrin. These mice developed more than 75% fewer early lesions, and none of the lesions became carcinomas. A normal cell thus checks two conditions before it divides: enough growth factors around it and enough attachment to the matrix. A ras oncogene removes both requirements. Cells transformed by ras grow in low amounts of serum, and many types of them grow without attachment.

Ras, a Switch That Turns Itself Off

The three ras genes make four Ras proteins, because K-ras makes two forms by alternative splicing. The four proteins are nearly identical and weigh about 21 kilodaltons. Each carries a fatty tail that anchors it to the inner face of the cell membrane.

Ras binds either of two small molecules, GDP or GTP. Bound to GDP, Ras is inactive. When a signal arrives from upstream, a protein called a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) makes Ras release its GDP and take up GTP. Bound to GTP, Ras changes shape and sends signals to other proteins. After a short time, Ras cuts the GTP into GDP and phosphate. Ras does this with its own enzyme activity, called a GTPase activity. It then returns to the inactive state.

Purified Ras cuts GTP very slowly. In cells, proteins called GAPs (GTPase-activating proteins) bind Ras when it holds GTP. They speed up the cutting as much as 100,000 times. How long Ras stays on therefore depends on how soon it meets a GAP. The Ras oncoprotein of Harvey sarcoma virus still binds GTP, but it has lost almost all of its GTPase activity. Once a GEF has switched it on, it cannot switch itself off. Instead of short pulses of signal, the cell receives a signal that lasts a long time, perhaps without end. The loss of NF1, one of the two well-studied GAPs for Ras, has the same effect on normal Ras, and it causes tumors of nerve tissue (Chapter 7).

A simple model shows the effect. In the model, a signal switches on GEFs for one unit of time. During the signal, GEFs load GTP onto inactive Ras 3 times as fast as GAP-assisted cutting removes it from active Ras. After the signal, only the cutting continues. For the mutant, the cutting is taken to be 100,000 times slower, the factor by which GAPs speed up normal Ras. By the end of the signal, 74% of normal Ras is active, and it falls back to near zero within a few units of time. For mutant Ras, 95% is active, and it stays there.

The structure of Ras explains why tumors carry mutations only at codons 12, 13 and 61 (Chapter 4). The amino acids at these positions line the pocket where GTP is cut. Almost any substitution there blocks the cutting triggered by GAPs, while the signaling part of Ras still works. Deletions and most other point mutations would destroy the function of the protein instead of increasing it. This is why the ras mutations in tumors are always substitutions of one amino acid, and never mutations that end the protein early. Mutations at other positions happen just as often, but they give the cell no growth advantage, so they are not found in tumors.

Ras Acts Downstream of Receptors

Src, ErbB and Ras work in different ways, but they have similar effects on cells. All three make cells rounder, remove contact inhibition, allow growth without attachment and reduce the need for growth factors. This suggested that they act in the same signaling chain. Cells transformed by ras release growth factors such as TGF-alpha, which could act back on their own EGF receptors. Ras might then act before the receptor in the chain. But ras also transforms cells that lack the receptors for these growth factors, so this cannot be the main explanation. Instead, EGF activates Ras near the cell surface within 5 minutes. Ras therefore acts after the receptor and passes its signal on.

In breast cancer cells, overexpression of HER2 changes many traits at once. The cells copy their DNA faster, grow better without attachment, form tumors more easily, spread more often and depend less on estrogen. Several separate pathways must therefore start from one receptor. Biochemistry could show what each protein does on its own, but not how the proteins pass signals to one another. These links were found by genetics in flies, worms and yeast, whose signaling pathways have changed little over hundreds of millions of years. Chapter 6 describes them.
