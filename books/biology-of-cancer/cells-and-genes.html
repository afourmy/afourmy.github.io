Chapter 1: Cells, Genes and Mutations

Genes and Proteins

A human cell stores its instructions in DNA. DNA is a long chain made of four kinds of units, called bases and written A, C, G and T. Two chains wind around each other to form a double helix. The bases pair across the two chains, A with T and C with G, so each chain determines the other. One full copy of the DNA sequence is called the genome. The human genome is about 3.2 billion bases long and is divided into separate molecules called chromosomes.

A gene is a stretch of DNA that the cell copies into RNA. For most genes, the RNA is then used to make a protein. Each group of three bases, called a codon, specifies one amino acid of the protein chain. The genome contains about 20,000 genes that code for proteins. The parts of the DNA that code for proteins make up only about 1.5% of the genome, and about 2% more controls when genes are used.

Proteins do almost all the work of the cell. They speed up chemical reactions, build the structures of the cell, carry signals and switch genes on and off. A mutation is any change in the DNA sequence. A change of a single base is called a point mutation. It changes at most one codon, and so at most one amino acid. Its effect depends on where it falls: most of the genome codes for no protein, and many changes of an amino acid do not stop a protein from working. Larger mutations delete or duplicate whole genes, or join pieces of two chromosomes. The cancer genes described in later chapters are a few hundred genes whose proteins decide when a cell divides and when it dies.

Two Copies of Every Gene

Human cells are diploid: they carry two versions of the genome, one inherited from each parent. Every gene is therefore present in two copies, except the genes of the sex chromosomes in males. The two copies of a gene can differ. The different versions of a gene are called alleles. A cell with two identical alleles of a gene is homozygous for that gene, and a cell with two different alleles is heterozygous. Often one working allele is enough for normal function. A defective allele is then called recessive: its effect appears only when both copies are defective. An allele is called dominant when a single copy changes the cell, even in the presence of a normal copy.

Many genes that stop cells from multiplying out of control are protected by having two copies. Almost all such genes are present in two copies, and both copies must be inactivated before a cell escapes their control. Losing one copy leaves the cell normal. Losing the second copy is the step that changes the cell. This explains why some cancers run in families (Chapter 7). A child who inherits one defective copy from a parent already carries the first loss in every cell of the body. Any one of these cells then needs only one more event.

Mutations and Clones

Copying 3.2 billion bases before every cell division is accurate but not perfect. In normal dividing human cells, fewer than one base per genome is copied wrongly at each division, an error rate below about one in 3 billion. The adult body contains about $3\times10^{13}$ cells. Worn-out cells are constantly replaced, so as many as $10^{16}$ cells are formed over a lifetime. With up to one new mutation per division, these divisions produce up to $10^{16}$ mutations in total. They are spread over the $6.4\times10^{9}$ bases of the two copies of the genome, so every base is hit up to about $1.6\times10^{6}$ times. A given point mutation can therefore arise many times in one body, in different cells.

The effect of a mutation depends on the cell in which it occurs. A germ-line mutation is present in a sperm or an egg, or in the fertilized egg itself. Every cell of the child that develops from it carries the mutation, including the child's own sperm or eggs, so the mutation is inherited. A somatic mutation occurs in one cell of the body after development has begun. It is passed to all the descendants of that cell and to no other cell, and it is not passed on to children. The descendants of a single cell form a clone, so a somatic mutation marks one clone of the body.

A tumor is such a clone. All the cells of a tumor descend from one ancestor (Chapter 2). The mutations of this ancestor are therefore present in every tumor cell and absent from the normal cells around the tumor. Each division is a new chance for an error, so the number of divisions a tissue makes matters for its cancer risk (Chapter 11). Tissues that renew themselves all the time, such as the lining of the gut, the skin and the blood, produce new cells at a high rate. The brain, by contrast, has little turnover.

Gene Expression

All the cells of a body carry the same genome. A liver cell and a skin cell still look and behave differently, because they use different sets of genes. Using a gene is called expressing it: the cell copies the gene into RNA and makes its protein. An average specialized cell expresses about 10,000 proteins at a time. Some genes, called housekeeping genes, are expressed in almost every cell, because every cell needs their proteins. Other genes are expressed in only one cell type. The set of expressed genes defines a cell type. A cell that loses part of this set, as cancer cells often do, loses part of its specialization.

Transcription factors are proteins that decide which genes are expressed. Each one binds a short DNA sequence of five to ten bases in the control region of a gene. This region is either the promoter, at the start of the gene, or a more distant enhancer. The transcription factor then increases or decreases the copying of the gene into RNA. About 1,639 human genes, roughly 8% of the protein-coding genes, encode transcription factors. A short sequence occurs near many genes, so one transcription factor can control many genes at once. The Myc protein of Chapter 8, for example, acts on thousands of genes. A single altered transcription factor can therefore change the behavior of a cell in many ways at the same time.

Proteins are also switched on and off after they are made. The most common switch is phosphorylation. An enzyme called a kinase attaches a phosphate group to a serine, threonine or tyrosine in a target protein. This changes the shape and the activity of that protein. Other enzymes, called phosphatases, remove the phosphate again. The signals that tell a cell to divide are passed from protein to protein by kinases, and many cancer genes encode kinases that stay switched on (Chapters 5 and 6).

Silencing Genes without Mutation

A cell can also pass the state of its genes to its descendants without any change in the DNA sequence. This is called epigenetic inheritance. The best understood mechanism is DNA methylation. A methyl group can be attached to a C that is followed by a G, a pair written CpG. When many CpGs near the promoter of a gene are methylated, the gene is silenced. C pairs with G, so a CpG on one strand faces a CpG on the other strand. Both C bases of the pair can therefore carry a methyl group.

The pattern survives cell division because of the way DNA is copied. Each daughter DNA molecule keeps one old strand and receives one new strand. The new strand is made without methyl groups. An enzyme called a maintenance methyltransferase finds each CpG that is methylated on the old strand but not on the new one, and it methylates the new strand to match. Both daughter cells therefore inherit the same silenced genes. Cancer cells use this mechanism to switch off genes that restrain growth (Chapter 7). The effect is the same as a mutation that destroys these genes.

Chromosomes of Cancer Cells

The first sign that cancer cells have abnormal genomes came from the microscope. Abnormal chromosomes in cancer cells were described as early as 1892. Perhaps 90% of human solid tumors, the cancers that form a mass rather than circulating in the blood, are aneuploid. This means that their cells carry a wrong number of chromosomes. A piece of a chromosome can also be exchanged with another chromosome in a translocation, turned around in an inversion, or lost in a deletion. A region can also be copied many times, which is called gene amplification. The copies appear either as a long stretch of repeats inside a chromosome or as many small separate pieces of DNA called double minutes.

Most of these changes differ from one tumor to the next. Some, however, occur at the same place in many independent tumors of the same kind. Such a recurring change points to a gene whose alteration helps the cancer cell. A recurring translocation marks a gene that the exchange activates or disrupts. A recurring amplification marks a gene whose protein helps the cell when it is made in large amounts. A recurring deletion marks a gene whose loss helps the cell. Chapter 4 describes how these clues led to the first human cancer genes.
