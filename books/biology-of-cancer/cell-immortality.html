Chapter 10: Cell Immortality and Telomeres

Normal Cells Divide a Limited Number of Times

In the 1960s, Leonard Hayflick showed that human fibroblasts grown in a dish divide well for about 60 population doublings and then stop. They enter a state called replicative senescence. In this state they stay alive, for as long as a year, but they no longer divide. Cancer cells in a dish have no such limit. For example, HeLa cells came in 1951 from an aggressive cervical adenocarcinoma of a patient named Henrietta Lacks, and they have been dividing ever since. A cell that can divide without limit is called immortalized, and nearly all cancer cells are immortalized.

Senescence depends on the pRb and p53 pathways. The inhibitors p16INK4A and p21Cip1 rise as cells approach senescence. The large T antigen of SV40 binds both pRb and p53, and it lets cells continue past this point. Cells that get past senescence in this way divide for a while longer. They then enter crisis: most of them die, and the survivors have chromosomes in disorder. Crisis is caused by the ends of the chromosomes, and escaping it requires a different change.

Why a Limit on Divisions Matters for Tumors

One cubic centimeter of tumor contains about $10^9$ cells. A tumor that threatens life measures about $10^3$ cubic centimeters, so it contains about $10^{12} \approx 2^{40}$ cells. If every cell divided and none died, 40 generations would be enough. That is fewer than the 50 or 60 generations allowed to normal human cells. In fact, 60 generations of doubling would make $10^{15}$ to $10^{18}$ cells, which would weigh around $10^6$ kilograms.

The limit matters because many tumor cells die in every generation. They die by apoptosis, by senescence, or from a lack of oxygen and growth factors. Suppose a fraction $d$ of the cells is lost in each generation. The population then grows by a factor of $2(1-d)$ per generation, and it takes $\ln 10^{12} / \ln(2(1-d))$ generations to reach $10^{12}$ cells.

Telomeres Shorten at Every Division

The ends of human chromosomes are called telomeres. They consist of the six-base sequence TTAGGG repeated several thousand times. In normal human cells, telomeres are 5 to 15 kilobases long, usually 5 to 10. One strand is longer than the other and sticks out at the end as a single strand several hundred bases long. With the help of proteins, the end is folded into a loop, which hides it from the cell's repair enzymes.

Telomeres get shorter each time the DNA is copied. One of the two new strands is made in short pieces, and each piece starts from a short RNA primer. At the very end of a chromosome, the last primer cannot be replaced by DNA, so the end is not fully copied. Enzymes also trim the ends. As a result, telomeres lose 50 to 100 bases in each cell generation.

The length of the telomeres therefore counts how many divisions a cell lineage has gone through. Human lymphocytes grown in a dish entered crisis when their telomeric DNA fell below about 3 kilobases. The cell treats a chromosome end without a telomere like a broken piece of DNA. In 1941, Barbara McClintock found that chromosomes of maize that had lost their telomeres fuse end to end with one another. A fused chromosome with two centromeres is pulled toward both ends of the cell when the cell divides, and it breaks. This starts a cycle of breaks and fusions.

Breakage-Fusion-Bridge Cycles

After a chromosome is copied, the two new copies, called sister chromatids, have ends of the same length. If both ends have lost their telomeres, they can fuse. The result is one chromosome with two centromeres. When the cell divides, the two centromeres are pulled toward opposite ends of the cell. The chromosome is stretched across the dividing cell like a bridge, and it breaks at a random point.

One daughter cell then receives a chromosome with an extra copy of a segment. The other daughter cell receives a chromosome that has lost a segment. Both new ends lack telomeres, so the process repeats at the next division. These cycles scramble the chromosomes and usually kill the cells. This is what happens in crisis.

Telomerase

Telomerase is the enzyme that lengthens telomeres. It has two parts: a reverse transcriptase called hTERT, and an RNA called hTR. The RNA carries a template, and hTERT copies it to add TTAGGG units to the end of the chromosome. Telomerase activity is found in 85 to 90% of human tumor samples. It is very low in most normal human cells, which make hTR but not hTERT.

In many tumors, mutations in the promoter of the hTERT gene switch the gene on. There are two such mutations, at 228 and at 250 bases before the start of the gene, and a tumor carries one or the other. Each creates a binding site for a transcription factor. They are found in 83% of primary glioblastomas, 66% of bladder carcinomas and 71% of melanomas. Myc also switches on hTERT.

Adding hTERT to human cells that would otherwise enter crisis makes their telomeres longer, and the cells then divide without limit. The other 10 to 15% of tumors keep their telomeres without telomerase. They copy telomere DNA from one chromosome end to another by recombination, a mechanism called ALT (alternative lengthening of telomeres). ALT is found in perhaps half of osteosarcomas and soft-tissue sarcomas and in a quarter of glioblastomas, but rarely in carcinomas. A drug that blocks telomerase would therefore favor the tumor cells that use ALT.

Short Telomeres Can Prevent or Promote Cancer

Laboratory mice do not strongly repress their telomerase. Their telomeric DNA is 30 to 40 kilobases long, about five times as long as in humans. This is why mouse cells easily become immortalized in a dish, while human cells need both large T and hTERT. Mice without the telomerase RNA are normal for several generations. Each generation starts life with shorter telomeres. By the fifth and sixth generations, the mice heal wounds poorly and have fewer offspring. When p53 works normally, short telomeres protect against cancer, because cells with very short telomeres stop dividing or die.

When p53 has been lost, short telomeres have the opposite effect. Cells with very short telomeres then survive the breakage-fusion-bridge cycles, and they accumulate large gains and losses of chromosome segments. Mice that lack both telomerase and p53 develop more carcinomas, the most common type of tumor in humans. Later, a tumor cell may switch on telomerase or ALT. This stops the cycles and fixes the scrambled set of chromosomes that the cell has reached. This sequence explains why many human carcinomas carry heavily rearranged chromosomes, the subject of Chapter 12.
