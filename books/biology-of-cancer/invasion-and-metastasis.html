Chapter 14: Invasion and Metastasis

Metastases Cause Most Cancer Deaths

Primary tumors cause about 10% of deaths from cancer. The other 90% are caused by metastases, which are the tumors that cancer cells form at sites far from the primary tumor. A cubic centimeter of tissue holds as many as $10^9$ cells. A tumor often reaches $10^{10}$ or $10^{11}$ cells before anyone notices it, so some of its cells may already have left.

Researchers have looked for mutations that are present in metastases and absent in the matching primary tumor. These searches have consistently failed. The ability to invade and to spread therefore appears to come from programs that cells switch on and off, not from new mutations.

The Steps From a Tumor to a Metastasis

More than 80% of life-threatening cancers are carcinomas. A carcinoma cell has to pass through a series of steps to form a metastasis. It first invades the tissue around it by crossing the basement membrane. It then enters a blood or lymph vessel, a step called intravasation, and travels in the circulation. It is caught in a small vessel at a distant site and leaves it, a step called extravasation. It then forms a small group of cells, a micrometastasis. In rare cases the group grows into a large metastasis, a step called colonization.

Cells Switch to a Mobile State

Epithelial cells are joined to their neighbors by the adhesion protein E-cadherin. They are polarized and sit on a basement membrane. Mesenchymal cells, such as fibroblasts, live as separate cells and can move. In an epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), an epithelial cell stops making E-cadherin and cytokeratins. It starts making vimentin, N-cadherin and fibronectin, which are typical of mesenchymal cells. It becomes able to move and to invade, and it resists apoptosis.

The EMT is a normal program of the embryo. During gastrulation, cells leave their epithelial layer by an EMT and move inside the embryo to form its middle layer. The EMT also runs during wound healing (Chapter 13). Carcinoma cells reuse this program.

The loss of E-cadherin is the central change. One study examined 26 human breast cancer cell lines. In 8 of them, mutations had stopped the expression of E-cadherin. In 5 the mutations truncated the protein, and in 3 a deletion altered it. In other carcinomas, the E-cadherin gene is silenced by methylation of its promoter or by transcription factors that repress it.

A small set of transcription factors runs the EMT. They are the same ones that run it in the embryo, and they include Snail, Slug, Twist, Zeb1, Zeb2, Goosecoid and FOXC2. Snail, Slug, Zeb1 and Zeb2 all bind the promoter of the E-cadherin gene and repress it. They also switch on one another. Twist, for example, binds the promoters of the genes for Twist, Snail, Slug and Zeb1.

Signals From the Stroma Start the Program

Cancer cells undergo an EMT mostly at the edge of a tumor, where they touch the stroma. Signals from the stroma start the program. The best studied is TGF-β. In experiments in the mid-1990s, mouse breast epithelial cells carrying a ras oncogene were exposed to TGF-β. They lost their cytokeratins and their E-cadherin, made vimentin, and took the shape of fibroblasts. Other signals that start an EMT include Wnts, EGF, HGF, TNF-α and a stiff collagen matrix.

The program can then maintain itself. Cells that have gone through an EMT often make their own TGF-β and Wnts, which keep them in the mesenchymal state. A second mechanism works inside the cell. Small RNAs of the miR-200 family block the messenger RNAs of Zeb1 and Zeb2. Zeb1 and Zeb2 in turn bind the genes of the miR-200 family and switch them off. Whichever side is stronger silences the other and holds the cell in one state. Most of the 60 cancer cell lines of the NCI-60 collection are therefore clearly epithelial or clearly mesenchymal.

The Program Also Confers Stem Cell Traits

The EMT changes how many cells can start a new tumor. In mouse breast tumors, 1 cell in 143 with a high level of Snail could start a tumor. Among cells with a low level of Snail, only 1 in 46,014 could. Normal breast cells behave the same way. Ten thousand normal mouse breast epithelial cells could not rebuild a mammary gland. As few as 100 cells that had briefly made Slug and a second factor, Sox9, could rebuild one.

Cells in a partial EMT, with both epithelial and mesenchymal traits, appear to be the best at starting tumors. This links invasion to the cancer stem cells of Chapter 11. The cells that found metastases must also be able to act as cancer stem cells.

The Program Is Needed, and Then Reversed

Blocking the EMT blocks metastasis. Mouse breast cancer cells were given a short RNA that suppresses Twist. Their primary tumors grew as before, but the number of lung metastases fell by about 85%. The few metastases that did form came from cells in which Twist had not been suppressed. The same experiment with Slug in melanoma cells cut metastases by about 90%.

The program must also be switched off at the destination. Mouse breast cancer cells were injected into the blood and followed in the lungs. Cancer cells were 0.1% of the cells of the lung at the start and 0.03% a week later. Among those cancer cells, the fraction in the mesenchymal state rose from about 1.5% to more than 20% in the first week. As the metastases then grew, that fraction fell back to about 1.8%. Cells in which the EMT could not be switched off failed to form growing metastases.

The stroma of the primary tumor releases the signals that keep the EMT running. A cell that arrives in a normal tissue no longer receives them. It returns to the epithelial state by an MET, and its descendants rebuild an epithelial growth.

Crossing the Basement Membrane

The basement membrane has pores as small as 40 nm. A cell can squeeze its cytoplasm through a narrow opening, but its nucleus is stiff and measures 3 to 10 micrometers, about 100 times more. A carcinoma cell must therefore cut the basement membrane to pass through it. The enzymes that cut it are the matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) of Chapter 13. Most of them come from stromal cells rather than from the cancer cells.

One MMP stays attached to the surface of the cancer cell, MT1-MMP. It is concentrated at small protrusions of the cell surface called invadopodia, so the cell cuts the matrix only where it pushes against it. MT1-MMP cuts collagen IV, which gives the basement membrane its rigidity, and it activates other MMPs.

Drugs that block MMPs were tested in patients. The trials were stopped because the drugs also blocked the normal remodeling of cartilage and other tissues, which caused stiff and painful joints.

Moving is a separate ability. The strongest inducer of motility in epithelial cells is HGF, which is also called scatter factor because it makes cells in a dish move apart in all directions. Epithelial cells carry Met, the receptor for HGF, and they start to move when they receive it.

Macrophages Help Cells Leave the Tumor

Macrophages are needed for invasion. Mice with breast tumors that could not make CSF-1 recruited almost no macrophages. Their primary tumors grew normally, but the tumors stayed enclosed and formed few lung metastases.

Cancer cells enter blood vessels at particular spots, where a cancer cell, a macrophage and an endothelial cell touch one another. The density of these triads predicts the outcome. One study looked at women whose breast tumors carry the estrogen receptor and not HER2. Such tumors are about 60% of all breast tumors. The third of the women with the highest density of triads had 2.7 times the risk of distant metastases of the third with the lowest density. This measure predicted the risk better than the grade of the tumor.

Travel Through the Circulation

A cancer cell in the blood is called a circulating tumor cell. Most capillaries have an internal diameter of 3 to 8 micrometers. Red blood cells are about 7 micrometers across and bend easily, so they pass through. Cancer cells are more than 20 micrometers across and do not bend, so they are caught almost at once.

Cancer cells leave the circulation quickly. In one experiment, more than 98% of injected cells had left the blood of a mouse within 10 minutes. In humans the half-life is longer, about 1 to 2 hours, because one circuit of the human circulation takes about 1 minute against about 4 seconds in a mouse. Shear forces tear cells apart, and natural killer cells attack them. Platelets that stick to a cancer cell shield it from these cells and release growth factors.

Where a cell is caught depends on the layout of the circulation. Blood from most organs returns to the heart and is pumped to the lungs, so cells from most tumors are caught in the lungs first. The veins of the gut, the pancreas and the spleen empty into the liver through the portal vein. Cells from colon and pancreatic tumors are therefore caught in the liver within seconds.

Cells also travel in the lymph. More than 30% of breast cancer patients have cancer cells in the lymph nodes that drain the breast at the time of diagnosis. In one study, 90% of the patients who survived a long time had one, two or occasionally three positive nodes. Fewer than 5% of the patients with more than five positive nodes stayed free of disease for a long time. The nodes do not cause the spread. They are a measure of how far the spread has already gone.

Few Cells Ever Found a Metastasis

Cancer cells reach distant organs in large numbers. The portal blood of 18 patients with pancreatic carcinoma held about 15 circulating tumor cells per milliliter. This amounts to about $3 \times 10^7$ cancer cells entering the liver every day. These patients developed only a handful of liver metastases over months.

Estimates in breast cancer give the same picture. Of the cancer cells that reach the bone marrow, about 1 in 1000 forms a micrometastasis. Of the micrometastases, about 1 in 1000 grows into a metastasis large enough to be seen. The steps that move cells to a distant organ work reasonably well. The step that makes them grow there almost always fails.

Each Organ Demands Its Own Adaptation

In 1889, Stephen Paget studied the records of 735 breast cancer patients. The organs in which they developed metastases could not be explained by chance alone or by the layout of the circulation alone. He proposed that a cancer cell, the seed, grows only in a tissue that suits it, the soil.

Colonizing one organ does not help a cell colonize another. Of the young patients with papillary thyroid carcinoma, 75% have metastases in lymph nodes, yet only 3% ever develop metastases elsewhere. Duodenal carcinoid tumors larger than 1 cm, which hold more than $10^9$ cells, often reach the lymph nodes but rarely the liver. Patients with abdominal fluid are sometimes treated with a shunt that drains the fluid into a vein. This puts billions of cancer cells into the circulation, and few metastases follow.

Cells That Wait

Cancer cells can sit in a distant organ for years without growing. Labeled breast cancer cells placed in the liver of mice stayed there as single cells for 11 weeks without dividing. They were still able to form tumors when taken out and injected elsewhere. They also survived a chemotherapy that shrank growing metastases in the same mice by 75%.

These cells are common in patients. About 1% of people without cancer have epithelial cells in their bone marrow. Among patients with stage I, II or III breast cancer, 36% have them. Their presence predicts a worse outcome, and less than half of those patients survived more than three years. Breast cancer can return one or even two decades after the primary tumor has been removed.

One case shows how long such cells persist. A man had a small melanoma removed and stayed free of symptoms for 15 years. After his death his kidneys were transplanted into two patients, who take drugs that suppress the immune system. Both developed melanomas that came from the donor, 16 years after his tumor had been removed.

Metastases in Bone

About 10% of the skeleton is replaced every year, so the whole skeleton is renewed over about a decade. Osteoblasts build bone and osteoclasts dissolve it. Osteoblasts carry the protein RANKL on their surface. When it touches the RANK receptor of an osteoclast precursor, that cell matures into an osteoclast. Osteoblasts also release OPG, a decoy that captures RANKL. The balance between RANKL and OPG sets the rate at which bone is built or removed.

Breast cancer cells in bone tip this balance toward removal, which produces osteolytic metastases. Prostate cancer cells tip it the other way and produce lesions of new, immature bone. A breast cancer cell in bone releases PTHrP, the protein that mobilizes calcium during lactation. PTHrP makes nearby osteoblasts produce more RANKL and less OPG. Osteoclasts then dissolve bone and release TGF-β, IGF-1 and calcium from it. IGF-1 and calcium help the cancer cells grow, and TGF-β makes them release more PTHrP. Each turn of this loop makes the next one stronger.

The loop can be broken at the osteoclast. Bisphosphonates are taken by mouth and stick to the mineral of bone, where they can stay for a decade or more. When an osteoclast dissolves that bone, the released drug kills it. Premenopausal women with breast cancer who received the bisphosphonate zoledronic acid had a 37% increase in overall survival over seven years. Denosumab, an antibody that binds RANKL, delays serious bone events better than bisphosphonates do.

The Cell of Origin Matters

Two cell types from the same breast tissue were given the same three genes, hTERT, the early region of SV40 and a ras oncogene (Chapter 11). In the cells that resembled the inner lining of the ducts, about 1 cell in $10^2$ could start a tumor. In the cells that resembled the outer layer, only about 1 in $10^6$ could. The first cells formed lung metastases in 10 of 11 mice and in 9 of 10 mice in a second test. The second cells formed none in 8 and in 6 mice. The same oncogenes therefore give different metastatic ability, depending on the cell they act on.
